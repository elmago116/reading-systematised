> **Abstract:** AbstractInformation systems serve as the “source of truth” for much of social reality, from credit scores to eligibility for boarding an airplane to the current time. In contexts of practical consensus, the system makes it so. I label this phenomenon system‐dependent truth. This paper advances a theory of performative truthmaking, wherein the agencies of giving as and taking as produce facts and truth as relations. I introduce the term systems of record to denote information systems that contain facts rather than propositions. I develop a suitable performative approach to the phenomenon of system‐dependent truth by synthesizing John Searle's social ontology, an account of truth, facts, and social reality, with Karen Barad's agential realism, an onto‐epistemology of human and nonhuman agency. Using several specific examples drawn from travel and migration contexts, including the US government's No Fly List, I show that system‐dependent truth arises when an agent takes information from a system as fact during the performance of sociotechnical truth. I argue that the agencies of truthmaking and factmaking are a distinct form of power, that the coordination of these agencies constitutes institutional rationalities of potentially global scale, and that systems of record are therefore critical sites for justice‐oriented information studies.

This paper argues that system-dependent truth is a
material-discursive consequence oftruthmaking, a socio-
technical performance that takes information from a sys-
tem of record as fact. The agency oftaking as fact, when
coordinated with the complementary agency ofgiving as
fact within a system of record, constitutes a distinct and
powerful rationality enabled by and accomplished
through information systems.
I first provide a definition of system-dependent truth.
Then, I provide a rationale for selecting a performative

and constitutive of things and properties (Austin,
1962;

and unitary phenomenon. Indeed, what I identify as the
effects of system-dependent truth have been observed in
a variety of settings and investigated in the literature of

2.2 | The world, pointing to systems
What happens when information systems are instead
pointed to? To examine this, we will consider a specific
example of reconciliation, in which “world” is aligned
with “system.” System-dependent truth involves a point-
ing into information systems. As with inventory systems,
this pointing is an accomplishment of specific sociotech-
nical practices rather than a property of the system or its
information. These practices make information within
systems of record into facts.
In the United States, the government maintains a No
Fly List of people who are denied boarding passes and
admission through airline security. If I make the state-
ment «I am not on the No Fly List», what makes this
statement true or false? In slightly more formal terms,
what factF determines the truth of this propositionP?
The factF to which the proposition«I am not on the
No Fly List» points is information that must be retrieved
from a specific system. Truthmaking agents, such as an
airport security officer, cannot“reconcile” this information
with the world, as one might with an inventory system.
The information that declares an in/valid traveler statusis
the fact. Any agent that re-inscribes information in this
system is not reconciling information with the world.
Rather, they materially alter facts and the corresponding
possibilities for truth. Agencies of inscription in a system
of record thus constitute power to change social reality.
The agencies such systems provide indicate that this
investigation closely links questions of justice with infor-
mation systems. This inversion of the traditional role of
an information system from proposition-containing to
fact-containing demands revision of the theoretical tools
used to analyze them.

3 | FACTS, TRUTH, AND
PERFORMANCE
In this section, I argue that truth and facts are most fruit-
fully studied as performances: material actions mutually
constituted with discursive import. I survey recent publi-
cations that employ performative approaches. My goal is
to argue that performativity holds great promise, not only
as a more parsimonious approach than those with repre-
sentationalist roots, but one that offers novel insights and
advantages to the study of system-dependent truth.
Materiality and discursivity are mutually constitutive
properties of information that arise in relation to agencies
of observation Barad (
2003, 2007), Butler (1988), Hollin
et al. (2017), Murris (2022), and Rouse (2004). Barad's
definition pushes the scope of discursivity far beyond spo-
ken or written language:“Discourse is not what is said; it
is that which constrains and enables what can be said.
Discursive practices define what counts as meaningful
statements” (Barad,
2003, p. 819). Discursive practices are
actions mostly undertaken by humans. These ideas may
be most familiar as influences on the sociomaterial
approach to the study of information systems (Boell &
Cecez-Kecmanovic,
2015; Jarrahi & Nelson, 2018;
Leonardi, 2013; Mutch, 2013; Orlikowski, 2007; Scott &
Orlikowski, 2013). Nonetheless, the ideas have broad pre-
cedent in information studies. I read Buckland's classic
exegesis of Briet (M. K. Buckland,1991, 1997), and his
subsequent elaboration of the concept of documentation
(esp. M. Buckland, 2014), for instance, as an integrated
material-discursive account of documents.
Documents are undeniably material; for Buckland,
their meaning and even their documentality is relational,
enacted by the practices that surround them. When infor-
mation, systems, and truth are each assumed to be
material-discursive (rather than either material or discur-
sive) we need not explain the phase transition between
meaning and materiality. Instead, we can approach infor-
mation as a materially constituted discourse and informa-
tion systems as discursively ordered material affordances.
A material-discursive starting point, inspired by docu-
ment studies, informs the performative approach I will
bring to the question of how truths come to depend on
information within specific systems.

4 | A RELATIONAL SOCIAL
ONTOLOGY OF FACTS
This section starts from Searle's mature social ontology
(2010), particularly his concept ofinstitutional facts,a sa
strong explanatory framework for socially contingent
truth.4 I find, however, that Searle's deep-seated
representationalist bias prevents his approach from over-
coming all the challenges presented by the phenome-
non of system-dependent truth. I argue that Searle's
substantialist interpretation of philosopher Elizabeth
Anscombe's originally relational concept of brute facts
(1958) obscures a promising resolution of these chal-
lenges. Specifically, Anscombe's original presentation
of brute facts can be added to Searle's framework once
his inessential representationalist commitments are
discarded. The resulting relational social ontology is
compatible with a material-discursive approach, enables
Searle and Barad's theories of performativity to be com-
bined, and is suitable for the theoretical task of explaining
system-dependent truth.

4.4 | Anscombe's relational ontology
of facts
Representational realists “[take] the notion of separation
as foundational” (Barad, 2007, p. 137). Barad provided a
way to avoid separating the discursive and material
aspects of system-dependent truth. So, this section asks,
can we also safely “throw away the ladder ”
(Wittgenstein,
2013, §6.54) of Searle's substantialist brute
facts?
Searle's institutional/brute facts dichotomy runs
throughout his account and, as noted above, is implicit in
the distinction betweenX and Y in his original definition
of constitutive rules. The cracks that are exposed by his
handling of the possibility of freestandingY terms widen
as we probe them, suggesting the need to revisit brute
facts. In doing so, Anscombe is revealed as an unexpected
ally in our search for a relational ontology of facts.
Searle claims to take the concept of brute facts from a
paper by Elisabeth Anscombe. Here is the essence of
Anscombe's conception:

5 | TAKING AS FACT: MATERIAL-
DISCURSIVE AGENCY
Anscombe's relational ontology of facts frees Searle's
social ontology from its representationalism. The final
dimension of my critique is that of agency. While Searle
is a leading exponent of speech acts and the study of illo-
cution, the agents undertaking such acts are surprisingly
passive in his account. This is where the branch of
J.L. Austin's intellectual legacy flowing through Judith
Butler (
1988) offers an intriguing solution. In short, this
section looks to exchange Searle's representationalist
dualism (memorialized by theX and Y of his formalisms)
with Barad's material-discursive performativity. Doing so
enables us to locate the situated agencies entangled with
facts and truth, as predicted by agential realism. Facts
and truth are thus not only relational but material-
discursive in Barad's sense.

Power has been visible throughout this analysis. I will

performative agencies of truthmaking and factmaking, so

7 | CONCLUSION
This paper has presented the phenomenon of system-
dependent truth as a major theoretical problem in the
study of information, information systems, and the socie-
tal effects thereof. I introduced the termsystem of record
to define systems that contain facts rather than proposi-
tions, and show that truth, facts, and information arise as
relations within the performance oftruthmaking using a
system of record. I augmented Searle's performative
account of institutional facts with Barad's concept of
material-discursive performativity, yielding a suitably per-
formative account of facts and truth without Searle's repre-
sentationalist commitments. This synthesis enabled me to
deeply analyze related examples of the performance of
system-dependent truth drawn from travel and border
security. Utilizing this theoretical machinery, I argued that
power is an inherent component of the phenomenon of
system-dependent truth. I showed how specific actions
constitute the performative contexts wherein system-
dependent truth is accomplished. I characterized the
power of factmaking agency by the extent of the performa-
tive context of truthmaking surrounding it. I identified
important ethical issues surrounding systems of record,
including inscriptive agency, performative context, and the
material-discursive nature of truthmaking and factmaking.
The resulting performative theory of sociotechnical
truth enables ethico-onto-epistemic approaches to the
study of system-dependent truth in information studies.
The entanglement of agency and objects suggested by
Barad's agential realism enables simultaneous investiga-
tions of what is (ontology), how and what we come to
know (epistemology), and what we should do (ethics) in
future empirical studies of system-dependent truth.
Holistic ethico-onto-epistemic approaches hold great
promise for addressing complex problems like epistemic
injustice (Anderson,
2012; Oliphant, 2021; Patin
et al., 2021). Regardless, the field of information studies
must reshape the agencies of truthmaking and factmak-
ing if we are ever to dismantle informational prisons
erected using systems of record.

Abstract  
Information systems serve as the “ source of truth ” for much of social reality,  
from credit scores to eligibility for boarding an airplane to the current time. In  
contexts of practical consensus,the system makes it so. I label this phenomenon  
system-dependent truth. This paper advances a theory of performative truthmak-  
ing, wherein the agencies of giving as and taking as produce facts and truth as  
relations. I introduce the term systems of record to denote information systems  
that contain facts rather than propositions. I develop a suitable performative  
approach to the phenomenon of system-dependent truth by synthesizing John  
Searle's social ontology, an account of truth, facts, and social reality, with Karen  
Barad's agential realism,an onto-epistemology of human and nonhuman  
agency. Using several specific examples drawn from travel and migration con-  
texts, including the US government's No Fly List, I show that system-dependent  
truth arises when an agent takes information from a system as fact during the  
performance of sociotechnical truth. I argue that the agencies of truthmaking  
and factmaking are a distinct form of power, that the coordination of these agen-  
cies constitutes institutional rationalities of potentially global scale, and that sys-  
tems of record are therefore critical sites for justice-oriented information studies.
