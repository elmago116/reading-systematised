 In the last decade, there has been an explo-

intelligence (AI) in our society. For the first time, the

fAIr LAC alliance
The fAIr LAC alliance [9], [10] is made up of a 
multidisciplinary group of Latin American experts from 
different governments, academic institutions, private 
companies, nongovernmental organizations, ethics 
experts, innovation centers, and specialists from dif-
ferent areas of the Inter-American Development Bank. 
The fAIr LAC team is advancing in the study of use 
cases of AI-based systems in critical areas for the cit-
izens such as health, education, or social security. In 
those cases, the local or national governments are key 
actors and the aim is to extract good practices that can 
be exported to other cases in the region.
The fAIr LAC is a regional initiative, but it will have 
local hubs. There are three in the list. The first hub will 
be implemented with the state of Jalisco in Mexico 
and local leaders in AI applications and policy-mak-
ing (see the “fAIr LAC Jalisco” section). The other two 
pilots will be installed in Costa Rica and Uruguay.
The work of the fAIr LAC initiative will focus on 
three axes [9]:
• develop standards and tools that guide respon-
sible and reliable use of AI in Latin America and 
the Caribbean through certifications, algorithmic 
audits, and specific guides.
• encourage responsible adoption through pilots 
and shaping regional hubs to promote awareness 
of the ethical risks of using AI in social services 
and ways to mitigate these risks.
• lead a dialogue around the responsible use of 
this technology-focused on citizens and from the 
perspective of diversity and inclusion, through the 
promotion of a diverse ecosystem with networks 
of experts, discussion tables, and conferences and 
the formation of an observatory of use cases.
fAIr aims are contributing to efficient public man-
agement, using the opportunities that arise from the 
AI-based technologies for the public good, as well 
as the linkages between governments and AI compa-
nies. Universities are part of all consortia.
fAIr strives to become a regional innovation hub 
for responsible use of AI and improving LAC socie-
ties’ trust in AI.
AI digital transformation in public 
services
AI-based technologies using the available data 
can solve problems from detecting epidemics to traf-
fic jams and disaster relief, but countries are not yet 
using this data to its full potential. As mentioned in 
the introduction, public administrations have strug-
gled in this digital transformation process, introduc-
ing AI and data analytics to improve their public 
services compared to private enterprises. There are 
several reasons why this is the case. One significant 
factor is a lack of understanding of the best approach 
for implementing AI and analytics in administrations, 
nor a clear vision of where there are low hanging 
fruits on which to base a case for AI public services. 
A manifestation of this lack of understanding is the 
scarcity of published successful cases to replicate. 
Case replication and adaptation are widespread in 
local and regional administrations. This is how gov-
ernments learn from each other and how the imple-
mentation of proposals gets improved. The lack of 
exemplary cases forces blue sky exploration that 
not many administrations are eager to engage with 
because of the inherent risks.

Another critical factor is the lack of collabora-
tion among administrations, the so-called smart 
collaboration [11]. There is little incentive to col-
laborate because administrations tend to perceive 
their needs differently and because local politics 
prioritizes serving and siding with the agenda of the 
local constituency over promoting new projects and 
innovation. This stands in dramatic contrast to the 
highly dynamic and collaborative world of AI, which 
is dominated by Open Source software. This field 
operates on collaboration between industry and 
academia, nonprofits such as Apache or NumFo-
cus and industry giants such as Google, Facebook, 
or Microsoft. Moreover, we cannot forget the series 
of visible and invisible barriers surrounding public 
administrations: difficulties to attract and retain tal-
ent within government jobs because salary levels 
are much lower than in the private sector; lack of 
diversity within internal teams or among established 
service providers—both key enabling elements for 
moving past the single solution to the problem.
Furthermore, venture capitalists and start-ups 
generally see the public sector as very complicated 
in terms of value capture. It is not enough to present 
a proposal that solves the problem and attracts the 
attention of the public, but the added requirement to 
solve the market problem and sell it to a myriad of 
public organizations full of bureaucratic procedures 
and vested political interests. This situation discour-
ages most of the start-up ecosystem from engaging 
with the public sector.
Finally, one more obstacle to the use of disruptive 
technologies in cities’ administrations is the risk and 
uncertainty associated with it. In many areas, the 
reward is not clear, while the costs and the imple-
mentation effort needed is high. Due to their public 
accountability, cities tend to be cautious about the 
use of resources in areas of high uncertainty like this 
one. Therefore, the use of external grants to fund 
pilots for exploring new areas is ideal for helping cit-
ies in blue sky exploration.
The long-term success of AI in the public sector 
depends on the integration of these technologies 
into services that allow the implementation of pol-
icies that benefit citizens and the administration. 
However, governments cannot carry out this inte-
gration by themselves; they need the collaboration 
of public universities, in the public and private 
sectors. These cross-sectoral partnerships involve

The same AI technologies that help to improve 
the functioning of services and decisions in the pri-
vate sector can contribute to solving some of the 
challenges that stifle public administrations such as 
reducing waiting times in the provision of services, 
detecting fraud, reducing the management bur-

for critical tasks. We portray some use cases of AI 
applied in different public sectors.
Health care: The multiple applications of AI in the 
health care are well known, for example, the use of 
deep learning (DL) techniques and computer vision 
in clinical imaging [13], demonstrating promising 
results in diagnosis spanning radiology [14], [15], 
ophthalmology [16], [17], or pathology [18], [19].
There are other applications of AI in the field of 
health care related with administrative aspects. For 
example, in the optimization of health inspections in 
restaurants using social media data to reduce food 
poisoning and derived hospital admissions [20], out-
patient visit forecasting in hospitals [21] or optimiza-
tion of bed rotation in hospitals [22]. The potential 
of AI in the field of public health care still has many 
frontiers to be explored, but we must be aware that 
medical data are among the most sensitive personal 
data and that with undesired processing they can 
produce serious harm to citizens [1].
Public safety: AI has started having a significant 
impact on the law enforcement agencies (LEAs) 
community and is being promoted through numer-

from autonomous drones to patrol and detect for-

criminal unmanned aerial vehicles [24], using facial

detect radicalization [26], among others. AI tech-

in malware detection [27], intrusion detection, and 
prevention systems optimization [28]. An example 
of AI put at service of LEAs was demonstrated by a 
public data aggregation technology that could pin-
point in 22 s the position of the shooter in Las Vegas 
Mandalay Bay tragedy by processing social media 
[29]. By using AI, LEAs will be able to respond in 
a more efficient way to incidents, prevent threats, 
improve resource utilization, and analyze criminal

Justice: The application of AI methods provid-
ing support to judicial decisions can help improve 
the consistency of sentences and the predictability 
of law application. These tools can be designed, 
for example, to provide general legal advice to the 
public, provide orientation based on jurisprudence 
to law professionals, or assist in court legal deci-
sion-making [32]. In the United States, AI is already 
being used in criminal justice across the country, for 
instance, to support making bail and parole deter-
minations with products like Correctional Offender 
Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions 
(COMPAS) [33].
The research carried out by the European Com-
mission for the Efficiency of Justice (CEPEJ) [32] has 
mainly identified six categories in which AI is used 
in the judicial environment: 1) advanced case-law 
search engines, 2) online dispute resolution [34];  
3) assistance in drafting deeds; 4) analysis (predic-
tive and scales); 5) categorization of contracts and 
detection of divergent or incompatible clauses; and 
6) chatbots to inform or support litigants in legal 
proceedings.
The implementation of predictive justice and AI 
in the judicial system has many and multifaceted 
implications, especially those related to ethics, fair-
ness, and potential biases, which will require a pru-
dent and balanced approach by decision-makers. 
For example, the study carried out by Propublica on 
the COMPAS tool mentioned above found that black 
individuals were far more likely than white individ-
uals to be incorrectly judged to be at a higher risk 
of criminal recidivism. In contrast, white individuals 
were more likely than black individuals to be incor-
rectly flagged as low risk of recidivism. When funda-
mental rights like freedom of individuals are at stake, 
sensible decisions that are based on AI decision 
support systems require guarantees that those sys-
tems are ethical, fair, unbiased, and sound.
Social services: The provision of social services 
can also benefit from the AI revolution by enhancing 
the ability to detect patterns in the population and 
more easily identify citizens at risk who require pub-
lic intervention and assistance. The potential offered 
by ML, NLP, image analysis, and decision support 
systems provides a range of tools to deal with the 
massive processing of large amounts of structured 
and unstructured data—in written reports, social 
networks, photographs, interagency databases—that 
are currently difficult to address manually, allowing 
case managers to spend more time interacting with 
people. Social services can benefit significantly 
from AI-based tools to significantly improve the lives 
of vulnerable populations. For instance, there are 
already studies that provide significant advances in 
predicting suicides, allowing health and social ser-
vices to anticipate a suicide attempt and provide pal-
liative interventions in time [36]. Other studies have 
used ML techniques to predict episodes of domestic 
violence, enabling the state to provide the necessary 
means in advance [37]. Social exclusion detection 
has also been addressed with AI tools [38], trying to 
provide novel methods to social workers to antici-
pate to chronification of social exclusion of vulner-
able citizens.
The application of AI in social services is a para-
digm of AI for good. However, even when the inten-
tion to do good is necessary, it is insufficient, we 
must take into account that in some situations the 
misuse of these tools, even unintentional, can cre-
ate or amplify other risks or harms. For example, the 
use of biased data or methodologies poses a consid-
erable risk that can generate new discrimination or 
unfairness situations. Trustworthiness is essential in 
technologies, in general, but is especially relevant in 
technology for social good.

Ethics and AI
Ethics is a broad concept of philosophy that seek 
to answer questions regarding human morality that 
aims to determine what are considered good and 
bad actions. One of the main challenges of today’s 
globalized world is to define a group of ethical 
values or norms that reach a general acceptance, 
due to social, economic, and cultural differences 
among different regions. Today, our society is ruled 
by a set of laws defined either at a primary level, 
such as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 
and a secondary level with national or international 
laws (e.g., General Data Protection Regulation of 
the European Union). However, regulations are, in 
general, a slow process that cannot follow the pace 
of technology progress. Therefore, AI is not legally 
bonded to any rule, although some aspects can be 
found in domain-specific laws, as it is the case of the 
European Medical Device regulation for the health 
care sector [44]. Hence, AI design, development, 
and use need an ethical guidance to avoid mali-
cious uses and scandals like those revealed during 
the last few years. Some of these events, like Cam-
bridge Analytica, have had direct consequences on 
democratic processes; other examples, such as the 
use of facial recognition in mass surveillance and 
law enforcement have had a significant impact on 
our society, our daily living, and our relationship 
of trust toward governments and new technologies. 
As a consequence, in 2019 there were over 100 
documents related to ethical principles published 
around the world, including governmental agen-
cies, but also big technological companies (e.g., 
Microsoft or Facebook), or academic and research 
institutions, and the number is increasing. In [45], 
authors analyze 84 of these AI guidelines and code 
all the existing ethical concepts into 11 ethical prin-
ciples. Among them are found the four principles 
defined in traditional medical research: autonomy 
(and freedom), nonmaleficence, beneficence, 
and justice (and fairness). Other concepts can be 
related to the Universal Declaration of Fundamen-
tal Rights and are strongly supported by the recently 
published 2030 Sustainable Development Goals of 
the United Nations [46], such as dignity, solidarity, 
or sustainability. Finally, we can find principles that 
are easily identified with dilemmas raised by the 
use of AI: transparency, which is the most preva-
lent principle with 73 occurrences, responsibility, 
privacy, and trust.
It is expected that public administrations that use 
AI-based solutions act to create a positive impact 
on society and environment, empowering citizens 
with the use they do of the technology and offering 
means to end users to be able to understand and 
interact with it as well. One of the most critical issues 
raised by AI is its impact on jobs and the economy 
[4]. We should highlight that the responsibility to 
build trust in AI relies on different stakeholders [47], 
[48] and not only on the AI system. These stake-
holders include different professional profiles, such 
as IT/AI experts, lawyers and regulators, experts in

Ethics and AI in public services
AI will have a key role in the digital transfor-
mation of public services and administrations. In 
the “AI digital transformation in public services” 
section, we have highlighted some examples of AI 
applications in the main domains of public services 
that are already implemented and used in practice. 
However, the more these technologies are being 
used by the public administration in automated 
decision-making or law enforcement, the more eth-
ical issues are raised, as the explanation for how 
and why an AI application reached its conclusions 
is still unclear. The opacity of AI systems in any area 
of public administration challenges the traditional 
responsibilities of administrators regardless of sub-
stantive policy area or whether decisions directly 
involve citizens. The core issue that is raised involves 
the accountability of the AI system and the responsi-
bility of the administrator who manages the program 
in which AI is used [50].
Governmental data controllers must notify cit-
izens which personal and sensitive data is being 
gathered, stored, and processed, how their data 
will be used, including the existence of automated 
decision-making, and, at least in those cases, mean-
ingful information about the logic involved [the 
algorithm(s)], as well as the significance and the 
envisaged consequences of such processing for the 
data subject. In other words, public administrations 
have the obligation to protect citizens from any pos-
sible harm caused by the AI system, while leveraging 
the benefits of computational and economic bene-
fits of such solution, or as explained in [51], to gov-
ern algorithms while governing by algorithms.
One example of the controversial use of AI solu-
tions in the public sector is the rollout of surveillance 
systems using facial recognition technology. With 
the final purpose of increasing public safety, these 
systems are collecting a massive volume of personal 
data that are affecting citizens’ privacy. Moreover, 
there have been cases in which the algorithm has 
showed to be biased, ending up in the discrimina-
tion of vulnerable groups of the society, generally 
people of color or other social and demographic 
minorities [52]. This same tradeoff between surveil-
lance and privacy has been a constant topic of dis-
cussion during the COVID-19 pandemic, where the 
rush to find solutions to help health care agencies 
to mitigate the spread of the virus led to the appear-
ance of digital contact tracing technologies that use 
sensitive data such as the localization or the Blue-
tooth signals of mobile devices to identify potential 
infected citizens along with their contacts and to 
apply preventive measures. Again, in this case, the 
proposed solution can leave out different social 
groups that will not benefit from the services pro-
vided by such technology as they do not have access 
to it. In addition, it might be the case that citizens 
are more willing to provide personal data in these

Currently, there is not a mature, global-standards 
body to help shape global governance of AI. Given 
that public sector organizations are aligned on the 
common good principle, these entities are best 
placed to come up with standards of ethics for AI 
that are beneficial for all. At the same time, no sin-
gle organization or institution is capable of shaping 
the governance guidelines for AI, given that we also 
have to consider ethics, legal, socioeconomic, and 
cultural aspects are sensitive to local variations. This 
is why the role of a global consortium, comprising 
multiple government entities, will be essential to pro-
vide a global reference for AI ethics.
There are currently proposals to address this issue 
with methodologies based on risk impact assessments. 
In Europe, Article 35 of the General Data Protection 
Regulation [53] defines a protocol to implement data 
protection impact assessments for those technologies 
that process personal data. Similar initiatives can be 
found now to audit the impact of algorithms based on 
automated decision-making, the so-called Algorithm 
Impact Assessment (e.g., [54] and [55]), which are 
expected to become one of the main facilitators to 
encourage the commercialization of transparent and 
accountable of AI systems [56].
fAIr LAC Jalisco
As we explained in the “AI digital transformation 
in public services” and “Ethics and AI” sections, AI 
offers great development potential for society and 
for improving the lives of citizens, but not without 
the risks associated with the misuse or poor design of 
these technologies or the biased data they are feed 
with. With these challenges in mind, to promote the 
responsible and ethical use of AI, improve social ser-
vices, and mitigate the growing social inequality in 
the region, the fAIr LAC project in Latin America and 
the Caribbean was born.
fAIr Jalisco
A partnership among the IADB, the State Govern-
ment of Jalisco and Instituto Tecnologico y de Estu-
dios Superiores de Monterrey (ITESM) are piloting 
a comprehensive AI center in Jalisco, Mexico, to 
accelerate the responsible use of AI for social poli-
cies and impact entrepreneurship.
The first pilot is centered in an AI-based applica-
tion for the health care public sector. It is support-
ing an inclusive approach of AI toward vulnerable 
and (possibly) excluded populations. Health care is 
clearly one of the most dynamic, yet challenging sec-
tors in Mexico and the LAC region. In Mexico, health 
care is a universal service and is free.
Nevertheless, in the whole region, health care 
faces major challenges of quality, accessibility, and 
affordability for a large section of the population:
• Shortage of qualified health care professionals 
and services like qualified doctors, nurses, tech-
nicians, and infrastructure.
• Nonuniform accessibility to health care services 
across the region with physical access and con-
nectivity continuing to be the major barrier to

Use case: AI for diabetic retinopathy
Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a complication of 
diabetes mellitus (DM) [57] and is the primary cause 
of blindness in developing and developed countries 
[58]. According to [57], early-stage DR detection 
reduces the risk of vision loss and blindness in DM 
patients. In Mexico, DR is especially challenging due 
to the following:
• High prevalence of diabetes: The World Health 
Organization (WHO) reported that the preva-
lence of diabetes in Mexico is around 10.4% in 
2016. Mexico also has one of the highest rates of 
deaths attributable to diabetes [59]. According 
to INEGI, there were 101,257 death cases caused 
by diabetes in Mexico in 2018.

Ethical considerations
In this use case, we take into account some 
recommendations reported at [63]–[65]. The most 
important considerations for this use case are pre-
sented as follows:
• General project ethical considerations: We have 
taken into account as much as possible the seven 
main principles proposed in [66] for the whole 
project: 1) social and clinical values; 2) scien-
tific validity; 3) fair subject selection; 4) favora-
ble risk–benefit ratio; 5) independent review; 6) 
informed consent; and 7) respect for potential 
enrolled subjects.
• Privacy by design: We set the control, limits, and 
conditions of the use of data developing a web 
application for this project with a clear data 
pipeline, restricted data access, and disclosures 
based on HIPAA privacy rules. As a result, we 
ensure the appropriate safeguards to protect 
the privacy of personal health information and 
work only with the information with patient 
authorization.
• Verifiability and replicability: In this use case, we 
propose to create an anonymous open data set 
for research and other government implemen-
tations. Besides, all the code and the model 
weights will be shared via Gitlab.
• Professional responsibility: To tackle this issue, 
we collaborate with an ophthalmologist com-
mittee and with a local retina association to 
show and discuss the advances of the project. 
Moreover, we are working to achieve at least a 
sensitivity of 80% and specificity greater than 
95% for the classification model.
• Human oversight: For all the use cases, we 
aim to take into account the preservation of 
the jobs. The proposed system only gives an 
opinion, and this can be reviewed, verified, 
and changed by a professional (remedy for 
automatic classification). This action helps us 
to clarify that, in this project, workstations are 
maintained, and technology is used as a tool 
for decision support.
• Prevention of bias using representative and 
high-quality data. In this case, we try to follow 
the recommendations proposed at [67]. In this 
case, data concerning ethnicity, gender, or 
income was explicitly not included. First, we 
draft the Research Ethics Board (REB) review. 
Only afterward, the data were collected in a 
second stage. The third step is data exploration 
and data cleaning only to maintain RD images. 
In the fourth step, the patients’ identity is pre-
served by assigning IDs to each of them, guar-
anteeing their privacy. The quality evaluation of 
the images was made using contrast, noise, and 
blurring metrics. Besides, we selected the low- 
and high-quality images visually and trained a 
CNN to classify the quality of the images, and 
finally, an expert labeled the images.

the fAIr pilot in Jalisco, we believe that to build

very beginning (see [3 ] and [74]). For us, it is

AI, we must ensure investment for good, fair, and 
inclusive incubation to scale solutions that work.
