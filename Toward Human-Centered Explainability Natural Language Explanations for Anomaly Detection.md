Abstract
This paper proposes a human-centered explainable artificial intelligence pipeline for anomaly detection, designed to gen -
erate meaningful, context-aware explanations using local large language models. The proposed pipeline translates model 
outputs and SHAP-based feature attributions into natural language explanations for cybersecurity alerts generated by an 
autoencoder within an enterprise network. It incorporates a human-in-the-loop component to ground the explanations in 
validated expert knowledge, enhancing their interpretability and alignment with human decision-making processes. Using 
a rubric-driven LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation, we benchmark several large language models and show that as smaller models 
receive more contextual grounding through human-in-the-loop, their explanatory performance improves significantly, nar -
rowing the gap with larger models while maintaining substantially lower computational demands. Our approach provides 
targeted, context-aware explanations designed to meet the cognitive and operational needs of security analysts, contribut -
ing to more ethical, trustworthy, and resource-efficient AI integration in critical cybersecurity environments.

truly human-centered XAI. The main contributions of this 
article are threefold. First, we present a comprehensive pipe-

their understanding process through a question-and-answer

strategies, exploring the role of domain expertise through 
different techniques to enhance contextual precision. Third, 
we analyze how the interaction between model size and the 
use of contextual information affects the clarity and ade -

2 Background and Related Work
Making network anomaly detection systems both effective 
and transparent involves bringing together advancements in 
different research areas. This section reviews relevant litera-
ture in the field of network anomaly detection, then analyzes 
how XAI is being applied to enhance model’s transparency 
and, finally, the emerging field of NLEs as a crucial step 
toward HC-XAI.
2.1 Network Anomaly Detection
The adoption of ML techniques for detecting cyberat -
tacks and suspicious activities has emerged as a significant 
research area within cybersecurity, driven by the continuous 
evolution of enterprise networks and the increasing com -
plexity of cyber threats. For example, in Hosseinzadeh et al. 
(2021), authors conduct a comprehensive review of anom -
aly detection schemes that utilize Support Vector Machines 
(SVMs), for identifying intrusions. Their work investigates 
and categorizes various approaches where SVM classi -
fiers are employed, often in conjunction with other ML 
techniques. Similarly, Keserwani et al. ( 2021) propose an

These approaches treat cyberattack detection as a classifi-

ability to adapt to novel or evolving attack patterns (Kes -

works introduce unsupervised anomaly detection meth -
ods. For example, in Zoppi et al. ( 2021), authors conduct 
an experimental comparison of 17 different unsupervised 
anomaly detection algorithms across 11 publicly avail -
able attack datasets, evaluating their effectiveness. Other 
popular methods rely on deep learning, with autoencoders 
being a leading example. Ortega-Fernandez et al. ( 2024) 
proposes an unsupervised Intrusion Detection System (IDS) 
for industrial control systems which utilizes a deep auto -
encoder for anomaly detection. The authors demonstrate 
that this technique can achieve high detection rates and low 
false alarms, outperforming both supervised state-of-the-art 
methods and unsupervised baselines. Similarly, Torabi et al. 
(2023) presents an autoencoder model for detecting anom -
alies in cloud networks. Authors introduce a modification 
in how the reconstruction error is calculated for anomaly 
detection that was able to improve accuracy while being an 
efficient way to identify and classify network attacks. Build-
ing upon the autoencoder architecture, Narmadha and Balaji 
(2025) combines this architecture with an LSTM network. 
With this integration, the autoencoder is trained to learn nor-
mal network patterns and reduce data dimensionality, while 
the LSTM captures complex sequential dependencies in 
network data.
While deep learning solutions can excel at anomaly 
detection, they usually lack transparent decision-making 
processes due to the use of neural network based ML mod -
els, which constrains the adoption of these solutions in 
critical decision-making scenarios (Ribeiro et al., 2016). In 
cybersecurity contexts, especially in the domain of intrusion 
detection, understanding why an anomaly was identified as 
such is crucial for several reasons: first, it provides interpret-
ability, allowing security analysts to understand the charac-
teristics that are indicative of potential threats, enhancing 
confidence in the system’s decisions. Second, explanations 
can facilitate actionable insights, enabling targeted investi -
gation, facilitating the implementation of mitigation strat -
egies, and reducing the mean time to resolve a particular 
cybersecurity incident.
2.2 Explainable Artificial Intelligence
The increasing complexity and “black box” nature of mod -
ern AI systems have led to a growing need for transparency 
and understanding in their decision-making processes. This 
has given rise to the field of XAI, which aims to provide 
insights into why AI models make certain predictions and 
how they arrive at their results (Somani et al., 2023). Cur-
rent state-of-the-art in XAI can broadly be categorized into 
model-agnostic and model-specific techniques.
Model-agnostic techniques treat the ML model as a “black 
box”, focusing typically on explaining individual predic -
tions based on input-output relationships. Surrogate model 
methods exemplify this by employing simpler, human-com-
prehensible models, such as decision trees or linear models, 
to explain predictions from opaque systems. Following this 
approach, LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016) generates explana -
tions by training a local surrogate model on perturbed data 
from the original instance. Then, the model approximates 
the behavior of the original model in that region. Similarly, 
the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method (Lund-
berg & Lee, 2017) treats features as players in a cooperative 
game, using Shapley values to measure their contribution 
to the model’s predictions. Despite challenges like choos -
ing baselines and the algorithm’s computational efficiency, 
SHAP is widely used for its strong theoretical foundation.
In contrast to model-agnostic approaches, model-specific 
methods leverage the internal structure of the ML model to 
generate explanations. Decomposition-based techniques, 
such as Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP, Binder et 
al., 2016) and Deep Taylor Decomposition (DTD, Monta -
von et al., 2017), break down the relevance score into lin -
ear contributions from the input. These methods propagate 
relevance scores through the network layers to decompose 
the prediction back to input features or contributions. Other 
methods in this category include gradient-based explana -
tions, with Integrated Gradients (IG, Sundararajan et al., 
2017) serving as their most representative technique. The 
fundamental mechanism of IG, and its variants, is to accu -
mulate the gradients obtained as the input is interpolated 
between a reference point and the actual input.
The use of XAI in the cybersecurity domain has been 
explored in Siganos et al. ( 2023). Authors incorporated 
SHAP within an IDS tailored to detect and classify cyberat-
tacks in IoT environments, providing both local and global 
feature attributions, which are then presented visually. Sim-
ilarly, Namrita Gummadi et al. ( 2024) introduce an XAI 
framework for anomaly detection in IoT environments. This 
framework integrates various XAI techniques, highlighting 
key features and their classification thresholds. Kobayashi et 
al. (2023) extend the LRP framework with DTD to interpret

centered XAI, but there is no single, universally accepted 
method (Sovrano & Vitali, 2024). However, in Kayser et al.

explanations in a medical environment for both expert and

3 Methodology
This section introduces the proposed methodology for HC-
XAI in the cybersecurity domain, which addresses key 
shortcomings in current approaches by integrating unsu -
pervised anomaly detection, contextualized explanation 
generation, and HITL feedback to support analyst decision-
making. Figure 1 depicts the proposed HC-XAI workflow, 
designed to translate predictions of a deep learning anomaly 
detection model into actionable NLEs for Security Opera -

structured into five distinct phases: (1) Data Analysis & Pre-
processing (2) Anomaly Detection, (3) SHAP integration, 
(4) Human-Centered Explanations, and (5) NLE alignment. 
A complete example of the proposed explainable detection

3.1 Data Analysis & Preprocessing
The evaluation of the proposed approach is performed using 
the CIC-IDS-2018 dataset (Sharafaldin et al., 2018), a com-
plex benchmark dataset for intrusion detection research, 
available from the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity. 
This dataset was selected due to its realistic representation 
of modern IT environments, encompassing a wide range of 
benign and malicious network activities, including sophis -
ticated cyberattacks such as infiltration, brute-force, dis -
tributed denial-of-service (DDoS), botnets, among others. 
The experimental setup of the dataset involved an attacker 
infrastructure of 50 machines targeting a simulated organi -
zational network, structured into five departments, compris-
ing 420 computers and 30 servers. This dataset is composed 
of bidirectional network flows, which were extracted from 
captured network traffic using CICFlowMeter-V3. Table 1

Each method presents different trade-offs. Domain adap-
tation deeply aligns the model with specific expert knowl -
edge but requires extensive training data and can become 
outdated. Feedback loops can iteratively refine outputs to 
build user trust, yet their success hinges on a well-calibrated 
reward function, which can be opaque. Finally, contextual 
retrieval offers a flexible, up-to-date grounding mechanism 
but is constrained by the quality of the retrieval process and 
the potential of inheriting expert biases. The selection of 
an appropriate method, therefore, depends on the specific 
requirements for data availability, model adaptability, and 
runtime performance.
Finally, even though recent advancements, a major chal-
lenge in this field is the evaluation of the quality of the mod-
el’s outputs. Traditional automated methods, like BLEU 
(Papineni et al., 2002) and ROUGE (Lin, 2004), are not suf-
ficient for assessing complex explanations, since these met-
rics cannot accurately measure the alignment of the outputs 
with human preferences or domain’s requirements. While in 
this context human evaluation is seen as the gold standard, 
it’s often too expensive, time-consuming, and inconsistent 
for large-scale use (Deriu et al., 2021).
To overcome these issues, the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm 
emerged as an effective alternative (Zheng et al., 2023). 
This paradigm leverages LLMs as evaluators, demonstrat -
ing that with clear guidelines, their judgments on qualities 
like alignment or coherence align remarkably well with 
human experts. However, this method is not without limi -
tations: AI judges can have biases, such as preferring lon -
ger or first-seen answers, or generate hallucinations (Li et 
al., 2024). While various mitigation strategies exist, a par -
ticularly effective approach (especially for domain-specific 
tasks), is the implementation of a structured rubric (Peng et 
al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). By employing a structured 
rubric, the judge’s assessment is guided by specific criteria, 
leading to an evaluation that is more transparent, replicable, 
and aligned with domain requirements (Li et al., 2025).
Despite growing interest in HC-XAI for cybersecurity, 
existing approaches fail to offer human-centered explana -
tions. Many explainability methods either stop at highlight-
ing anomalous features or rely heavily on visual outputs 
without evaluating their practical utility for security ana -
lysts. Critically, they lack robust evaluation frameworks 
and fail to incorporate domain expertise into the explana -
tion process. Our work addresses these limitations by intro-
ducing a novel evaluation framework, focused on assessing 
explanations against criteria essential to an analyst, such as 
threat awareness. Additionally, we present a pipeline that 
generates NLEs, enriched with domain-specific knowl -
edge, and delivers them via an interactive chatbot interface 
designed to facilitate analysts’ comprehension and engage -
ment with security alerts.

3.2 Anomaly Detection
Following the preprocessing of the dataset, this phase 
focuses on the implementation and training of the anomaly 
detection model. For this purpose, an unsupervised deep 
learning model, specifically a deep autoencoder (Rumelhart 
et al., 1986), was selected. Autoencoders are particularly 
well-suited for anomaly detection tasks due to their abil -
ity to learn complex, non-linear representations of normal 
behavior from unlabeled data. By training the autoencoder 
exclusively on benign traffic, it learns to reconstruct normal 
patterns. Consequently, when presented with anomalous or 
malicious traffic, which deviates significantly from these 
learned patterns, the model will exhibit a higher reconstruc-
tion error (the discrepancy between the original input and 
the autoencoder’s reconstruction).
An instance can be classified as an anomaly if its recon -
struction error exceeds a predefined threshold. This thresh -
old can be determined by analyzing the distribution of 
reconstruction errors on a validation set of unseen benign 
data. Therefore, each defined experiment involves four spe-
cific subsets of benign instances: training (60 %), used to 
train the autoencoder; validation (10 %), used for model 
validation during training; threshold (10 %), reserved for 
determining the anomaly detection threshold; test (20 %): 
used for final performance assessment.
To determine the optimal configuration of the model, a 
hyperparameter tuning was performed. Various deep auto -
encoder neural network architectures, with different number 
of hidden layers and neurons at each layer, and other param-
eters like dropout and regularization were evaluated. The 
selected deep autoencoder architecture consisted of 6 hidden 
layers for both the encoder and the decoder plus the latent 
space. Each hyperparameter training was performed for 100 
epochs (with early stopping) using the Mean Squared Error 
(MSE) as the loss function. The final model used a learning 
rate of 0.01, a batch size of 256, a dropout rate of 0.01, the 
ReLU activation function, and an L2 regularization value 
of 0.001.

Prior to model training and evaluation, the raw data went 
through several preprocessing steps. First, the different 
capture days were aggregated and merged to form a uni -

some unsupervised models, such as autoencoders, are par -

Autoencoders, by their nature, learn complex, often non-
linear, interdependencies, and correlations among input 
features. Consequently, the poor reconstruction of a particu-
lar input feature is often not just due to an anomaly in that 
specific feature. Instead, its value may be anomalous in the 
context of other co-occurring features. While these learned 
correlations are fundamental to the effectiveness of the 
autoencoder, they also mean that the reconstruction of one 
feature is influenced by many others. Antwarg et al. ( 2021) 
demonstrate that incorporating SHAP-based feature attribu-
tions alongside reconstruction errors significantly improves 
explanation quality by clarifying these interdependencies, 
something that raw feature values or reconstruction errors 
alone cannot provide.

3.4 Human Centered Explanations
Although feature-level attributions from XAI techniques 
like SHAP improve model transparency, they are often too 
technical for immediate interpretation. Converting these out-
puts into NLEs makes them more comprehensible and better 
aligned with domain conventions. LLMs provide an effec -
tive mechanism for automatically generating such NLEs. 
However, despite their remarkable capabilities, LLMs are 
not without limitations (Ji et al., 2023; Ye & Durrett, 2022). 
They may produce explanations that are overly generic, con-
textually inaccurate, or misaligned with the actual behavior 
of the underlying predictive model. These issues can arise 
due to the reliance of LLMs on training data that may not 
fully capture the complexities of specific domains, such as 
cybersecurity (Ali & Kostakos, 2023; Hasanov et al., 2024), 
or because of challenges in interpreting structured SHAP 
values. To mitigate these challenges and ensure the reli -
ability of generated explanations, HITL (Yang et al., 2023) 
techniques were recently introduced to incorporate expert 
knowledge into the AI explanation process.
Considering the characteristics of the methods reviewed 
in Section 3, this work adopts a contextual retrieval 
approach using RAG (Lewis et al., 2020b), which combines 
the strengths of information retrieval and LLMs, enabling 
more accurate and context-aware responses by incorporat -
ing external knowledge into the response generation pro -
cess. In this context the HITL aspect refers to the process 
of continually improving the system’s explanations with 
expert knowledge provided by the RAG component. The 
human expert comes into the loop by expanding this knowl-
edge base in the RAG component: when a cybersecurity 
analyst determines that an LLM-generated explanation is 
incomplete or could be improved, he/she can write a more 
precise, expert-level explanation. This new, human-written

each feature, its reconstructed value becomes the specific 
prediction that SHAP aims to explain, with the autoencoder 
as the prediction function. To this end, KernelSHAP (Lun -

observing how its reconstructed value changes as it sam -

This SHAP variant combines the principles of linear LIME 
(Ribeiro et al., 2016) with Shapley values to construct a

and samples from the possible coalitions of input features. 
A crucial design choice in this variant is the definition of the 
background data, which represents the reference distribu -

benign behavior, constructed from the subset of clean, non-
anomalous training data. This set is then summarized using 
K-Means clustering (Macqueen, 1967), reducing the back -

approach maintains computational efficiency while provid -

5 Conclusions
This study contributes to the field of HC-XAI by present -
ing a pipeline to improve model interpretability and enable 
more effective cybersecurity operations. We show that com-
bining technical feature attribution techniques like SHAP 
with Natural Language Explanations creates a more robust 
and human-centered approach to XAI. By using LLMs to 
generate these explanations, complex outputs are translated 
feature “Fwd Seg Size Min”. Moreover, this approach also 
links the XAI outputs to plausible attacker TTPs, such as 
Nmap fragmentation scans, producing an explanation that is 
both technically grounded and operationally informative for 
cybersecurity analysts.
However, it is worth noting that the explanation module 
is inherently dependent on the autoencoder’s output. Con -
sequently, if the autoencoder misclassified a benign flow as 
anomalous (false positive), the explanation module does not 
autonomously correct this prediction. Instead, it attempts 
to contextualize the classification by explaining the spe -
cific feature deviations which triggered the detector. This 
transparency also serves as a reliability mechanism for the 
human analyst by exposing the specific logic behind a false 
positive (e.g., highlighting that a minor deviation in packet 
size was the sole driver of an “anomaly”). Additionally, the 
controlled incorporation of alert and explanation pairs of 
resolved false positives into the retrieval knowledge base 
would assist the model to identify and flag potential mis -
classifications. However, this introduction must be carefully 
limited to prevent the model from misclassifying subtle 
attacks as false positives.
Our evaluation results demonstrate that while larger 
models such as Qwen2.5-32B generally achieve higher 
scores, smaller models can deliver competitive perfor -
mance with a significant reduction on resource consump -
tion when enhanced with contextual retrieval strategies. In 
fact, smaller models like Mistral-7B and Gemma-9B with 
HITL, were able to outperform the explanations produced 
by larger models like Qwen2.5-32B lacking this grounding. 
These findings show that the effective use of context strate-
gies and the integration of human knowledge can have a 
greater impact than the model size alone. This is particularly 
significant for organizations seeking to balance the need for 
high-quality, human-aligned explanations with the practical 
constraints of hardware resources and data privacy.
From an end-to-end perspective, the total time from alert 
to explanation is dominated by the feature attribution and 
NLE generation stages. While the initial anomaly detec -
tion by the autoencoder is fast (2794 ± 89 µs), the SHAP 
analysis already introduces a notable delay, with an average 
runtime of 19.83 ± 0.90 seconds per flow. This is caused by 
the evaluations of the model on perturbations of the input 
to approximate feature contributions, a process that scales 
poorly with the number of features and background sam -
ples. However, the final NLE generation is the most time-
consuming phase, with generation times ranging from 28.42 
to 87.88 seconds in average for the HITL approach.

viable for SOC operations, as explanation generation is 
typically not on the critical path of a real-time threat con -

Moreover, it is widelyrecognized that no universally valid method exists for pro-ducing effective explanations: different contexts requiredifferent approaches. In particular, combining textual andvisual explanations has been suggested as a way to improveuser understanding, and natural language explanations(NLEs) are increasingly seen as a promising path to moretransparent, responsible, and trustworthy explanations(Sovrano & Vitali, 2024).Despite the potential of these techniques, their practi-cal application still faces significant challenges. Producinghigh-quality NLEs requires integrating outputs from predic-tive models with XAI techniques and enriching them withcontextual information and domain expertise. While manystudies employ large language models (LLMs) to gener-ate explanations, their utility decreases without sufficienthuman alignment and contextualization. Some research-ers attempt to address these limitations by adopting larger,more capable LLMs. However, hosting large-scale LLMslocally is often impractical, and systems that employ thesemodels generally rely on cloud-based APIs. This introducescertain regulatory risks, as transmitting potentially sensitivedata to third-party servers can conflict with data-protectionor data-sovereignty requirements in certain jurisdictions.
