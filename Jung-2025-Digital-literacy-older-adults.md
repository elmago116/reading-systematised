Abstract
Digital technology is used to improve the quality of life for the growing number of 
older adults in the United States, but it also exacerbated the digital divide between 
generations and regions. Digital literacy programs effectively designed for older 
adults have a potential to mitigate this issue; however, sustaining an effectively 
designed program can be a challenge due to practical constraints. This case study-
mixed methods study examined effectiveness and challenges of implementing a dig-
ital literacy program that is designed with six instructional strategies known to be 
effective for older adults. Data sources included surveys and interviews from learn-
ers, instructors, including teaching assistants, and an administrator involved in the 
project throughout three implementation cycles at a regional library. Overall, partici-
pants reported enhanced confidence in using digital literacy domains and satisfaction 
towards the program. Various implementation challenges identified are aligned to the 
instructional strategies used. The study emphasizes the importance of developing and 
sustaining an inclusive and flexible digital education for seniors aimed to reduce the 
digital divide and promote their engagement in an increasingly digital society.

One approach to addressing the digital divide among older adults in rural com-
munities is to provide effective digital literacy instruction and ensure its con-
sistent availability. Digital literacy, defined as the “abilities to access, manage, 
understand, integrate, communicate, evaluate, and create information safely and 
appropriately through digital technologies” (Law et al., 2018, p. 6), plays a piv -
otal role in improving the quality of life for older adults through digital technol-
ogy (Kim & Feng, 2021; Tirado-Morueta et al., 2018). Recognizing this potential, 
prior studies have explored the digital literacy skills older adults need to acquire 
(Pihlainen et al., 2023), the unique characteristics of older adults as technology 
learners (Bülbül et al., 2022), and the instructional methods proven effective for 
this demographic (Rasi et al., 2021).
Although understanding these would ensure effective program design, few 
studies examined digital literacy programs for older adults from a sustainability 
perspective. Older adults often seek additional learning opportunities through 
community organizations, such as public libraries and social service agencies 
(Casselden, 2023). However, researchers have highlighted significant practical 
constraints—such as limited budgets, inadequate resources, and a lack of staff-
ing—that can hinder the effectiveness of community-led instructional programs 
(Detlor et al., 2022; Serenko et al., 2012). These challenges are often more pro-
nounced in rural libraries, where funding and resources are typically scarcer than 
in urban or suburban areas (Colibaba et al., 2019; Real et al., 2014).
To date, few studies have explicitly examined the implementation challenges 
of instructional programs for older adults alongside their learning outcomes (e.g., 
Botner, 2018). To address this gap, we developed a digital literacy program for 
older adults and implemented it multiple times, incorporating revisions, at a 
regional library serving older adult learners across three counties, two of which 
were classified as rural with populations under 50,000 as of 2021. The findings 
from this study are expected to enhance our understanding of how community-led

2  Digital literacy instruction for older adults
2.1  Digital literacy
Digital literacy can be broadly defined as cognitive, technical, and social-emotional 
competencies and skills required to function effectively in digital environments (Eshet-
Alkalai, 2004; Ng, 2012). Functioning in this context includes finding, assessing, cre-
ating, and sharing meanings individually and together as literate members of a soci-
ety. However, there is no consensus on what specific competencies or skills should be 
included in discussing digital literacy (Law et al., 2018; Tinmaz et al., 2022). Eshet-
Alkalai (2004, 2012) suggests that digital literacy comprises six distinct skills: (a) 
photo-visual thinking, (b) reproduction skills, (c) branching skills, (d) information 
skills, (e) socio-emotional skills, and (f) real-time thinking. European Union’s Digital 
Competence Framework for Citizens (DigComp 2.2) proposes five competency areas: 
(a) information and data literacy, (b) communication and collaboration, (c) digital con-
tent creation, (d) safety, (e) problem solving (Vuorikari et al., 2022). While early defini-
tions focused on attitudes and skills related to using digital devices, more recent defini-
tions embrace multidimensional aspects of digital technology use, emphasizing critical 
thinking beyond mere access to or use of information in various digital forms (Peng & 
Yu, 2022).
Despite the increasing prevalence of digital technologies and the growing impor -
tance of digital literacy and skills in the digital era, levels of use and competence vary 
significantly based on individuals’ backgrounds, such as age, region, and socioeco-
nomic status (Peng & Yu, 2022). Generally, younger generations demonstrate greater 
confidence in learning and accessing new digital devices and skills. In contrast, older 
generations tend to be less motivated and less confident in adopting digital technologies 
and devices, often facing limited access to these technologies as well (McDonough, 
2016; Vercruyseen et al., 2023).
2.2  Digital literacy programs for older adults
Digital literacy skills are crucial for older adults’ physical, psychological, economic, 
and social well-being (Bülbül et  al., 2022; Lee et  al., 2022). Furthermore, older 
adults can be exposed to more social support and experience cognitive and affec-
tive engagement as they learn to use new technology tools, such as tablet computers 
(Tsai et al., 2017). According to Mayhorn et al. (2004), older adults want to learn 
computer technology to enhance communication, search information, remain active

and independent, and have fun, but do not want to learn technical jargons. Older 
adults tend to have positive attitudes towards computers and a willingness to learn 
technology, but they often hesitate or struggle due to prior negative experience, anx-
iety, low confidence and usefulness related to technology and their age (Barrie et al., 
2021; Blažič & Blažič, 2020; Mayhorn et al., 2004; McDonough, 2016).
Research shows that older adults are uniquely challenged to learn digital literacy. 
Older adults experience age-related declines in visual (presbyopia, increased sensi-
tivity to glare), motor (computer mouse control), and cognitive (perceptual speed, 
memory, spatial ability) abilities (Fisk et  al., 2020; Mayhorn et  al., 2004), which 
contribute to their slower adoption rate of new technologies, compared to younger 
demographics (McDonough, 2016; Yap et al., 2022). Vercruyssen et al. (2023) sug-
gest that older adults face learning barriers in three key areas: IT-related language 
and jargon, understanding and using digital device hardware, and grasping software 
concepts and internet use. Consequently, digital literacy programs for older adults 
should address these specific challenges by focusing on the development of basic 
digital skills rather than prioritizing comprehensive or critical skills—which con-
tradicts the trend of general digital literacy learning as noted by (Peng & Yu, 2022).
To meet these needs, digital literacy programs for older adults often adopt diverse 
pedagogical approaches, designed with consideration for older learners’ needs and 
objectives. Ahmad et al. (2022) identified six key strategies for computer instruction 
for older adults: collaborative learning, informal learning setting, teaching aids (to 
leverage audiovisual resources, easy-to-understand handouts, the potential of gami-
fication), pertinence (by relating content to their practical and daily needs), design-
ing basic lessons (that afford learner repetition and more time for learning), and 
obtaining feedback and assessment from a systematic review. Researchers also have 
pointed out the importance of fostering meaningful teacher-student and student-stu-
dent interactions to support learning (Rasi et al., 2021; Tomczyk et al., 2022) and 
offering task-based and project-based learning experiences (Jimoyiannis & Gravani, 
2010). Such learner-centered and individualized approaches are particularly effec-
tive in addressing learners’ unique experiences, characteristics, needs, challenges, 
and goals, while also maximizing their learning engagement and skill acquisition 
(Rasi et al., 2021).
2.3  Community‑led digital literacy programs
One popular way older adults receive digital literacy instruction is through com-
munity organizations, such as public libraries (e.g., Barrie et al., 2021; Lee et al., 
2022). Community organizations, such as public libraries, provide more accessible 
learning opportunities and environments, particularly for marginalized populations. 
Public libraries, in particular, offer various digital literacy programs for older adults, 
contributing to their well-being, social and human connections, and digital literacy 
skill development (Casselden, 2023). Barrie et  al. (2021) also suggest that public 
libraries, as hubs of community-based learning, provide older learners with a safe, 
comfortable, and supportive space. These environments help reduce fear and anxiety 
about digital and computer technologies while facilitating experiential learning.

However, these community-led instructional programs encounter various practi-
cal constraints that hinder their ability to create lasting positive impacts within the 
community. According to Detlor et al. (2022), factors of learning environment and 
program components affect learning outcomes of digital literacy programs offered 
by local community organizations. In particular, the authors identified lack of fund-
ing, classroom resources, qualified and sustainable teaching staff (scheduling), qual-
ity evaluation, and knowledge sharing (i.e., sharing of best practices) as the learn-
ing environment factors to attend to. In terms of program components, Detlor et al. 
purported that timing of the instruction (e.g., difficulty of scheduling), marketing, 
and types of training (e.g., basic vs. advanced digital skills training) also affected the 
outcomes of community-led digital literacy programs.
In particular, evaluating a community-led digital literacy program for older 
adults needs a broader perspective in terms of what counts as indicators for suc-
cess and creativity in data collection methods (e.g., interviewing the instructors or 
other stakeholders of a program, Detlor et al., 2022), as ensuring quality evaluation 
design and collecting quality data at a scale from older adults learning digital liter -
acy appear to be challenging in general (e.g., Oh et al., 2021). One needs to consider 
multiple factors beyond the traditional measures, such as learners’ short-term learn-
ing outcomes (task performance, satisfaction) and/or long-term learning outcomes 
(transfer, retention) (Mayhorn et al., 2004).
2.4  Our program
Following the guideline from prior research, we designed a Digital Literacy Pro-
gram for Older Adults to promote their digital literacy competencies and skills at a 
regional library. Below we describe how we approached the overall program design 
process and why, what contents were covered in the program (and how they changed 
over time), and how the program was implemented at the library.
2.4.1  Approach to program design
We considered the goals for instruction, the learning content, target learners (e.g., 
motivation to learn, prior experience, special characteristics of older adult learners), 
and the particular context in which the instruction is being implemented to systemat-
ically design effective instruction (Morrison et al., 2019). Among many approaches 
to instructional design, rapid prototyping, which aims to “(build) a model of the 
system to design and develop the system itself … (through) the parallel processes 
of design and research” (pp. 36–37, Tripp & Bichelmeyer, 1990), was used as the 
instructional situation was deemed complex and unfamiliar to the authors who 
designed the instruction. This approach highlights the iterative nature of the learn-
ing design that is centered on learners as opposed to the learning content (Morrison 
et al., 2019; Schmidt et al., 2020). As such, learner and stakeholder feedback were 
prioritized during the program redesign as opposed to what was defined as compo-
nents of digital literacy in the literature.

2.4.2  Program overview
The program consisted of four 60-minute-long residential sessions implemented 
weekly in a computer lab at the library. There were two volunteer instructors who 
were content experts and two teenage volunteers who played a role of teaching assis-
tants for the entire project. Three of them were present in each class to lead instruc-
tion and support learning. There were three design and implementation cycles that 
occurred over a period of one and a half years. Concepts related to the selected tech-
nology tools and tasks (e.g., accessibility, phishing) were introduced as a mini-lec-
ture by the lead instructor followed by class discussion to enhance digital literacy 
learning (i.e., Rasi et al., 2021). For procedural learning (e.g., how to send email, 
how to create a birthday party invite), which took up the majority of the program, 
the lead instructor demonstrated how to perform a task first and students performed 
the task on their own computer with support from other instructor or TAs.
The program is intended to cover the five digital literacy competence areas from 
DigComp 2.2 (Vuorikari et al., 2022): information/data literacy (IDL), communica-
tion/collaboration (CC), digital content creation (DC), safety (SF), problem solving 
(PS). Actual technology tools and tasks performed changed each implementation 
cycle based on the feedback from the learners. To ensure effectiveness, the program 
incorporates six instructional strategies identified in Ahmad et  al. (2022) that are 
specifically tailored to older adults learning digital technology: Collaborative learn-
ing with teaching assistants (personalized assistance), informal learning setting at 
a local library, teaching aids, pertinence (relevance to student needs), basic lesson 
design for repetition and slow pacing, feedback and assessment. Details of the pro-
gram are discussed below and summarized in Table 1.
In the Fall of Year 1 (2022), we chose to teach digital literacy by teaching older 
adults how to use Google Apps, which are widely adopted in the society, freely and 
easily accessible regardless of the device one uses. We identified basic tasks one 
might perform with Google Apps and mapped them to the digital literacy compe-
tence areas from DigComp 2.2 to ensure all five areas are covered. For example, 
learners were expected to create a Google account (PS, SF), create a Google Docs 
(DC), add someone as a collaborator to author and edit (CC), and find and organ-
ize the document in Google Drive (IDL). We originally planned to cover Chrome, 
Gmail, Google Docs, Slides, Sheets, Drive, Sites, and Forms. However, due to the 
slow pacing, we ended up covering Chrome, Gmail, Google Docs, Slides and Drive. 
No formal data were collected, as this iteration served as a pilot study. Informal 
feedback from the first implementation suggested that the original version was too 
packed and advanced for the older adults we served.
In the Spring of Year 2 (2023), we implemented a new version of the program twice 
(once in February and another in March) covering more basic digital literacy skills 
related to basic computer and Internet use (problem solving, information/data literacy), 
online social connection (communication and collaboration, digital content creation), 
and safety. In this version, examples of tools that a target skill can be used with were dis-
cussed instead of instructor demonstrating how to perform a series of tasks with a par-
ticular technology tool. For example, learners were told that one could use Google, You-
Tube, or Bing to search for information online. In this iteration, formal data collection

3  Methods
3.1  Case study‑mixed methods design
This study utilized the Case Study-Mixed Methods Design (CSMM; Guetterman & 
Fetters, 2018), to explore the design and implementation of a particular instructional 
program as a case study (Yin, 2014) while integrating both quantitative and qualita-
tive methods to fully understand the case phenomenon. The case in this study is ref-
ereed to as the Digital Literacy Program for Older Adults designed by the research 
team and implemented three times at a regional public library primarily serving the 
surrounding rural communities. Multiple sources of quantitative or qualitative data 
were gathered from learners, instructors, and a librarian administrator who played 
a key role in the program’s design and execution, providing both quantitative and 
qualitative insights into various aspects of the training. Despite the relatively small 
number of participants, the CSMM approach in this study offers a multifaceted 
understanding of the complex issues people experience during the evolving program 
design and changing implementation settings.
3.2  Data sources
Multiple data were sourced from various stakeholders in the project. Details of what 
data were collected following each implementation cycle can be seen in Table 1.
3.2.1  Basic computer skills survey (BCSS)
BCSS is a researcher-developed survey that consists of demographics questions 
(age, gender, ethnicity) and twenty 5-point Likert self-report items (1: Strongly

Disagree – 5: Strongly Agree). These items were on learners’ willingness to adopt 
new technology (New Tech Adoption, 1 item) and confidence in performing digital 
literacy tasks in the following areas: (i) problem solving (8 items; “I can turn on and 
turn off my computers properly.”), (ii) information/data literacy (3 items, “I can use 
a web browser to find and view websites.”), (iii) communication/collaboration (3 
items, “I can communicate with friends or family members digitally …”), (iv) digi-
tal content creation (1 item, “I can create and share my content …”), and (v) safety 
(4 items, “I can create and use strong and unique passwords to secure my personal 
information.”). Ethan (librarian) and the instructors reviewed the survey to ensure 
face and content validity. The pre-survey also had demographic questions, and the 
post-survey had an additional section for satisfaction (4 items) and three open-ended 
questions on perceived benefits, challenges, and suggestions for improvement.
Data used in this study consist of 19 pre-survey responses and 13 post-survey 
responses collected from the two classes in the second program implementation. 
In total, program participants were older adults aged from 60 to 87 (M = 75.11, 
SD = 7.67). They were mostly white (White = 23; Hispanic = 3; African Ameri-
can = 1; Unknown = 5) and female (female = 26; male = 6). According to the pre-sur-
vey, the participants reported using primarily smartphones, as well as other devices 
(17 have used smartphones; 12 desktops, 9 printers, 8 laptops, 6 tablets).
3.2.2  Instructor interviews
Instructional service providers (i.e., two main instructors David and Tara, two teach-
ing assistants) were interviewed after the second and third implementations of the 
program, addressing a set of questions on perceived instructional experience–what 
worked and did not work, and why–and suggestions for program improvement. 
These interviews lasted about 30 min each time and were recorded and transcribed 
for analysis.
3.2.3  Librarian administrator interview
The librarian, Ethan (pseudonym), who oversaw the administrative aspect of the 
program implementation, was interviewed for about 30 min. Eleven questions relat-
ing to his prior experience implementing learning programs for adult members in 
the community, his perceptions about the Digital Literacy Program and its imple-
mentations over time, including areas for improvement, were discussed.
3.3  Data analysis
To examine the effectiveness of the Digital Literacy Program (RQ1), item responses 
for each competence area were averaged to compute five competence scores. Then, 
independent samples t-tests were used to compare means of New Tech Adoption, 
Problem Solving, Information/Data Literacy, Communication/Collaboration, Digital 
Content Creation, and Safety between pre and post survey data. Additionally, post

survey satisfaction score means and standard deviations were calculated and post 
survey comments were thematically categorized.
To identify the challenges of implementing the Digital Literacy Program (RQ2), 
a qualitative thematic analysis was performed with the stakeholder interview data 
both inductively and deductively (Braun & Clarke, 2006). First, the transcripts 
were read multiple times and open coded with highlights. Second, the codes were 
reviewed in a research team to identify instances of challenges versus descriptions of 
the experience. Third, experience themes and data are aligned to the six categories 
instructional strategies by Ahmad et al. (2022) to better illustrate the characteristics 
of the program implemented. Then, challenge themes and data were mapped to these 
six categoires. Fourth, the narratives for each of the six categories were drafted and 
refined within the research team and shared with program instructors (David, Tara) 
and the librarian (Ethan) as a member-check to ensure credibility (Tierney & Clem-
ens, 2011). No major edits were made as a result of the member-check. The results 
of the thematic analysis are summarized in Table 2.
4  Findings
4.1  Effectiveness of the digital literacy program for older adults
Effectiveness of the program is examined in terms of perceived learning (confidence) 
and learner satisfaction based on data collected from the two classes in the second 
implementation. Before the program, the participants were highly willing to adopt new 
technology (M = 4.68, SD = 0.58) despite relatively low overall confidence in digital lit-
eracy areas (M = 2.87, SD = 1.53). The participants were least confident in their Safety 
competences (M = 2.51, SD = 0.86), followed by Digital Content Creation (M  = 2.53, 
SD = 1.22). The older adults were most confident in their Information/Data Literacy 
competences (M  = 3.18, SD = 1.13), followed by Communication/Collaboration 
(M = 2.93, SD = 1.11). After the program, the participants were most confident in Prob-
lem Solving competence (M = 4.50, SD = 0.59) and least confident in Digital Content 
Creation (M = 3.69, SD = 1.44).
The t-test result indicated that students’ adoption intent increased slightly by 0.16 
after the program, but the difference was not statistically significant (p > .05). Accord-
ing to the t -tests, participants’ confidence in overall digital literacy competence 
increased significantly by 1.5 (p < .05), as well as five area-specific competences after 
the program. Of the areas covered, the largest increase was observed in their Safety 
competence (Mdiff = 1.84). Their responses in the post survey also suggest that they felt 
“more confident in trying new things.” Detailed statistics are reported in Table 3.
According to the post survey data, the participants were satisfied with the program 
(M = 4.69, SD = 0.48), the instructors (M = 4.85, SD = 0.38), and the usefulness of the 
learned skills (M = 4.77, SD = 0.44). They also reported that they are likely to recom-
mend the program to others (M = 4.85, SD = 0.38). According to the post survey com-
ments, the participants appreciated that they learned something and had an opportunity 
to refresh their knowledge and get personal assistance. However, they acknowledged

diverse student needs and low confidence in using computers. The participants also 
suggested that the program to be continued and offers personalized assistance.
4.2  Implementation challenges
Stakeholders of this program included learners, two instructors (David, Tara), 
two teaching assistants (TAs), and one librarian (Ethan) who played the adminis-
trator role in the program implementation. Interviews with these stakeholders, as 
well as comments from learners, provided insights on major implementation chal-
lenges confronted. Below, we describe various implementation challenges perceived 
in alignment to the instructional design strategies we adopted for program design.

4.2.2  Informal learning setting at a local library
The local public library offered a physical space that is “welcoming and friendly 
to everyone” (TA interview) for the Digital Literacy Program that required 
access to computer devices for its experience-based curriculum and personal-
ized support. The researchers and the instructors had prior relationships with 
the library as its patrons and service providers, which helped them in knowing 
the space and its instructional programming. The informal nature of the library 
instruction allowed us to flexibly develop the curriculum for the program and 
schedule classes based on the availability of the instructors, who were also vol-
unteers. Having the library as our partner was crucial at the inception of the 
program. The library’s existing system and infrastructure for community-facing 
instructional programs were flexible enough to accommodate our needs in terms 
of scheduling, space and tool reservation, and program promotion and partici-
pant recruitment (e.g., listserv, social media).
A challenge related to implementing the program in the informal learning set-
ting was managing the participants after recruitment. According to Ethan’s reg-
istration record, pre-registered participants ranged from 5 to 17 across the three 
cycles of program implementations. This range was typical, according to Ethan. 
David, however, pointed out that the actual participants fluctuated even more 
and that not having a consistent class membership each day disrupted the class 
flow. Even with those who participated in the program consistently, he experi-
enced hardships of implementing the “hands-on activities as [he] envisioned.”

4.2.3  Teaching aids
According to David, who was the main instructor that served in all of the imple-
mentations, the primary methods for instruction in the program were “presen-
tation and hands-on activity (demonstration).” In the first implementation, the 
primary teaching aid was the multimedia presentation, which was displayed in 
the classroom as the instructor talked about the content. In the latter implementa-
tions, the instructors prepared additional handouts that listed the key steps of the 
activities in a larger font size, alongside the presentation. In addition, Tara pro-
jected her instructor computer screen as she demonstrated procedures for a digital 
content creation activity with Canva. She suspected that [instructors projecting 
the same screen content as what the learners see] made learners “able to follow 
along.”
Despite the efforts made to accommodate the needs of older adults, David 
affirmed that the learners had struggled with understanding some technology terms, 
such as “artificial intelligence”, “cloud computing.” Similar sentiment was observed 
from the TAs, who recommended avoiding “excessive use of jargons” as a strat-
egy for supporting older adults. Tara also mentioned that understanding “lingo” 
was an issue in her class. This challenge with technology terms, even with ones that 
are considered simple, such as going to “File” in the menu bar, has caused a chal-
lenge in learners following instruction. Ethan, the librarian, later pointed out that 
the speed and quantity of information and technology can be “really intimidating” 
to older adults and that “a lot of the terminology” is another barrier for older adult 
learning of technology.
4.2.4  Pertinence
Pertinence, or content relevance to learner needs, was considered during the design 
of the instruction based on the literature review and informal conversations with 
Ethan about his older adult patrons. Both indicated a need to focus on the basics. As 
such, we initially developed a curriculum focused on what we considered the basics 
and commonly used digital technology, such as Google Docs, Google Drive, Gmail, 
and Facebook. In the final implementation, a digital content creation activity with 
Canva, which may be considered more than a basic, was tried.
One challenge with ensuring pertinence was that our conception of basic instruc-
tion differed from what the learners needed. According to Ethan, the librarian, 
most of the older adult learners he encountered at the library are “intimidated by 
the technology.” He reported that he had seen instructors “rewrite lesson plans [for 
different instructional programs]” as most of his learners at that time needed “very

basics.” David and the TAs also reported that the participants needed “a lot more 
basic” training. In the second implementation, we selected topics that appeared to 
be more fundamental than the first iteration topics (e.g., Google apps), such as con-
necting computers to a Wi-Fi network, finding and opening different applications on 
a computer, and searching information for problem solving. Yet, some older adults 
still perceived them to be “new” (post-survey). Several episodes demonstrating the 
technology experience of the older adult participants were shared in the instructor 
interviews. For example, some participants did not know “the location of the power 
button” or the presence of Google Apps on their smartphones (David interview). 
Yet, Tara recalled that her students “loved the Canva” compared to other tools that 
were “more technical.” She commented that her students would have liked it as it 
allowed them to “play, see colors, … put pictures … and did not need an account to 
play,” suggesting that older learners would enjoy learning that meets their interest.
Another challenge was that the older adult learners had varying motivations for 
participation. Our data suggests that older adults had various motivations for partici-
pating in the Digital Literacy Program, divided between those who simply want to 
“keep their brains active” and those who want to “change careers.” (Ethan). David 
reported that he and his TAs would occasionally receive questions about “random” 
technology issues that are not immediately related to their instructional topics, such 
as “why does my home internet [not work]?” He also remembered one participant 
who was hoping to figure out a way to promote his non-governmental organization 
in Google search. Although these are meaningful questions from the perspective of 
learners, the instructor pointed out these occurrences prevented him from “stay[ing] 
on track.” The instructor also noted that teaching older adults was somewhat “more 
challenging [to manage]” than teaching younger children due to the various reasons 
that brought them to participate in the program.
4.2.5  Basic lesson design for repetition and slow pacing
To design a basic form of a lesson, we initially identified a limited number of key 
technology tasks to perform (e.g., send an email) and described steps to guide stu-
dents. Pacing and repetitions were not explicitly considered. The instructors did 
their best to cover the contents prepared, but they were not bound by the program to 
cover everything. As a result, the instruction often did not cover everything that was 
prepared within the given time periods. The following issues emerged as implemen-
tation challenges unique to older adults that slowed the process.
Tara pointed out “dexterity” as a major learning challenge that hindered older 
adults to smoothly “navigat(ing) the system.” The learners were offered with a 
mouse and a keyboard when they were working on the Canva activity on Chrome-
book, as using touchpads were challenging for them. However, Tara explained that 
her learners had “hard time using the mouse” as well, as the activity required them 
to perform left, right, or double clicks, such as selecting an option from a “drop 
down” menu. This challenge hindered the learners to smoothly navigate the pro-
grams that they needed to use to perform the learning tasks.
As a part of the instruction, the learners were asked to download an application 
on their smartphones. However, David noted that many learners could not complete

the task because they did not remember their own password as their caregivers (i.e., 
children) had set it up for them. As it is challenging for them to keep private infor -
mation, Tara mentioned using applications that require registration and log-in may 
be an issue with older adults (e.g., 2 factor authentication).
4.2.6  Feedback and assessment
As a library-based instructional program, traditional assessment was not planned. 
However, we attempted to collect learner feedback through surveys and instructors 
observing the learner in-class performance and informally engaging in conversions 
with them.
According to our data, inconsistent participation and diverse learner needs were 
the reasons for assessment challenges. For example, class membership across the 
four-week-long implementations always fluctuated (Table  3), resulting in a lower 
number of students who actually completed the entire curriculum. David com-
mented that checking “class progression was a challenge” due to such inconsistent 
class participation. Instructor interviews also indicated presence of unusual students 
who complicated collecting quality and actionable feedback. For example, David 
reported students who did not want to touch computers due to a health reason and 
who kept diverting from the course content, which made him suspect that the stu-
dent had join the program simply to “seek(ing) social interaction.”
5  Discussions
Overall, the Digital Literacy Program for Older Adults was a blend of personally 
guided, hands-on learning activities designed based on the six evidence-based prac-
tices (collaborative learning, informal learning, teaching aids, pertinence, basic 
design, feedback and assessment, Ahmad et al., 2022). Three versions of the pro-
gram systematically developed through a rapid prototyping approach (Dong, 2021; 
Tripp & Bichelmeyer, 1990) were implemented multiple times in a regional public 
library.
Our program improved the older adult learners’ confidence in becoming more 
digitally literate, according to the survey responses from the learners (Table  2), and 
received overall positive feedback from the stakeholders. At the same time, multi-
ple implementation challenges were reported and mapped to the particular instruc-
tional strategies used to design the program. The insights gleaned from this case 
study offer avenues for advancing our understanding of sound design and execution 
of digital literacy programs tailored for older adults.
5.1  Inclusive and flexible programming of older adults: microlearning 
with an agentic approach
Our data, despite the small sample size, showcased where learner diversity may lie. 
For example, pre survey data suggest that the older adults in our study were most 

13325
Education and Information Technologies (2025) 30:13309–13330 
diverse in terms of their Digital Content Creation (SD = 1.22) and Problem Solv -
ing (SD = 1.16) competences, which included skills for basic computer and Internet 
use. Learner diversity issues were also observed in the qualitative data (i.e., various 
conceptions of basic, varying motivation for participation) as we were seeking perti-
nence throughout the iterations. In line with prior studies that suggest heterogeneity 
within the older adult learner group (Arthanat et al., 2019; Barrie, 2021), our find-
ings suggest a need to provide inclusive and flexible programming to prevent further 
digital exclusion of older adults. We concur with Vercruyssen et al. (2023) in that 
basic digital literacy skills, compared to digital literacy instruction that foregrounds 
critical thinking, are more essential for older adults. Our study also explicitly 
addressed the pedagogical aspect of designing digital literacy learning programs, an 
area often overlooked in the literature on geragogy, the educational theory and prac-
tice for older adults (Gates & Wilson-Menzfeld, 2022).
One form of instruction that would accommodate such diverse learner needs is 
microlearning, a model of instruction that utilizes “bite size” modules on essen-
tials (Zhang & West, 2020, p. 314). Furthermore, a more agentic approach to older 
adults’ use of digital media is needed when designing an instruction. According to 
critical gerontologists, the agentic approach to aging “takes seriously people’s eve-
ryday interactions with digital media and uses these experiences to explore what it 
means to be a critical citizen … in a process of collaborative knowledge creation” 
(Hebblethwaite, 2016, p. 103). The core ideas in this approach resembles those of 
asset-based teaching or funds of knowledge that defies the deficit model of educa-
tion (Tewell, 2020). Together, the microlearning model and the agentic approach 
may offer more socially just learning opportunities for older adults (Wynia Baluk 
et al., 2023).
5.2  Accessible and sustainable program development
Public libraries, such as the one we collaborated with, can provide an accessible 
space for informal learning (Barrie et  al., 2021; Wynia Baluk et  al., 2023). Yet, 
limited funding and staff resources often result in implementation challenges. Our 
study shed light on some challenges of implementing a volunteer-based instructional 
program at a regional public library (e.g., space/device reservation, securing vol-
unteer instructors and assistants, marketing instructional programs). Although the 
regional library we collaborated with was not considered a rural library according 
to the American Library Association standards (Real et al., 2014), two out of three 
counties this library is funded to serve are considered rural (Georgia Department of 
Education, 2024). In its Community Impact report in FY2023, the library reported 
having 22 full time staff-equivalent working across 6 branches (and a kiosk and a 
traveling library). The adult learning programs for all of the branches were run by 
one staff member (Ethan) and volunteers.
As noted by our participants, digital literacy instruction for older adults seems 
to thrive with personalized learning approach. Arthanat et  al. (2014) found that a 
home-based individualized information communication technology training was 
effective in ensuring that older adults sustainably used the technology. It is, however, 

13326 Education and Information Technologies (2025) 30:13309–13330
worth noting that their program was heavily supported with one occupational ther -
apy graduate student per participant (aged about 70 years old on average) as the 
“personal coach.” Each coach provided monthly at-home training sessions for three 
months, with the first one being most important and lasting about 3 h, as well as on-
going email or phone support between visits. Each older adult participant received 
$150, iPad, and a $50 iTunes card for their 6-month-long participation to the study. 
Our program, on the contrary, was mostly run by volunteers with no additional fund-
ing. We also struggled to find more volunteer instructors, including TAs, and find 
common time that volunteer instructors, older adult learners, and the library are 
available.
Research on self-sustaining community system building highlights the need for 
local ownership (Rey-Moreno et al., 2015), community engagement (Wisesa et al., 
2017), collaborating with regional public universities (Koricich et  al., 2020), and 
understanding the characteristics and motivations of the volunteers in the area (e.g., 
Colibaba et al., 2019; Resnick et al., 2013). Volunteering at a library promotes civic 
engagement (Scott, 2011), but sustaining such volunteer-based programs requires 
additional efforts. To this end, Colibaba et al. (2019) recommends considering inter-
personal, operational, and structural dimensions to sustain volunteer-based rural 
libraries. In particular, the authors highlight the need to provide support, training, 
recognition, and formal means of communication to volunteers to ensure their posi-
tive interpersonal relationship with the library. They also argue having standardized 
policies and procedures for volunteering alongside provision of a more flexible vol-
unteer model that accommodates volunteers’ personal schedules, under the guidance 
of a volunteer coordinator.
6  Conclusion
Our Digital Literacy Program for Older Adults at the local public library has proven 
to be an effective way of empowering this demographic with essential digital skills. 
The program improved participants’ technical abilities and increased their confi-
dence in using technology to create and share contents, contributing positively to 
their social connections and safety online. The study’s findings emphasize the need 
for continuous efforts in understanding diverse digital literacy needs for older adults 
and designing programs that are not only accessible and effective, but also efficient 
and sustainable. Such initiatives are vital in ensuring that older adults remain con-
nected and active in an increasingly digital world.
While this study provides valuable insights into the design, effectiveness, and 
sustainability of a digital literacy program for older adults, several limitations 
should be acknowledged. First, our sample size was limited, with 19 pre-survey and 
13 post-survey responses in the second version of program implementation, which 
restricts the generalizability of the findings. A larger sample would allow for more 
robust statistical analyses, such as inferential tests, and would improve the reliabil-
ity of the conclusions drawn about the program’s effectiveness. Class membership 
was not kept for 4 weeks each implementation was performed. Moreover, the third 
design and implementation cycle lacked participants who met our age expectation

(i.e., older adults around 65 or more), preventing us from collecting more participant 
data for the study. Although our program was advertised as targeting “older adults,” 
participant recruitment and commitment in the real-world was severely limited. Sec-
ond, the study was conducted at a single public library within a specific community, 
primarily serving rural older adults. Future studies should consider examining the 
similar issues in other regional libraries to ensure the findings transfer.
As the demographic of older adults continues to grow, thoughtfully designed 
digital literacy programs that are easily accessible to diverse older adult learners, 
including the ones in rural areas, will be key in promoting their inclusion and partic-
ipation in our digital society. Future research should seek ways to sustain the effec-
tive programs with active participation from the community (both as volunteers and 
program participants) and explore long-term, broader impacts of such instructional 
programs on the quality of life for older adults.
