> **Abstract:** The interaction between Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums (GLAMs) and Citizen Science (CS) has been explored mostly from a prospective perspective. This paper presents an empirical analysis of five CS pilots addressing urban sustainability challenges, conducted within or in close collaboration with six GLAMs. In Barcelona, a university partnered with a public library to assess heat exposure in public spaces. In Delft, children’s perceptions of urban spaces were collected and discussed through a collaboration between a university and a public library. In Tallinn, a community museum supported the testing of a digital visualization tool to enhance citizens’ feedback in urban planning. In Vienna, an urban research institute and an academic library co-designed two pilots addressing urban heat and the transformation of public spaces. Findings draw on GLAMs’ distinct contributions, the structural organization of the pilots, and the perceptions of librarians and researchers, collected through surveys, interviews, and collective writing processes. A strong community-oriented focus and the engagement of underrepresented citizen groups were common features across all pilots, though organizational structures and actor typologies varied by city. GLAMs consistently acted as powerful connectors between researchers, citizens, and local stakeholders. Embedding CS initiatives within social and community-oriented venues helps mitigate power imbalances and foster horizontal, mutual learning between academics and GLAM staff. GLAM can also provide the crucial social infrastructure to significantly embed sustainability challenges-related CS initiatives in many knowledge ecosystems, over different countries and cities.

Abstract 
The interaction between Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums (GLAMs) 
and Citizen Science (CS) has been explored mostly from a prospective 
perspective. This paper presents an empirical analysis of five CS pilots 
addressing urban sustainability challenges, conducted within or in close 
collaboration with six GLAMs. In Barcelona, a university partnered with a public 
library to assess heat exposure in public spaces. In Delft, children’s perceptions 
of urban spaces were collected and discussed through a collaboration between 
a university and a public library. In Tallinn, a community museum supported the 
testing of a digital visualization tool to enhance citizens’ feedback in urban 
planning. In Vienna, an urban research institute and an academic library co -
designed two pilots addressing urban heat and the transformation of public 
spaces. Findings draw on GLAMs’ distinct contributions, the structural 
organization of the pilots, and the perceptions of librarians and researchers, 
collected through surveys, interviews, and collective writing processes. A strong 
community-oriented focus and the  engagement of underrepresented citizen 
groups were common features across all pilots, though organizational structures 
and actor typologies varied by city. GLAMs consistently acted as powerful 
connectors between researchers, citizens, and local stakeholders. Embedding CS 
initiatives within social and community -oriented venues helps mitigate power 
imbalances and fosters horizontal, mutual learning between academics and 
GLAM staff. GLAM can also provide the crucial social infrastructure to

Introduction  
 
Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums (GLAMs) are increasingly recognized 
as key civic institutions whose roles are undergoing active redefinition (Aabø, 
2005). Recent scholarship highlights their potential to operate as social 
infrastructures—physical and institutional infrastructures that foster community 
interaction and social connections (Gooch et al., 2025; Klinenberg, 2018; 
Latham and Layton, 2019) . Combined with their intrinsic role in  knowledge 
creation, curation, and dissemination, as well as their broad geographical 
distribution (Benoit, Iii et al., 2025), GLAMs hold significant promise as partners 
in Citizen Science (CS) initiatives. 
 
While archives have recently developed crowdsourcing practices (Chiquet et al., 
2023; Graf, 2024; Hansson and Dahlgren, 2022), natural history museums have 
a longstanding tradition of involvement in CS, leveraging their research 
expertise and collections  and contributing to expanding CS to new audiences 
(Ballard et al., 2017; Spear et al., 2017). Other museums have embraced more 
experimental CS approaches, including citizen-curated exhibitions (Runnel et al., 
2024) and co-created universal design recommendations (Hedvall Per-Olof et 
al., 2018). 
 
Among the GLAMs, the interaction between CS and libraries has been extensively 
explored. Since the 2010s, growing attention has been devoted to understanding 
how public, academic, and research libraries can collaborate with CS initiatives  
(Liu and Liu, 2023; Mumelaš et al., 2025; Mumelaš and Martek, 2024) . The 

 5 
literature also focuses on identifying opportunities and challenges for integrating 
CS within library contexts, often through surveys and interviews with librarians 
in different  countries (Filipeti and Mumelaš, 2025; Kowalska -Chrzanowska, 
2024; Martek et al., 2022; Nurfarawahidah et al., 2025; Pimentel and Esteves 
Gomes, 2024; Rammutloa, 2023; Silva-Aboy and Martin-Melon, 2025; Svahn et 
al., 2025). 
 
Cohen et. al (2015) were among the first to identify the potential roles of 
libraries in CS initiatives, highlighting their capacity to foster community 
building, support data curation, and provide spaces, technologies, and 
resources. Subsequent studies have expanded on this perspective by examining 
stakeholder perceptions  and analyzing the skills and competencies librarians 
require (Nielsen et al., 2024; Nurfarawahidah et al., 2025; Tautkeviciene and 
Pranckute, 2024) . Several studies have  assessed specific library -based CS 
initiatives to identify the practical enablers and key drivers of successful 
collaborations (Cigarini et al., 2022, 2021; Fortuño et al., 2025; Ignat et al., 
2019, 2018; Moyle et al., 2011; Overgaard and Kaarsted, 2018; Wiederkehr, 
2022, 2019). 
 
Overall, the literature converges in recognizing libraries as key partners in CS 
initiatives. However, the analysis of CS practices within libraries and other 
GLAMs remains limited to single cases and to specific contexts and topics, mostly 
related to biodiversity monitoring (Ballard et al., 2017; Fortuño et al., 2025) or 
to the transcription and curation of library and archival collections (Moyle et al., 

 6 
2011; Wiederkehr, 2019) , while  there is, to our knowledge, a lso a  lack of 
documented cases addressing urban sustainability challenges.  
 
The aim of this paper is to together analyze five CS pilots implemented across 
four European cities  and countries, representing Southern Europe (Barcelona, 
Spain), Western Europe (Delft, The Netherlands), and Central and Northern 
Europe (Tallinn, Estonia and Vienna, Austria). All pilots shared the overarching 
goal of addressing urban sustainability challenges by drawing on local knowledge 
ecosystems and developing specific CS initiatives conducted within or in close 
collaboration with GLAM institutions. 
 
The pilots were implemented within the framework of the Open Urban 
Sustainability Hubs (OPUSH) European project, which established a community 
of practice comprising librarians and researchers over a three -year period. The 
overarching goal was to make a si gnificant and scalable contribution to the 
development of contemporary knowledge hubs addressing urban sustainability 
and social inequality. OPUSH fostered capacity building through a CS approach 
in which academic libraries , higher education institutions, GLAMs, public 
authorities, SMEs, and citizens collaborated. 
 
Accordingly, this paper addresses the following research questions: 
RQ1: What are the communalities and differences in between the five CS pilots 
implemented across four European cities?  
RQ2: What are the distinctive contributions of GLAMs within these CS initiatives 
specifically targeting urban sustainability? 

 7 
RQ3: How can the CS–GLAM alliance be effectively operationalized, and what 
added value does this partnership have in an urban sustainability scope? 
 
The analysis draws on desk research and on data collected after the completion 
of the five pilots, including self-reflection exercises, collective writing processes, 
and interviews with both librarians and researchers. Together, these sources 
provide an empirical basis for answering the research questions outlined.

B_CC (Heat Chronicles) 
Urban heat islands and extreme heat are a growing problem for many cities. 
Nevertheless, the focus is mostly put on the physical characterization of the 
phenomena, while neighbors’ needs are seldom considered. The goal of the 
B_CC pilot is to document neighbors’ perspectives on urban heat in relevant 
public spaces of their neighborhood and at a micro -scale, considering their 
knowledge as residents. Five especially affected neighborhoods in the Barcelona 

 9 
region were selected. Diverse citizen groups , such as primary and secondary 
school students, kindergarten families, young people involved in summer camps, 
and self -organized groups of elderly people , were involved. A public library 
(Figure1A), situated in the neighboring city of L’Hospitalet de Llobregat, the 
second most populous municipality of Catalonia, closely collaborated. The library 
facilitated connections with socially relevant actors of the neighborhood while 
acting as a community hub. The different groups of participants identified 
important public spaces, and, during thermal walks (Figure 1B), shared their 
thermal sensation and comfort in these spaces. Physical parameters such as 
temperature, humidity, and GPS locations were also measured. The results were 
collectively interpreted with t he participants. Data show ed that several 
sociodemographic parameters, such as age and gender influence resident’s heat 
perception. 
 
D_CJP (Check out your spot) 
D_CJP is a CS pilot that invites children to share their perspectives on the city 
of Delft and take an active role in shaping it. Too often, urban spaces are 
designed without the voices of young residents, reinforcing an adult -centered 
view of the city. This pilot aims to strengthen children’s sense of agency, foster 
their willingness to participate in civic life, and contribute to creating safer and 
more comfortable places for them to enjoy.  Using a methodology developed at 
Utrecht University, the project began with participants collecting data on their 
favorite spots around Delft, mapping not o nly where these places are but also 
what makes them important. In the second phase, the focus shifted to 
redesigning DOK, the city’s public library. Supported by Blok74, an organization 

 10 
specializing in participatory urban planning, children engaged in a co -design 
process to create their own concepts for the library’s future (Figure1C). TU Delft 
researchers then transformed these ideas into detailed drawings and shared 
them with the library, aiming to inform its next redesign (Figure  1D). In this 
way, D_CJP combines playful exploration with tangible impact, demonstrating 
that children’s ideas can shape the spaces they use. 
 
T_NT (Niine street) 
The focus in Tallinn is on the use of digital tools to support the potential of 
changing planning practices in the context of digital transformation, thereby 
contributing to better inclusion through CS and other collaborative processes. 
T_NT pilot addresses the limited use of citizens’ feedback in urban planning in 
Tallinn to identify improvements in citizens’ engagement processes aligned with 
the Tallinn 2035 strategy goals of creating friendly urban spaces, promoting 
healthy mobility, and designing street s as welcoming community spaces. The 
project tested a digital visualization tool, UrbanistAI, to facilitate co -design 
activities and to assess the tool’s usability in collaboration with citizens and other 
stakeholders in urban planning projects (Figure 1E). Participants explored how 
AI-generated images could transform abstract ideas into shared, visualized 
urban concepts. The Kalamaja Museum, a community museum where neighbors 
curate and co-create exhibitions, actively participated in the pilot (Figure 1F). 
The results demonstrated that AI tools like UrbanistAI enhance citizen 
collaboration, sparking meaningful discussions and fostering a collective vision. 
Despite the tool's limitations, it successfully encouraged new perspectives and 
innovative ideas, supporting a more inclusive, citizen -centered approach to 

 11 
urban planning. The results offer actionable recommendations for improving 
Tallinn’s citizen feedback and engagement practices.   
 
V_UH (Urban Heat Stories) 
Data on urban climate change and social inequalities are becoming available 
through increasingly sophisticated modeling and simulation at ever smaller 
scales, which is particularly important for urban planning. However, there is a 
lack of knowledge about the perception of heat and related social effects at the 
micro level. To bridge this gap, V_UH collect s qualitative and quantitative data 
on small -scale heat effects and individual perceptions, aiming to derive 
recommendations for city planning. During the preparation phase, stakeholder 
interviews and workshops identified the research topic , target groups  and 
potentially connectable urban planning processes, instruments and authorities . 
The pilot ultimately involved two groups: i) senior citizens, as they are especially 
affected by urban heat, and ii) school children, as their needs are often 
overlooked in urban planning. The first workshop comprised community mapping 
exercises and questionnaires as a basis for gathering qualitative data (Figure 
1G). Areas and points of interest were defined together with the co-researchers, 
and “thermal walks” for temperature measurements were carried out. In a 
second workshop, the library's data visualization space hosted the collective data 
interpretation, highlighting  its role as social infrastructure (Figure 1 H). The 
results were presented to the city administration, which expressed its interest in 
further developing this approach. 
 
V_GF (GUMPI Research) 

 12 
The aim of the V_GF pilot is to find ways to engage marginalized groups in 
redesigning their neighborhood and to provide additional data, in the form of 
perceptions and concerns, to an ongoing planning process in the city of Vienna. 
The project focused on two groups: i) “single parents” (mostly single mothers) 
and ii) adolescent school pupils. Both groups depend on high-quality public space 
and well-functioning public transport, but they ha d not been the focus of the 
planning process prior to the research i ntervention. Defining itself as a low -
threshold urban knowledge hub, the academic library served as a contact point 
for schools and single parents, took on roles in stakeholder and citizen 
engagement, and hosted CS workshops. Methods used within the cooperation 
with schools included community mapping and photowalks, brainstorming, and 
collective data interpretation. Methods used within the cooperation with the 
single parents’ group included contextualization of local urban development, 
mapping, model-making, expert interviews, research cafés and a research diary 
with reflection protocols. Results were communicated to city planning officials 
through a booklet and co-reflection workshops. 
 
Methods  
The framework to develop the analysis lies on  the intersection between CS, 
sustainable urban transformation , urban sustainability and urban planning 
(Bonhoure et al., 2025, 2024) . Before starting to implement the pilots  and  
based on theoretical-conceptual groundwork (Peer, 2023), in-house prospective 
work on open science skills (Bernhardt et al., 2024) and on landscape analysis 
of open science related to urban sustainability practices (Peer et al., 2024), three 
main dimensions , subdivided into several subdimensions,  were identified: 

 13 
Individual Competency Development (IC), Social Learning (SL ) and Inter- and 
Transdisciplinary Collaboration (TC).  Members of partner institutions directly 
involved in the pilots are referred to as promoting teams’ members.  The full 
description of the subdimensions is provided as supplemental material (see 
Supplemental File 1: Table S1). 
 
The IC dimension targets promoting teams’ members skills and competences , 
including, for example, the acquisition of n ew/better digital competences (e.g. 
use of digital tools or platforms)  (IC5). The SL dimension deal s with n ew 
knowledge and practices structured and facilitated between stakeholders with 
different perspectives and focuses on subdimensions such as inclusion (SL1) or 
urban transformation (SL4). Finally, the TC dimension encompasses  the 
integration of knowledge from various scientific and societal bodies of 
knowledge, namely the use of new concepts/methodologies (TC1) or new tools 
(TC2).  
 
Data collection is deeply intertwined with the analysis process follow ing 
grounded theory methodology (Chun Tie et al., 2019) and emplo ying an 
inductive approach (Rambaree, 2013). Several iterative cycles of data collection 
and analysis were conducted (see Figure 2).  
 
First iteration 
Promoting teams’ members  were invited to complete an anonymous digital 
survey (see Supplemental  File 2: Template 1) assessing the pilots’ outcomes  
according to the previously detailed dimensions (Supplemental File 1: Table S1). 

 14 
The survey included statements such as: “In the OPUSH pilots, citizens’ groups, 
that had not or rarely been involved previously in CS projects or city -related 
decision-making projects, were involved ”. Respondents rated each statement 
using a five -point Likert scale, ranging from 1 ( absolutely disagree) to 5 
(absolutely agree). A total of 20 responses were received, from 9 women and 
11 men. The overall results, as well as those disaggregated by city, are 
presented in Figure 3. As they obtained high evaluation scores in the survey and 
are in addition aligned with the social transformative power of GLAMs , the 
subdimensions SL1, SL2, and SL3—corresponding to inclusion, horizontality, and 
social transformation—were identified for in-depth analysis. 
 
Second iteration 
To ensure an unbiased approach, data collection for the subsequent cycles was 
led by an author (RB) who was not involved in OPUSH activities. Efforts were 
made to ensure balance in gender, professional roles (researchers and 
librarians), and city representation.  The professionals engaged in this iteration 
and the following ones were the promoting teams ’ members most actively 
involved in the pilots ´ implementation (see Supplemental File 3: Table S2 ). 
Participating professionals were asked to jointly compose an essay for each city, 
following a template ( see Supplemental File 2: Template 2 ). These essays 
focused on the previously identified relevant Social Learning subdimensions SL1, 
SL2 and SL3 alongside with a discussion on Knowledge Infrastructures, as this 
is a transversal theme for all pilots. All essays were reviewed by RB, who sought 
clarification from the authors when necessary, and identified the key outcomes 
emerging from each text.  

 15 
 
Third iteration 
To further learn about the  role of GLAMs  in CS initiatives , seven promoting 
teams’ members, both librarians and researchers,  were interviewed using a 
structured interview protocol ( see Supplemental File 2: Template 3 ). The 
interview questions were derived from the key insights identified in the essays 
and  related literature (Cigarini et al., 2021; Filipeti and Mumelaš, 2025; Fortuño 
et al., 2025; Kaarsted et al., 2023; Kowalska -Chrzanowska, 2024; Liu and Liu, 
2023; Mumelaš et al., 2025; Svahn et al., 2025; Tautkeviciene and Pranckute, 
2024).  
 
Of the participating professionals, four were affiliated with research institutions 
and three with public or academic libraries. The three librarians interviewed held 
different professional positions: two were directors of an academic and a public 
library, respectively, while the third, affiliated with the academic library through 
the University open science program, oversaw community and civic engagement 
at the University. The researchers interviewed had backgrounds in architecture 
and urban planning (n=2)  and CS and complex systems science  (n=1). Five 
professionals participated to online interviews, that were transcribed verbatim , 
while two provided written responses. The interviewer (RB) is a native English 
speaker and all interviewees but one were fluent in English. One interview was 
thus conducted in Spanish and subsequently translated into English.  
 
Metrics in Data analysis 

 16 
Data was analyzed using ATLAS.ti software (version 25.0.1). For the four written 
essays, a word frequency analysis was conducted. Only nouns were considered 
for the analysis, and the frequencies of singular and plural forms were combined 
when both appeared. A refined exclusion list was applied to filter non-informative 
nouns such as project or pilot. A total of 698 nouns were encountered across all 
four essays, and a cumulative frequency threshold was set (37,5%) to be able 
to analyze each essay specificity while maintaining a manageable sample. The 
final analysis included 32 nouns, each associated with its relative frequency in 
each essay (see Figure 4). 
 
Data-derived concepts and ideas were inductively coded, refined through 
iteration, and grouped into five families. Each family represents a distinct feature 
analyzed in relation to GLAMs: Community (C), Individual (I), Knowledge 
Transfer (KT), Challenges (CH), and Opportunities (O). A complete list and 
definitions of all codes are provided in Supplemental File 4: Table S5 . All 
documents were coded by a single researcher (RB) to ensure consistency, and 
the frequency of codes were extracted. A sentence or paragraph could be 
associated with multiple codes simultaneously. The occurrences of each code 
within a document (essay or interview) or a group of documents were converted 
into percentages based on the total number of codifications in that document or 
group. This normalization minimized differences due to text length. 
 
For the essays, all codes (55) were ranked in descending order according to their 
total frequency across all four cities. A cut-off threshold was applied when the 
cumulative percentage of codes reached 75% across the grouped essays, 

 17 
resulting in the inclusion of 2 4 codes (see Figure 5). All 2 4 codes appeared in 
Tallinn’s essay, while 23 appeared in Barcelona and 22 in Vienna and Delft. Using 
a higher cut -off increased disparities between cities, whereas a lower cut -off 
excluded some important codes at the city level. 
 
For the interviews, the same number of responses were obtained from librarians 
(n = 3) and from researchers (n = 3). Librarians and researchers were analyzed 
as two separate groups  using the same codes as in the essays . Within each 
group, code frequencies were arranged in descending order, and a cut -off 
threshold was established once the cumulative percentage of codes reached 
90%—a higher percentile chosen to capture a statistically relevant diversity of 
codes. This res ulted in comparable code cut -off frequencies of 1.30% for 
librarians and 1.13% for researchers. Consequently, 30 codes were included for 
the librarians’ interviews and 33 codes for the researchers’ interviews (see Figure 
6 and Supplemental File 7: Figure S3).  
 
Results 
The pilots examined in this study exhibit distinct characteristics in terms of the 
types of GLAMs involved, the communities engaged, their thematic focus, and 
the CS methodologies and participatory dynamics employed. Each pilot is first 
analyzed in detail based on the data collected. 
 
Pilots 
The pilots involved a range of stakeholders, categorized as partners, external 
GLAM partners, educational institutions, social actors, and public administrations 

 18 
(see Supplemental File 3: Table S3). In total, six GLAM institutions participated 
across the pilots, with different levels of engagement. From an organizational 
perspective, two distinct pilot ’s types emerged. The first group —comprising 
D_CJP and T_NT —featured compact organizational structures . They  were 
implemented within a GLAM (a public library and a community museum, 
respectively) with the help of educational institutions and social organizations, 
and, in the case of T_NT, also with city and state administrations. Both initiatives 
pursued concrete, well -defined goals: the redesign of a public library (D_CJP) 
and the testing of AI-based tools for participatory urban planning (T_NT). 
 
In contrast, the other three pilots (B_CC, V_UH, and V_GF) displayed a higher 
level of organizational complexity, involving between 14 and 16 stakeholder s. 
These pilots engaged a diverse array of educational institutions —including 
secondary schools in all three cases, and, in the case of B_CC, also 
kindergartens, primary schools, and special education units. Social actors were 
broadly represented, primarily  through non -governmental organizations 
(NGOs), self -organized citizen groups, and community centers. Additionally, 
public administrations participated in both Vienna-based pilots. 
 
Identification key dimensions and frequent words and codes 
Twenty members of the promoting teams  completed a post –self-reflection 
survey. The outcomes are presented in Figure 3, both disaggregated by city and 
as an overall average. The mean scores per city ranged from 3.2 (Delft) to 4.4 
(Barcelona), indicating notable variation across the perception of the promoting 
teams in different contexts. Moreover, the ranking of the evaluated dimensions 

 19 
differed markedly between cities, highlighting substantial heterogeneity among 
the pilots. The highest-rated dimensions in each city corresponded to either the 
Social Learning (SL)  dimension—specifically, SL1 (Inclusion) in Barcelona and 
Vienna, and SL2 (Horizontality) in Tallinn —or the Inter- and Transdisciplinary 
Collaboration (TC)  dimension—namely, TC1 (Use of new 
concepts/methodologies) and TC2 (Use of New tools) in Delft. Within the SL 
category, SL3 (Social transformation) was also rated highly in Barcelona, Delft, 
and Tallinn. 
 
Within the Individual Competency Development (IC) category, which evaluates 
the teams’ members learnings,  the highest -rated dimensions varied across 
cities: Scientific Research in Barcelona; Open Knowledge and Data Literacy  in 
Delft and Tallinn; Communication in Tallinn; and Data-Based Decision-Making in 
both Barcelona and Tallinn. On the contrary , competencies such as Project 
Organization and Management , Digital Competencies , and Teamwork were 
consistently among the lowest rated across all cities. On average, SL1 – 
Inclusion emerged as the highest -rated dimension overall, followed by SL3 – 
Social Transformation and TC2 – Use of New Tools. 
 
Complementary insights were derived from the nouns’ frequency analysis of the 
essays (Figure 4). Nouns  referring to key actors in the CS pilots—such as 
citizen(s), group(s), participant(s), and stakeholder(s)—appeared with high (3–
4%) or medium (1 –3%) frequency across all c ities, underscoring a shared 
emphasis on citizen participation. Certain city pairs also revealed specific 

 20 
thematic alignments, such as a focus on (urban) planning in Tallinn and Vienna, 
and collaboration with schools in Barcelona and Vienna. 
 
Differences, however, confirm the high degree of heterogeneity among cities, as 
each exhibited a distinct lexical pattern.  In Delft, the frequent occurrence of 
children, library/ies, and space(s) (all above 2.5%) reflects both the active 
involvement of children in the library and the fact that this space was itself the 
subject of study. In Barcelona, the prominence of groups and sessions indicates 
an organizational structure in which multiple participant groups engaged in a 
series of iterative sessions. In Tallinn, the frequent use of community/ies and 
tool(s) aligns with the testing of new digital tools (UrbanistAI) in collaboration 
with local communities. Finally, Vienna gives much importance to research, 
which is organized through workshops.

In terms of overall codes frequency (Figure 5), the three most prevalent codes 
belong to the Community (C) family: Community Engagement , Stakeholder

Community Engagement and Stakeholder Engagement—show consistently high

The second code family that stands out is Knowledge Transfer (KT). Codes such

Perspectives (C), Delft prioritized Public Visibility  (I), Tallinn highlighted

Engagement, mostly in Vienna and Communication, mostly in Barcelona.   The 
Opportunities (O) spotted were Increased Awareness & Visibility and Policy Shift. 
 
GLAMs, CS, and urban sustainability challenges  
A broader analysis of the synergies between CS and GLAM institutions was 
conducted through structured interviews. The explor ation allows to figure out , 
from a general perspective, how CS initiatives and GLAMs can strengthen their 
collaboration and maximize mutual benefits.

Discussion  
The results of our cross-case analysis indicate that GLAMs can serve as powerful 
allies in the development and implementation of CS initiatives addressing urban 
sustainability challenges, including urban planning, public space design, and 
climate-related vulnerabilities. The involvement of GLAM staff in CS generates 
informal and non-formal learning settings for capacity building at the individual 
and organisational level, enabling even better support for transdisciplinary, 
participatory research in the future (Lang et al., 2012).   
 
GLAMs’ differential value 
In Tallinn, collaboration with a community museum strengthened the local 
embedment of participatory urban planning research led by the Faculty of 
Architecture and Urban Planning. In Vienna, the academic library’s efforts and 

 24 
the use of its data visualization facilities , combined with the engagement of 
specific community groups (e.g., older adults, single -parent NGOs, and 
students) and the expertise of an urban transformation research team, created 
a framework to investigate urban heat and adapt urban planning research to the 
needs of underrepresented populations. 
 
Still in relation to the  urban heat topic, in Barcelona, a public library served as 
a connector between a citizen science –focused research group, residents, and 
community organizations. This collaboration built on the library’s previous 
support to residents’ demands to ensure neighbors’ access to climate shelters. 
In Delft, through the university’s Open Science program —implemented by the 
Academic Library and the Faculty of Architecture —an innovative co -design 
process was embedded within a public library tha t already hosted a well -
established group of engaged children. 
 
The results also revealed a complex and multifaceted picture of the CS pilots 
involving GLAM institutions  when addressing urban sustainability issues . Each 
pilot engaged a diverse set of actors  and institutions , carefully selected to 
address specific urban sustainability challenges, and all displayed a notable 
degree of organizational and thematic complexity. Despite their differences, 
several common features emerged across cases. A shared emphasis on 
community and stakeholder engagement, the pursuit of soc ial transformation, 
the operationalization of knowledge transfer, and the inclusion of 
underrepresented groups consistently characterized the pilots. In two pilots, 

 25 
moreover, digital innovation was represented with the use of UrbanistAI and of 
a dedicated facility for data visualization.  
 
Contrasting results with GLAMs literature 
The results presented here have an empirical foundation, as they are based on 
real case studies. It is, however, interesting to analyze how they align with more 
prospective studies. For example, Svahn et al. (2025) recently identified three 
key concepts underpinning CS in public libraries, based on surveys answered by 
librarians: People and partnerships , Spaces and facilities , and Knowledge 
resources and infrastructures. Using this framework, which differs from the one 
applied in the present study, the specific contributions of GLAM institutions can 
be examined within the context of urban transformation (see Supplemental File 
3: Table S4 ). Across all cities, GLAMs played a  central role in engaging 
participants through diverse channels and mechanisms with different degrees of 
participation in scientific research and urban planning , and different ways of 
generating urban impact in terms of sustainable transformation. 
 
Within the People and Partnerships category, public libraries served as key 
connectors to local entities and communities (Barcelona) or connected to youth 
groups already engaged in other activities where they were asked to contribute 
to research and informing urban planning (Delft). The community museum in 
Tallinn maintained an organic relationship with its surrounding neighborhood in 
terms of institutionalized civic engagement for public space and hosted a CS 
activity where participants were asked to contribute to an AI -supported 
modelling experiment that simulates consultation on a street transformation, 

 26 
which possibly will be planned in future , while the academic library in Vienna 
took the initiative in connecting to local politicians responsible for urban 
planning, planning authorities and commissioned landscape architects and 
planners as well as coordinating community engagement activities. The level of 
planning participation was that of consultation  and participants were asked to 
collaborate in scientific research.  
 
Regarding Spaces and Facilities , GLAM venues were used to host CS sessions 
and workshops, each contributing a distinct added value: serving as the 
headquarters for experiments (Barcelona), functioning as the research object 
itself (Delft), offering a particularly welcoming , inclusive and symbolically 
significant in terms of political empowerment environment (Tallinn), or providing 
access to a specialized data visualization facility  and rooms for workshops and 
presentations (Vienna). 
 
Finally, within the Knowledge Resources and Infrastructures  category, a 
common feature across all pilots was the active participation of GLAM staff in 
the workshops. In Vienna, the academic library staff played an especially 
prominent role—not only as an OPUSH partner but also as but also in supporting 
data visualization through expertise and infrastructure (Data Visualization 
Space). In Barcelona, the library staff’s involvement extended beyond the 
project itself, as they had previously advocated for exten ded summer opening 
hours to ensure residents could access the library as a climate refuge during 
extreme heat events. 
 

 27 
GLAMs and the community 
While approaches to engaging communities may vary, the GLAMs’ engagement 
methods are well-established and operational in the CS initiatives studied. A hint 
to support  this effective community integration is reflected in the perceived 
impact of the pilot projects on inclusion (see Figure 3) and the recurring 
prominence of the Community Engagement code across all data sets (see Figure 
5 and Supplemental File 6: Figure S2). 
 
Librarians interviewed also assert their roles as connectors within the pilots’ 
ecosystems (see Figure 6). Their knowledge of the urban context and close ties 
with local communities can help counterbalance power asymmetries perceived 
by researchers (see Figure 6).  These findings reinforce previous work 
highlighting the potential of libraries as community hubs for citizen science 
initiatives in specific projects (Cigarini et al., 2021; Fortuño et al., 2025; Ignat 
et al., 2019)  and recurrently discussed in prospective surveys or systematic 
literature reviews (Cohen, C., 2015; Filipeti and Mumelaš, 2025; Kowalska -
Chrzanowska, 2024; Liu and Liu, 2023; Martek et al., 2022; Mumelaš et al., 
2025; Mumelaš and Martek, 2024; Nielsen et al., 2024; Nurfarawahidah et al., 
2025, 2023; Pimentel and Esteves Gomes, 2024; Rammutloa, 2023; Silva-Aboy 
and Martin -Melon, 2025; Svahn et al., 2025; Tautkeviciene and Pranckute, 
2024). 
 
As for museums, the results highlight that open and inclusive museums such as 
the Kalamaja community museum in Tallinn, already tightly connected to their 
surrounding community, are possible new partners for CS urban sustainability 

 28 
projects, building on the previously positive experiences reported (Hedvall Per-
Olof et al., 2018; Runnel et al., 2024).  
 
Another insight is the converging vision of public and academic librarians within 
this context, highlighting strong potential for collaboration between the two 
types of institutions and nuancing the barriers identified in a previous publication 
(Martek et al., 2022) . In two pilots, both types of libraries were involved 
simultaneously. In D_CJP, the academic library contributed to framing the pilot 
within an open science context, while the pilot itself was implemented at the 
public library, which was also the focus of the study. In V_GF, workshops with 
students were conducted in both schools and the academic library, whereas 
workshops with single parents took place in the public library. The results also 
show that diverse and complex partnerships not only with GLAMs bu t also with 
civil society organizations, educational institutions and public administrations are 
necessary to deal with the complexity of urban sustainability challenges.  
 
In another level and in the interviews, librarians and researchers both recognized 
the critical role of community engagement and viewed the inclusion of 
underrepresented groups as a key opportunity  for their own libraries and for 
their academic research . However, researchers emphasized the importance of 
individual engagement, informal knowledge, and capacity building, whereas 
librarians focused more on libraries’ social and connective role within the 
community. In all  cases, though, t he collaboration between researchers and 
GLAMs in the pilots extends well beyond a mere utilitarian and unbalanced  

 29 
relationship—one in which GLAMs simply provide participants for initiatives and, 
in return, receive new CS activities.  
 
The results show evidence that the establishment of a horizontal relationship 
and co-learning focus between GLAMs and CS practitioners positively influence 
the diversity and relevance of the pilots implemented. Namely, long/mid-term 
funding allowed for sustained interactions between CS researchers and GLAMs 
for three years and cross -city approach enabled a deeper and structured 
reflection as well as mutual learning .  Similar strategies with long -term 
collaboration might be key  to deepen the mutual understanding and trust 
between GLAMs and researchers. 
 
On a more conceptual side, if  CS practitioners recognize GLAMs as important 
social infrastructures (Latham and Layton, 2019) , capable of generating new 
community-based knowledge, and GLAMs view CS initiatives as a means to 
diversify and enhance the societal relevance of their participatory and 
collaborative activities (Aabø, 2005), a full range of possibilities open . Among 
them, urban sustainability , which refers to the application of sustainability 
principles in cities, encompassing societal, economic, and environmental 
dimensions (Finco and Nijkamp, 2001; Jenks and Jones, 2008; Pallathadka et 
al., 2023), is a quite unexplored field albeit the clear interest of residents  to 
have a voice  in urban planning . CS initiatives in which GLAMs are taking part 
can make unique contributions.

Conclusion  
This cross-case study in four different cities, involving five pilots and six different 
GLAM institutions, provided new empirical insights regarding the present and 
possible future role of GLAMs in relation to urban CS initiatives. Results are 
limited to European cities, but it will be of interest to extend the analysis to other 
urban contexts such as in Global South cities. 
 
In relation to the  communalities in -between the five CS pilots implemented 
(RQ1), all pilots showed a strong community -oriented focus, often engaged 
citizens’ groups that are usually not involved or underrepresented  in urban 
sustainability discussions and urban planning processes. In terms of differences 
(RQ1), the pilots presented some diversity in terms of organizational structure 
(compact versus complex), the types of actors involved , the thematic 
background and the systemic interface to ongoing urban planning processes. 
 
Regarding the  distinctive contributions of GLAMs within these CS initiatives 
(RQ2), GLAMs showed to be powerful connectors to citizens’ groups and local  
socially relevant partners, due to their embedment in the community and their 
physical and digital infrastructures.  The alliance with GLAMs also situates CS 
initiatives in a more social and communitarian venue, shifting the individual 
perspective and power imbalance issues and democratizing scientific 
production.  
  
A way to more effectively operationalize the CS–GLAM alliance (RQ3) would be 
to strengthen the links between the CS academics and GLAM staff , building 

 31 
stable and long -term relationships as well as enhancing horizontal mutual 
learning. Likewise, the implementation in GLAMs of urban sustainability 
challenges-related CS initiatives  (RQ3) is a realistic opportunity  in many 
knowledge ecosystems as GLAMs can offer the necessary social infrastructure to 
develop research agenda , over different countries and cities . Integrating CS 
within GLAMs strengthens their role as transformative knowledge hubs  and 
potentially enable community-driven action s targeting urban sustainability 
challenges.
