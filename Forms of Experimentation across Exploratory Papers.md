Abstract
This positioning paper takes a step back to look at exploratory
papers that were selected for PDC 2026, and it finds some com-
mon themes in how they experiment with Participatory Design.
Instead of focusing on specific topics, these papers together explore
the bigger questions about how participation works, like what it
means to participate, who gets to participate, and how we can make
participation more inclusive and durable. This positioning paper
identifies six main areas where these experiments are happening:
thinkingofparticipationasawayofbeingratherthanjustamethod,
considering non-human actors as important participants, design-
ing for situations where there are conflicting interests, building
infrastructure to support participation as a political act, challenging
traditional ways of knowing and making meaning, and focusing on
long-term commitments that go beyond individual lifetimes. These
areas aren’t fixed or separate, but rather they overlap and evolve
together. When we look at all exploratory papers together, we get
a sense of a field that is actively questioning its own foundations
and trying to find new ways to work together in a world that is
constantly changing. This positioning paper argues that this kind
of experimentation is not just something that happens on the side,
but it is actually a key part of what makes Participatory Design able
to navigate complex social and technical issues and build collective
futures.

1 INTRODUCTION: EXPERIMENTATION AS A
MODE OF DISCIPLINARY RENEW AL
Participatory Design (PD) has never been a field that advances
through linear accumulation. Its history is marked by discontinu-
ities, tensions, and repeated acts of redefinition. From the early
Scandinavian engagements with workplace democracy to contem-
porary forms of civic participation, public-sector innovation, and
socio-ecological transition, PD has grown through situated exper-
iments that test not only methods but also values, alliances, and
assumptions. Experimentation, in this sense, is not an accessory to
the discipline; it has functioned as a mode of disciplinary renewal,
enabling PD to confront shifting relations among technology, insti-
tutions, publics, and everyday life.
It is important to clarify from the outset that the forms of ex-
perimentation discussed in this positioning paper should not be
understood as entirely new thematic territories within PD. Many
have long genealogies in the field. Infrastructuring participation
hasbeencentraltoPD’sevolutionformorethanadecade[ 9,17,27].
Agonistic participatory design has foregrounded conflict as consti-
tutive rather than aberrational [2, 23, 24]. Feminist, decolonial, and
care-oriented traditions have insisted on the epistemic and ethical
dimensions of participation [6, 14, 26]. More-than-human perspec-
tives have drawn on relational ontologies and STS traditions to
destabilize anthropocentric assumptions [1, 15, 19,28].
Whatdistinguishesthepresentmomentisnotnovelty,butrecom-
position and intensification. These lineages are being reassembled
in response to overlapping crises—climate breakdown, democratic
erosion, algorithmic governance, geopolitical conflict, and epis-
temic fragmentation. The theme of PDC 2026—Peace, Dialogue
and Coexistence—makes this conjuncture explicit. Peace cannot
be reduced to the absence of direct violence [14]; dialogue cannot
be assumed as neutral deliberation [8]; coexistence cannot be ro-
manticized as harmony, but must be negotiated across difference,
inequality, and conflict [23, 25].
The exploratory papers selected for PDC 2026 are particularly re-
vealing in this regard. Exploratory contributions often occupy a dis-
tinctive epistemic position: they may be less invested in consolidat-
ing results and more committed to probing uncertainty. They com-
monly articulate work in progress, conceptual proposals, method-
ological provocations, or early empirical insights. This does not
make them weaker as scholarly contributions; rather, it makes
them sensitive indicators of where the field is trying to go, what
it is struggling to articulate, and which questions are becoming
unavoidable.
A transversal reading of these papers suggests that what is
emerging is not simply a set of novel topics. Many of the themes
addressed—democracy, inclusion, care, technological mediation,
institutional change, ecological crisis—are not new to PD. What is

new is the way in which these themes are being approached: the
underlying conditions of participation are being reworked. The
contributions collectively explore how participation is sustained
or undermined, how agency is distributed, how infrastructures
shape power, how knowledge becomes legitimate, and how time is
conceptualized within participatory processes. In other words, the
field appears to be experimenting with the structural premises that
enable participatory practice.
This positioning paper does not summarize individual contri-
butions, nor does it attempt to rank or typologize them exhaus-
tively. Instead, it identifies recurring forms of experimentation
that traverse the exploratory papers. These forms are not mutually
exclusive; their boundaries are blurred. They overlap, intersect, and
sometimes contradict one another. They are best understood as
emerging configurations, still in motion, rather than stable cate-
gories. They offer a snapshot—a provisional photograph—of a field
in transition. Such a photograph is not predictive. It cannot tell us
how these forms will evolve, consolidate, or fade. Yet it can help
articulate the present terrain of experimentation and the questions
that terrain raises.
This positioning paper proceeds in three moves. First, it clarifies
the interpretive approach used to read across the exploratory pa-
pers, framing this as a form of mapping that remains deliberately
non-taxonomic. Second, it articulates six forms of experimentation:
participation as condition; expanding agencies; designing within
antagonism; infrastructuring participation; epistemic reconfigura-
tion; and temporal reorientation. Third, it draws out implications
in the form of open questions and provisional orientations for the
future of PD.
Throughout, I maintain two commitments. The first is to treat
exploratory contributions as central material for the conference:
they are not a margin or an appendix, but a space where PD re-
hearses possible futures and tests its own boundaries. The second
is to avoid turning that rehearsal into a premature synthesis. The
aim is not closure, but clarity about what is currently in motion.
2 HOW TO READ A MOVING FIELD: A
TRANSVERSAL MAPPING
Tospeakof“formsofexperimentation”isalreadytomakeamethod-
ological choice. The choice is not to cluster papers by theme in the
conventional sense, nor to reduce them to methodological families,
nor to sort them by application domain. Instead, it is to read across
them for recurring patterns in what is being tested. In PD, this
question matters because “participation” is never only a technique.
It is a political arrangement, an epistemic stance, a moral claim,
and an infrastructural achievement [9, 18]. A transversal mapping
therefore looks for how participation is being reconfigured along
these dimensions.
Such a mapping is necessarily interpretive. It involves identi-
fying repeated moves across different contexts: how authors de-
fine participation, where they locate agency, what they treat as a
medium, how they position institutions, which temporal horizons
they assume, what kinds of knowledge they privilege, and how
they handle conflict and asymmetry. The mapping is also neces-
sarily partial. The field is larger than fifty-five papers; the papers
themselves are partial windows; and the forms of experimentation
identified here are not exhaustive. The point is not to legislate the
field’s future but to offer a plausible reading of how PD is currently
renewing itself.
This transversal mapping is deliberately not a formal coding
exercise. It does not proceed through a predefined analytical grid,
nor does it claim the neutrality of a systematic content analysis.
Instead, it draws on a qualitative reading practice that is common
in disciplinary positioning work: a close attention to recurring
conceptual moves, rhetorical framings, and problem formations
across heterogeneous contributions. The aim is not exhaustiveness
but intelligibility—making visible patterns that might otherwise
remain implicit when papers are read in isolation.
To do so, the mapping follows a set of orienting questions that
repeatedly surface in PD scholarship: how is participation defined
and bounded; where is agency located and how is it distributed;
what is treated as the medium of participation (workshops, infras-
tructures, artefacts, platforms, narratives); how are institutions
positioned (as partners, obstacles, sites of negotiation, or targets of
critique); what temporal horizon is assumed (project cycles, long
durations, intergenerational scales); and what kinds of knowledge
are authorized or contested through participatory practice. These
questions are not imposed from outside; they are inferred from
the papers’ own emphases and from the way they mobilize PD’s
conceptual repertoire.
A further limitation must be stated. The exploratory papers se-
lected for PDC 2026 are not the field as a whole. They reflect a
particular selection moment, shaped by the conference call, the con-
ditions of contemporary research, and the generative constraints
that make exploratory work possible. The mapping therefore does
not claim to represent PD comprehensively. Rather, it treats the
selected exploratory corpus as a situated window into how the
field is currently experimenting with itself. In this sense, the six
forms proposed here should be read neither as a taxonomy nor as
a definitive diagnosis, but as a provisional lens—useful insofar as it
helps articulate what is now becoming difficult to ignore.
Thedecisiontoforegroundblurredformsratherthandiscretecat-
egories responds to a recurring tension in PD scholarship. On the
one hand, PD often benefits from conceptual consolidation, which
helps clarify its commitments and make its claims legible in inter-
disciplinary arenas [18]. On the other hand, PD risks domesticating
its own critical force when it stabilizes too quickly. Exploratory
contributions tend to resist such stabilization. They often hold
ambiguity, prototype in conceptual form, or articulate provisional
propositions. Treatingthemasblurredformsisthereforeconsistent
with their epistemic character.
Within this interpretive frame, the six forms that follow should
be read as overlapping configurations. A paper experimenting with
infrastructuring participation might simultaneously experiment
with epistemic redistribution; a paper exploring distributed agency
might also question temporality; a paper engaging antagonism
might reframe participation as condition rather than method. The
forms do not compete; they entangle.

3 PARTICIPATION AS CONDITION: FROM
EVENTFUL METHODS TO SUSTAINED
ECOLOGIES
The first form of experimentation, and arguably the most pervasive
across the exploratory papers, involves relocating participation
from method to condition. PD has long been associated with recog-
nisable participatory formats: co-design workshops, design games,
probes,livinglabs,participatoryprototyping,collaborativescenario
building. These formats remain vital. Yet across many exploratory
contributions, participation appears less as a discrete event and
more as a fragile condition that must be maintained, governed, and
cared for. [12]
This shift can be understood as a response to two pressures. The
first is temporal: participation rarely ends when the workshop
ends. PD practitioners have long noted that participatory processes
entail long durations of relationship building, trust negotiation, and
institutional coordination. The second is political: participation is
increasingly embedded in contexts where institutions are unstable,
publics are fragmented, and infrastructures are contested. Under
suchconditions, participationcannotsimplybe“organized”; itmust
be sustained against forces that undermine it.
Several exploratory contributions therefore treat participatory
practice as the creation of enabling ecologies. Instead of focusing
on the moment of co-design, they focus on what makes co-design
possible: the infrastructures that hold an ongoing conversation,
the artefacts that circulate and anchor meaning over time, the
governance arrangements that distribute responsibility, and the
care practices that enable sustained involvement. This resonates
with infrastructuring approaches in PD [9, 17, 27], but here the
emphasis is on fragility and endurance. Participation becomes a
maintenance problem as much as an invention problem.
Seen from this angle, the practical “site” of participation shifts.
It is no longer only the co-design session, the workshop, or the
intervention, but also the socio-material arrangements that make
these events possible and meaningful over time. Participation-as-
condition brings to the foreground the work of maintaining access,
continuity, and interpretability: keeping channels open, sustain-
ing shared reference points, revisiting decisions, onboarding new
participants, negotiating responsibilities, and absorbing frictions
without collapsing the process. This work often happens outside
the spectacular moments that are easiest to narrate in research
accounts, yet it is precisely what determines whether participatory
practices remain viable.
This also has consequences for governance. If participation is
an ecology, then it requires forms of stewardship that can hold
together openness and responsibility. Here, PD’s infrastructuring
sensibility intersects with broader discussions on commons-like ar-
rangements and collective resource management [25, 27,29]. Partic-
ipatory infrastructures—archives, platforms, protocols, civic media,
and shared artefacts—can be understood as resources that must be
governed, not merely built. Governance, however, is never neutral:
it distributes authority, sets thresholds of access, and determines
who can withdraw, contest, or reshape the conditions of participa-
tion. The question thus becomes how to design governance that
remains responsive and contestable, rather than rigidly procedural.
Participation-as-condition also reframes evaluation in a deeper
way than is often acknowledged. Conventional indicators tend to
privilege what is countable and time-bounded: attendance, out-
puts, deliverables, short-term impacts. Yet when participation is
understood as a condition, what matters are often qualitative and
longitudinal: whether interpretive authority has shifted, whether
participants gain sustained capacity to act, whether relationships
endure across conflict, whether infrastructures remain legible and
accessible, and whether the process can recover from breakdown.
These are not easily captured by standard evaluation frameworks,
but they are central to the political claims PD often makes.
Finally, participation-as-condition resonates strongly with the
conference theme, without needing to make it the paper’s organiz-
ing axis. Peace, dialogue, and coexistence are not outcomes that
can be “achieved” through a single participatory event; they are
ongoing accomplishments that depend on maintenance, repair, and
institutional endurance. In this sense, the emphasis on condition
does not simply expand PD’s methodological repertoire; it reasserts
a foundational insight of the field—that participation is always a
fragile achievement, continuously made and unmade.
Understanding participation as condition also complicates PD’s
relationship with evaluation. If participation is an ecology rather
than an event, then conventional metrics—number of participants,
number of sessions, deliverables produced—become insufficient.
What matters is the durability of relationships, the redistribution
of interpretive authority, the ongoing capacity to negotiate conflict,
and the degree to which participatory conditions remain accessible
over time. The question is not only whether participation occurred,
but whether the conditions that made it possible remain in place,
adapt, or collapse.
This form of experimentation also reframes PD’s ethical commit-
ments. When participation is treated as condition, questions of care
and maintenance become central. Care scholarship in design and
STS has argued that sociotechnical systems are sustained through
often invisible labour [3–5]. Exploratory contributions echo this
insight by foregrounding the work required to keep participatory
conditions alive: coordination, translation, emotional labour, nego-
tiation of institutional procedures, and the cultivation of safe or at
least workable spaces of disagreement.
A future-oriented implication follows. If PD increasingly under-
stands participation as condition, the field may need to develop
stronger conceptual tools for describing and designing participa-
tory maintenance. What does “participatory durability” mean?
How can participatory conditions be protected without becoming
exclusionary? How can participation remain open while also ac-
countable? These questions point beyond method to governance
and ethics. [7]
4 EXPANDING AGENCIES: PARTICIPATION
BEYOND THE HUMAN-CENTERED FRAME
The second form of experimentation concerns the expansion and
destabilization of agency. PD has always been shaped by shifting
assumptions about who participates. Early PD often presupposed
identifiablehumangroups—workers, users, citizens. Contemporary
PD has expanded to communities, publics, marginalized groups,
institutional stakeholders, and dispersed networks. Exploratory

contributions extend this further, experimenting with more-than-
human and distributed agency.
This expansion takes multiple forms. Some contributions inter-
rogate artificial intelligence and algorithmic systems as mediators
of participation, shaping what is visible, how knowledge circu-
lates, and which voices are amplified. Others foreground ecologi-
cal entities—animals, rivers, forests, atmospheres—as participants
whose agency cannot be reduced to human preferences. Still others
experiment with material and microbial processes, treating them as
co-constitutiveforcesinpracticesofdesignandparticipation. Place-
naming and linguistic ontologies appear as sites where agency is
distributed through grammar, animacy, and relational categories
rather than individual intention.
These experiments resonate with relational ontologies and more-
than-human scholarship [1, 15, 19, 22]. They also align with the
broader “ontological turn” in design, where the focus shifts from
designing objects to designing relations and worlds [11, 15, 19].
In this frame, agency is not an attribute possessed by discrete
subjects but an effect of assemblages. Participation is not merely
a matter of inviting actors into a process; it is a process through
which actors and boundaries are enacted.
However, the exploratory contributions also reveal how diffi-
cult it is to operationalize expanded agency within participatory
practice. Nonhuman entities do not speak in conventional delib-
erative formats. Their agency is often mediated through proxies,
interpretive practices, sensors, or representational devices. Artifi-
cial intelligence systems complicate authorship, accountability, and
transparency. Ecological entities operate on temporal scales that
exceed human project cycles. Material processes challenge the idea
that agency is intentional.
These challenges generate a set of methodological and ethical
tensions. How can PD engage more-than-human agency without
turning it into metaphor? How can nonhumans be represented
without being appropriated? How can designers claim to work
with distributed agency while maintaining accountability to hu-
man communities, especially those already marginalized? These
tensions are not problems to be “solved” once and for all; they are
themselves constitutive of the experimentation underway.
One implication is that PD may need new vocabularies for de-
scribing participation when agency is distributed. Terms such as
“stakeholder” may become insufficient. The field may need to artic-
ulate new modes of attunement, representation, and accountability
that acknowledge the limits of deliberation while still sustaining
democratic commitments. Exploratory contributions suggest that
these vocabularies are emerging, but still unsettled.
5 DESIGNING WITHIN ANTAGONISM:
PARTICIPATION UNDER STRUCTURAL
CONFLICT
The third form of experimentation situates PD within antagonis-
tic contexts. PD’s relationship to conflict has always been com-
plex. While PD has often been associated with collaborative co-
production, its origins are deeply political, rooted in struggles
over workplace power and the distribution of expertise. In recent
decades, as PD has expanded into public-sector and civic contexts,
it has sometimes been framed through deliberative models em-
phasizing dialogue and consensus. Yet agonistic theory has long
argued that democratic life is constituted by conflict rather than
consensus [11, 13]. Exploratory contributions intensify this agonis-
tic sensibility by engaging contexts where conflict is structural and
institutions are not necessarily partners.
Across the exploratory papers, antagonism appears in multiple
ways. Some contributions confront institutional repression and
surveillance, where participatory infrastructures may be targeted,
censored, or disrupted. Others address contexts such as carceral
institutions, where participation is entangled with coercion and
asymmetrical power. Still others engage polarized public spheres,
where participation is shaped by distrust, disinformation, and con-
testation over legitimacy.
In such contexts, participation cannot be assumed to be safe,
open, or cooperative. It is vulnerable to capture, manipulation,
or suppression. Designers may find themselves positioned not as
facilitators of consensus but as protectors of participatory space.
The design problem becomes how to sustain contestation without
escalating harm, how to enable participation under surveillance,
andhowtonavigateinstitutionalcontextsthatmayinstrumentalize
participation.
This form of experimentation reopens foundational questions for
PD. What does it mean to “participate” in contexts where refusal
may be the most meaningful political act? How can PD avoid
legitimizing oppressive institutions while still engaging with those
affected by them? How can participatory processes sustain conflict
as productive rather than destructive? How can designers remain
accountable when institutions constrain what can be said, shown,
or acted upon?
Agonistic PD scholarship has argued for designing spaces where
conflictcanbearticulatedratherthanerased[ 2]. Exploratorycontri-
butions extend this by foregrounding the infrastructural and digital
conditions of antagonism. Conflict is no longer only discursive; it
is mediated by platforms, surveillance systems, and institutional
architectures. Designing within antagonism therefore becomes an
infrastructural challenge as much as a political one.
A future-oriented implication follows: PD may need to articu-
late more robust theories and practices for “protective participa-
tion”—participation that is not simply inclusive but resilient under
antagonism. This includes questions of security, care, and ethics
that PD has not always foregrounded.
6 INFRASTRUCTURING PARTICIPATION:
FROM PROJECTS TO POLITICAL
ARCHITECTURES
The fourth form of experimentation foregrounds infrastructures
as political architectures of participation. In PD and STS, infras-
tructure has long been understood not as a neutral substrate but
as a relational accomplishment that shapes what can be done, by
whom, and at what cost [19, 27]. Infrastructuring in Participatory
Design extends this by emphasizing that participatory processes
often require creating or reshaping socio-material arrangements
capable of sustaining collective action over time [9, 17].
Across the exploratory papers, participation is configured not
only through meetings and workshops but through archives, civic

platforms, data ecologies, dialogic scaffolds, and media infrastruc-
tures. These arrangements structure visibility, access, and legiti-
macy. They shape which voices persist, how knowledge circulates,
andhowdecisionsarestabilized. Infrastructuresthusbecomeactive
mediators of participation rather than passive supports.
This aligns with Star and Ruhleder’s argument that infrastruc-
tures are embedded, relational, and only visible when they break
down [27]. In participatory settings, infrastructures are often taken
for granted until they fail to hold space for dialogue or collapse
under institutional pressure. The exploratory contributions make
these infrastructures visible as sites of design intervention and
political negotiation.
Importantly, infrastructuring is not only technical but political.
Decisions about who maintains an archive, who governs a plat-
form, who defines categories of evidence, or who has access to
digital systems are decisions about power distribution. This res-
onates with Ostrom’s work on commons governance [25], which
highlights that shared resources require carefully designed insti-
tutional arrangements to avoid enclosure or misuse. Participatory
infrastructures similarly require stewardship, accountability, and
adaptive governance. [2, 16]
However, infrastructuring participation introduces tensions. In-
frastructures tend toward stabilization, while participation often
requires openness and contestability. Systems designed to pro-
tect participants may also limit openness; systems designed for
openness may increase vulnerability to surveillance or harassment.
Institutional uptake can translate participatory outputs into stan-
dardized forms that neutralize dissent [6]. The exploratory papers
surface these tensions without resolving them, showing that infras-
tructuring is itself a site of experimentation.
In relation to Peace, Dialogue and Coexistence, infrastructures
become the material conditions that enable or constrain dialogic
engagement. Dialogue depends not only on willingness but on
systems that sustain interaction. Coexistence requires infrastruc-
tures that can hold plural publics over time. Designing such in-
frastructures becomes a political act that shapes the durability of
participation.
A central question emerges: how can Participatory Design build
infrastructures that sustain participation ethically and politically
while remaining adaptable and resistant to capture?
7 EPISTEMIC RECONFIGURATION:
PARTICIPATION AS MEANING-MAKING
AND KNOWLEDGE POLITICS
The fifth form of experimentation concerns epistemic reconfigura-
tion. Participatory Design has always implied that those affected by
design decisions possess knowledge that matters. Yet knowledge is
not simply present to be included; it is structured by institutional
norms, cultural assumptions, and power relations [19].
Across the exploratory papers, participation becomes epistemic
work. Contributions engage hermeneutical injustice [13], feminist
and decolonial epistemologies [11, 26], and relational ontologies
[1, 15] to question who defines problems, whose interpretations
are credible, and which categories shape collective understanding.
In contexts such as healthcare, migration, environmental gover-
nance, and AI design, participatory practices seek to redistribute
interpretive authority.
Fricker’s concept of epistemic injustice[13] clarifies howindivid-
uals can be wronged in their capacity as knowers when dominant
interpretive frameworks fail to accommodate their experiences.
Several exploratory contributions implicitly address this by expand-
ing collective meaning-making processes. Participation becomes
not only decision-making but interpretive negotiation.
Thisepistemicdimensionconnectsdirectlytodialogue. Dialogue
requires shared interpretive resources; without them, speech does
not translate into understanding. When interpretive frameworks
privilege dominant groups, dialogue risks reproducing inequality
rather than alleviating it. Coexistence, in this sense, depends on
epistemic conditions that allow difference to be articulated and
recognized.
However, institutional translation complicates epistemic plu-
rality. Bureaucratic systems often demand standardization and
comparability. Participatory outputs may be translated into metrics
or categories that flatten nuance. As Callon’s sociology of trans-
lation suggests [4], knowledge does not travel unchanged; it is
transformed through institutional mediation.
The exploratory contributions highlight this fragility. Epistemic
redistribution is provisional and contested. Participatory Design
must therefore grapple with how to sustain plural meaning-making
while engaging systems that require stabilization.
This raises a critical question: how can PD design translation
processes that maintain epistemic plurality rather than neutralize
it?
8 TEMPORAL REORIENTATION:
PARTICIPATION ACROSS LONG HORIZONS
AND LAYERED TIME
The sixth form of experimentation concerns temporality. Many
contemporary challenges addressed by PD—climate change, demo-
cratic erosion, technological transformation—unfold across tem-
poral scales that exceed project cycles. Exploratory contributions
reflect this by experimenting with long-horizon participation and
layered temporalities.
Participation is depicted as unfolding across ecological cycles,
generational memory, and iterative infrastructuring. It involves
long-term role shifts, where responsibility and agency gradually
redistribute among actors. It demands sustained relational labour
rather than episodic intervention.
This resonates with scholarship on care and maintenance [20,
26], which emphasizes ongoing labour as central to sustaining
worlds. It also aligns with democratic theory that frames politics as
continuous negotiation rather than final settlement [23, 24]. Peace,
understood through Galtung’s lens [14], is not static but a dynamic
processrequiringcontinuousefforttoreducestructuralandcultural
violence.
Temporal reorientation challenges performance-driven gover-
nance models and short-term funding structures. Participatory
ecologies require time to build trust, develop interpretive resources,

dialogue requires time; participation requires long-term commit-

9 CONCLUDING REFLECTIONS:
ENTANGLEMENTS AND IMPLICATIONS
The six forms described above are not isolated. They overlap and
entangle. Participation as condition often depends on infrastruc-
turing; infrastructuring shapes epistemic redistribution; epistemic
plurality requires temporal commitment; designing within antag-
onism may demand protective infrastructures; expanding agency
complicates accountability.
These entanglements generate productive frictions. For instance,
expanding agency beyond the human can deepen ethical commit-
ments, but it may also diffuse responsibility. Infrastructuring par-
ticipation can sustain collective action, but it may risk institutional
capture. Designing within antagonism may require limiting open-
ness to protect participants. Epistemic plurality may resist institu-
tional translation, yet institutional engagement may be necessary
for material change.
Such tensions are not failures but indicators of a field grappling
with complexity. Participatory Design has long navigated trade-
offs between empowerment and institutionalization, critique and
collaboration, openness and structure [9, 18]. The exploratory
papers reveal these tensions intensified under contemporary crises.
Taken together, these frictions suggest that the field’s current
experimentation is not only methodological but also programmatic.
Acrosstheexploratorypapers,threeshiftsbecomeespeciallyvisible.
First, PD appears to be moving from a focus on participatory “tools”
toward a concern with participatory conditions—how participation
is sustained, what infrastructures and governance arrangements
are required, and how participation remains legible and actionable
when it travels across institutional contexts. Second, the locus
of critique increasingly targets not only exclusion in the abstract,
but the concrete mechanics through which people, perspectives,
and forms of life remain outside participatory processes—and the
waysinwhichsuch“outsideness”isreproducedbytimeconstraints,
infrastructural barriers, epistemic conventions, and institutional
procedures. Third, PD’s engagement with politics is less often
framed as a question of bringing more voices into a conversation
and more often as a question of designing political architectures:
arrangements that can hold disagreement, protect participation
under antagonism, and support coexistence across asymmetry.
These shifts help explain why the boundaries among the six
forms are blurred. A concern with conditions quickly becomes a
concern with infrastructure; a concern with infrastructure becomes
a concern with knowledge politics; a concern with knowledge poli-
tics becomes a concern with temporality; and antagonism pushes
the field to confront the costs of openness and the ethics of protec-
tion. Rather than resolving these tensions, the exploratory papers
often hold them in place. This “holding” is itself a form of exper-
imentation: a refusal of premature closure in contexts where the
demand for decisive outcomes can easily neutralize complexity.
From this perspective, the six forms should be understood less as
stable categories and more as questions-in-motion. They indicate
where PD is currently testing its own premises: what participation
can mean under conditions of crisis; how dialogue is mediated
by infrastructures and epistemic regimes; how coexistence can
be enacted without denying conflict; and how design can operate
as a political practice without collapsing into either procedural
technocracy or moral rhetoric. The value of mapping these forms is
therefore not to impose unity, but to make the present landscape of
experimentation more speakable—so that future work can engage
it critically, extend it, and contest it.
Framing these patterns as blurred forms of experimentation
avoids premature synthesis. It acknowledges that PD’s future will
likely involve ongoing negotiation among these forms rather than
resolution into a single paradigm.
The exploratory papers selected for PDC 2026 collectively reveal
Participatory Design as a field engaged in structural experimenta-
tion. Participation is relocated from method to condition; agency is
expanded and destabilized; antagonism is embraced as constitutive;
infrastructures are politicized; epistemic plurality is foregrounded;
temporal horizons are extended.
These forms are blurred and evolving. They constitute a pro-
visional photograph of a discipline renegotiating its foundations
in a moment of global crisis. The photograph is not predictive; it
does not determine which forms will dominate. But it provides
orientation by clarifying which structural questions are pressing.
InrelationtoPeace,DialogueandCoexistence,theformsmapped
here suggest that PD is not offering simple reconciliatory tools.
Rather, it is reworking the structural conditions that make peace-
ful coexistence possible under strain. Peace requires reduction of
structural violence [14]; dialogue requires epistemic justice [13]; co-
existence requires infrastructures capable of holding plural publics
over time [27].
For the future of PD, the challenge is not to codify these forms
into a closed framework but to sustain the questions they raise.
How can participatory conditions endure without becoming exclu-
sionary? How can distributed agency be acknowledged without
diffusing accountability? How can participation survive within
antagonistic institutions? How can infrastructures remain open
yet protected? How can plural meaning-making persist within sys-
tems demanding standardization? How can temporal commitments
necessary for coexistence be institutionally supported?
Exploratory contributions do not provide definitive answers.
They are valuable precisely because they hold these questions open.
They show PD thinking in motion—experimenting with its own
premises and rehearsing collective futures in uncertain times. In
a world marked by instability and fragmentation, sustaining such
experimentation is not peripheral to the field. It is central to its
ongoing responsibility.
