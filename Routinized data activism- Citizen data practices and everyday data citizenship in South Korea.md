Abstract
Based on ethnographic research with a grassroots civic hacking community in South Korea, this article examines how
civic hackers enact data citizenship under the temporal and labor pressures of dataﬁed societies. Drawing on a prac-
tice-oriented framework, it analyzes how civic hackers integrate data work with everyday practices to secure autonomy
and navigate limited time and resources. The study develops the concept of routinized data activism to describe data-
mediated civic engagement shaped through habitual participation, peer learning, and coordination. Routinization operates
as a strategy under constraint, enabling civic hackers to cultivate data citizenship not through technical competence or
data access alone, but through the interplay of everyday practices, collective identities, and sociopolitical conditions.
Keywords
Civic hacking, citizen data practice, data citizenship, dataﬁcation, media as practice, routinized data activism, South Korea
Introduction
Contemporary digital capitalism operates by folding every-
day activities— consumption, interaction, and attention—
into continuous processes of data extraction and valuation
through platform-based infrastructures (Zuboff, 2019).
This reconﬁguration reshapes not only economic relations
but also the temporal conditions of civic life, accelerating
work and interaction while constraining time for reﬂection,
care, and collective deliberation (Wajcman, 2015). In this
context, daily interactions with platforms, data infrastruc-
tures, and algorithmic systems become key sites of civic en-
gagement, where power is negotiated and civic claims are
articulated in situated ways (Milan, 2019). To examine
how data citizenship takes shape through ordinary engage-
ments with data under the temporal pressures and labor con-
ditions of dataﬁed societies, this study investigates how
grassroots civic hackers routinize data activism by embed-
ding data practices into their everyday lives.
Civic hacking refers to voluntary efforts by technically
skilled and civically engaged individuals who use digital
technologies and data to address public problems and pro-
mote government accountability (Patel et al., 2013;
Tauberer, 2014). In the early 2010s, nonproﬁt foundations
such as Code for America, Knight Foundation, and
mySociety helped popularize open data and civic hacking
around ideals of openness, collaboration, and data-driven
problem solving in governance and public services
(Baack, 2018; Schrock, 2016). However, civic hacking
has also attracted critique for reproducing the logics of
digital capitalism and promoting entrepreneurial citizen-
ship. Rapid and intensive software production at hacka-
thons cultivates ideal workforces for new forms of
capitalist accumulation in the creative economy (Irani,
2015; Tkacz, 2012). Rather than critically interrogating da-
taﬁcation and institutional power, civic hacking in such
contexts can normalize data-driven solutionism by framing
technological interventions as inherently innovative re-
sponses to public problems (Davies, 2018; Gregg, 2015).
Yet research across diverse national and political con-
texts demonstrates that civic hacking is not a uniform phe-
nomenon. Its practices range from collaborative urban
innovation to explicitly political or subversive interventions

Rather than treating civic hacking and data activism as
separate or fully formed modes of social intervention, this
article brings them into dialogue through a practice-
oriented approach. Drawing from research on the political
potential of everyday data practices (Dencik, 2019;
Fotopoulou, 2019; Kennedy, 2018), this study fore-

into daily routines to challenge information asymmetries,
articulate sociopolitical critique, and negotiate power hier-

Building on scholarship on data citizenship that examines
how critical engagements with data cultivate collective ci-
vic agency and new political subjectivities (Carmi and
Yates, 2023; Gabrys et al., 2016; Isin and Ruppert,
2020), this article analyzes how civic hackers’data citi-
zenship is constituted through their everyday engagements
with data rather than through accelerated production cy-
cles or technocratic interventions.
In this study, citizenship does not denote legal status or a
ﬁxed set of rights and duties. It refers to subjective and per-
formative processes through which individuals understand
their position within networks of public and private rela-
tions and enact civic agency (Hermes and Dahlgren,
2006). From this perspective, communication scholars con-
ceptualize digital citizenship as a process of becoming,
whereby individuals recognize themselves and others as ci-
vic actors through mediated participation (Bakardjieva,
2009; Dahlgren, 2006). In dataﬁed societies, such processes
increasingly unfold through interactions with data infra-
structures and algorithmic systems, giving rise to data citi-
zenship enacted through lived experiences of claiming
citizen rights using data-driven technologies (Hintz et al.,
2018; Isin and Ruppert, 2020).
Drawing on 15 months of ethnographic ﬁeldwork
(2017–2019) with Nullfull, a grassroots civic hacking
community in South Korea, and in-depth interviews con-
ducted between 2017 and 2021, I examine how civic hack-
ers’ everyday data practices constitute sites where data
citizenship is formed and enacted. In the mid-2010s,
amid increasing social inequality and widespread political
distrust, Nullfull pursued slow and sustained forms of data
engagement oriented toward social critique and public ac-
countability. Employing a practice-oriented approach in
digital media and technology studies (Bräuchler and
Postill, 2010; Couldry, 2004; Mattoni and Treré, 2014),
the analysis focuses on how their data-related activities
intersect with other everyday practices such as media
use, social interaction, and informal gathering. These prac-
tices form patterned and adaptive modes of civic engage-
ment with data, which I conceptualize as routinized data
activism.
The analysis asks: What practices structure civic hack-
ers’engagement with data, and what do these practices en-
tail? How are these data practices interwoven with other
routines and social relations? How do South Korea’s histor-
ical transformations of citizenship and contemporary socio-
political conditions shape data citizenship through these
routinized practices? By addressing these questions, the
study offers an empirically grounded account of how data
citizenship takes form through sustained, embodied engage-
ment with data in everyday contexts.
Practice-oriented approach to data
activism and everyday data citizenship
This section develops a practice-oriented framework for
examining civic hacking, everyday data engagement, and
data citizenship in South Korea. It brings together three
strands of scholarship: practice-oriented research in media
and technology studies, studies of citizen data practices
and data citizenship, and scholarship on digital activism
and citizenship in South Korea.
This study ﬁrst draws on practice-oriented research in
media and technology studies, inspired by social practice
theory in sociology (Reckwitz, 2002; Schatzki et al.,
2001). Practice theory conceptualizes social life as consti-
tuted through routinized and embodied activities rather
than as the outcome of individual choices or overarching
structures. A practice consists of interconnected elements
— forms of bodily and mental activities, the use of material
objects, background knowledge and know-how, as well as
affective and motivational orientations (Reckwitz, 2002:
249).
Media scholars have extended this “practice turn” to
understand media and technology not merely as texts or in-
stitutions, but as conﬁgurations of practice situated within
“webs of interdependent action ” (Couldry, 2004). A
practice-oriented approach focuses on what people do
with media and digital technologies and how those activ-
ities are embedded in wider networks of social action
(Bräuchler and Postill, 2010; Mattoni, 2017; Stephansen
and Treré, 2019). As Couldry (2004: 57) explains, such re-
search analyzes how the combination of competency, ma-
teriality, and meaning in media-oriented practices is
enacted and reproduced within the complex web of other

pre-existing practices in everyday life and broader social
processes. The media practice perspective enables contex-
tualized and non-media-centric accounts of mediated activ-
ism by foregrounding actors’agency and practices rather
than media technologies (Mattoni, 2017).
Practice-oriented analysis has also proven useful in stud-
ies of technologists’politics and collective action (Kelty,
2010; Kubitschko, 2015). Kelty (2010), for instance, high-
lights how free software activists’practices of discussion
and argument are integral to the formation of a“recursive
public”oriented toward shared moral and technical imagin-
aries. Similarly, Kubitschko (2015) shows how hackers’
technical interventions are interlocked with media engage-
ment and institutional politics. Together, these studies
underscore how technological work is intertwined with dis-
cursive, organizational, and relational practices, forming
techno-political action.
Building on this perspective, this study approaches civic
hacking as a constellation of interdependent practices that
connect technical work, media engagement, and social
interaction. By foregrounding coordination and inter-
dependency among these practices, a practice-oriented
lens provides the analytical foundation for examining how
civic hackers’activities are entangled with everyday rou-
tines and coordinated for collective action, and how such
entanglements give rise to routinized data activism within
broader social processes.
Second, to understand how civic hackers’everyday en-
gagements with data can constitute data citizenship, this
study draws on scholarship on citizen data practices and
digital acts of citizenship. Whereas data activism is often
framed as coordinated efforts that explicitly challenge
data power, practice-oriented media research broadens the
concept to include grassroots and mundane engagements
with data politics Dencik, 2019; (Fotopoulou, 2019;
Milan, 2019). Milan (2019) deﬁnes data practices as an
open set of activities oriented around data and its infrastruc-
tures, emphasizing how such practices emerge through
communicative, material, and embodied engagements em-
bedded in everyday life. Scholars in this tradition highlight
that working with data— whether building tools, analyzing
datasets, or managing information ﬂows— is often inter-
twined with routine behaviors and social roles (Dencik,
2019; Kennedy, 2018). This perspective is particularly use-
ful for analyzing civic hackers, whose activities span formal
activism, digital tinkering, and everyday media use. While
not always framed as political, these practices can accumu-
late civic meaning when embedded in patterns of care, re-
sponsibility, and social connection.
Building on scholarship on citizen media (Baker and
Blaagaard, 2016; Stephansen, 2016, 2019), Milan and col-
leagues suggest understanding data activism as an evolution
of citizen media with constitutive capacities that reshape
what counts as civic participation and who gets to partici-
pate (Milan and Gutiérrez, 2015; Milan, 2019). Rather
than merely producing alternative information, citizen
media enable individuals to build civic agency and solidar-
ity through participatory, embodied engagement with social
issues (Rodríguez, 2011; Stephansen, 2016). From this per-
spective, citizen data practices can function as sites where
citizenship takes shape through everyday engagements
with civic or political issues using data.
This understanding of data practices as constitutive of ci-
vic agency resonates with critical scholarship on digital citi-
zenship, which approaches citizenship as an ongoing
process through which civic agency is formed and ex-
pressed in digital acts that make claims, contest power,
and reconﬁgure public engagement (Isin and Ruppert,
2020; McCosker et al., 2016). In the context of dataﬁcation,
scholars of civic agency highlight how data citizenship
emerges through grassroots and collective engagements
with data, often unfolding in informal settings (Carmi and
Yates, 2023; Gabrys et al., 2016). These studies emphasize
that the capacity to interpret and act on data develops
through relational processes— such as networks of literacy
and collaborative sense-making— rather than through indi-
vidual technical expertise alone.
Together, studies on citizen data practices and digital
citizenship provide a framework for understanding how ci-
vic hackers collectively build civic agency and enact data
citizenship through their everyday engagements with data.
While existing research on civic hackers often emphasizes
entrepreneurial citizenship and techno-optimistic imagin-
aries in hackathons and data-driven innovation (Avle
et al., 2017; Irani, 2015, 2019), this study instead examines
how civic hackers’
technological and non-technological
practices, embedded in everyday life, contribute to the for-
mation and enactment of data citizenship in speciﬁc socio-
political contexts.
Third, this study draws on scholarship on modern Korean
citizenship and digital activism, providing the socio-
historical grounding to analyze how data practices operate
as acts of data citizenship in South Korea. I situate Korean
civic hackers’practices within a broader transformation in
the repertoire of citizenship shaped by digital technologies
and participatory internet cultures— transformations that ne-
gotiate and contest earlier forms of citizenship grounded in
developmental nationalism.
Studies of modern Korean citizenship emphasize how ci-
vic belonging has historically been structured through nation-
alism and developmentalism, in which citizens were
positioned as contributors to state-led modernization and col-
lective economic goals (Chang, 2012; Cho, 2008). In this de-
velopmental framing, citizenship functioned not only as a
legal status but as a political formation shaped by state
power, economic productivity, and gendered norms, with
shifting expectations of duty and sacriﬁce (Moon, 2005).
These accounts underscore that citizenship in South Korea
has long been a contested terrain marked by uneven access
to civic voice and recognition (Chang, 2022).

Following democratization and the diffusion of the inter-
net in the late 1990s and 2000s, digitally mediated collect-
ive action reshaped the terrain of civic participation.
Research on internet activism and the“netizen” movement
shows how online spaces enabled new modes of mobiliza-
tion and claim-making less dependent on formal organiza-
tions (Kang, 2016; Lee, 2012). Kang (2016) demonstrates
that postauthoritarian digital activism reﬂected changed no-
tions of the state and politics of younger generations and
was also tied to their everyday concerns and struggles for
expanding social and cultural rights. Through sustained par-
ticipation in networked publics, diverse actors cultivated
new forms of civic agency and incorporated new social
claims into public discourse.
Situated within these trajectories of citizenship forma-
tion and digital activism, Korean civic hackers’ engage-
ments with data can be understood as acts of data
citizenship. Their political engagements emerged amid ra-
pid neoliberal transformations following the 1997ﬁnancial
crisis (Cho, 2008; Shin, 2000), alongside changing civic
norms historically grounded in developmental citizenship
and intensiﬁed socio-economic inequalities and precarious
labor conditions (Chang, 2022). In this context, civic hack-
ers in the 2010s extend the repertoire of digital citizenship
by mobilizing data infrastructures, code, and technical ex-
pertise as means of claim-making and public intervention.
In doing so, they participate in a longer history of mediated
civic action while foregrounding the distinctive role of tech-
nologists and data practices in reshaping civic engagement
in contemporary Korea.
Building on this contextual grounding, the following
analysis examines how civic hackers’data practices are sus-
tained through coordination and routinization in everyday
life, and how these practices shape the formation and enact-
ment of data citizenship in South Korea.
Ethnographic ﬁeldwork with civic hackers
in South Korea
This study draws on ethnographic research conducted be-
tween 2017 and 2021 with civic hackers in Seoul, South
Korea, including a 15-month period of on-siteﬁeldwork.
Guided by a practice-oriented framework that emphasizes
socially embedded, coordinated, and habitual dimensions
of media and technology practices, I use ethnography to
examine the material, affective, and relational dynamics
of civic hackers’data work. My data collection combined
participant observation, semi-structured narrative inter-
views, and analysis of publicly shared group materials.
The primary site of this research is Nullfull, the longest-
standing grassroots civic hacking community in South
Korea. Nullfull traces its origins to Code for Seoul (CfS),
founded in 2014 as one of the earliest civic hacking commu-
nities in the country. In the early 2010s, the nonpro ﬁt
Creative Commons Korea organized early initiatives for
open government and data in Korea including Codenamu
and CfS. Civic hacking movements gained nationwide visi-
bility among young technologists through large-scale civic
hackathons, supported by the Seoul Metropolitan
Government and IT corporations (Ahn, 2015; Yang,
2013). Over time, however, many ﬁrst-generation civic
hackers became disillusioned with
institutional collaborations that prioritized technological
showcases and data monetization over participatory govern-
ance. As a result, many early civic hacking communities,
including Codenamu and Code for America–inspired com-
munities, disbanded or signiﬁcantly reduced their activities
within a few years of their founding (for a detailed account
of this formation and decline, see Yoo, 2023).
In contrast, CfS sustained consistent weekly gatherings
into the mid-2010s, even as many founding members de-
parted to initiate their own projects or communities.
Following its reorganization as Nullfull in 2017, the group
continued operating as an independent, developer-led grass-
roots collective. Its continuity and self-directed character
make it a particularly valuable case for examining civic
hacking practices sustained beyond short-term hackathon
cycles and institutional partnerships.
Nullfull’s weekly meetings typically involved 8 –12
regular attendees and operated without formal membership.
While a couple of participants in their 40s and 50s were ex-
perienced developers with stable employment, the majority
were in their 20s and 30s and had experience working as
freelance or contract-based IT workers. Meetings were pub-
licly announced through Meetup and Facebook and held in
spaces open to public or civic community use, such as a
conference room at NewsTapa— an independent investiga-
tive journalism outlet— and the Seoul Public Activities
Center, operated by the city government. While weekly in-
person meetings were held in Seoul, where most partici-
pants lived or worked, the group’s projects, code repositor-
ies, public announcements, and Slack workspace remained
publicly accessible, with the Slack workspace open through
a self-generated invitation link that did not require prior
approval.
I joined the group as a participating member in June
2016, when it was still operating as CfS, and began for-
mal participant observation i n February 2017, witnes-
sing the group’s reorganization into Nullfull in March–
April 2017. Across two main ﬁeldwork periods — nine
months in 2017 and six months in 2019— I regularly at-
tended Monday night meetings and public civic tech
events, including Open Data Day and Global Civic
Hacking Day. Although I lacked technical expertise, I
contributed by posting updates on social media, welcom-
ing newcomers, and converting PDF documents into
machine-readable data. During meetings, I maintained
an observant stance and refrained from in ﬂuencing
group decisions. With informed consent, I took

Nullfull: Reorganizing civic hacking for
autonomy and sustainability
Drawing on participant observation, interviews, and
archival materials, this section examines how Nullfull
reorganized civic hacking around autonomy and sustain-
ability in response to the constrained conditions of IT
work, institutional disillusionment, and limited time and re-
sources. Contrary to assumptions of economic security
among IT workers, freelance software developers in
South Korea often faced long working hours, unpaid over-
time, delayed payments, and unstable employment under
weak labor protections (Lee et al., 2013; Yoo and Lee,
2020). A national survey of 949 software freelancers re-
ported an average annual income of approximately 36.15
million KRW (about USD 32,000), with one-third earning
below 30 million KRW— a relatively limited income given
the high living costs of the Seoul metropolitan area (Yoo
et al., 2019). Industry reports linked these labor conditions
to Korea ’s multi-layered IT subcontracting structures,
which expanded under the post-1997ﬂexible labor regime
(Yoo and Lee, 2020).
Compounding these pressures, government and corpor-
ate actors often framed developers’ work as operations
and maintenance services, limiting their role in organiza-
tional decision-making and system innovation (Yoo et al.,
2019). Against this backdrop, civic hacking initiatives
emerging in the United States in the late 2000s were per-
ceived by Korean developers as alternative spaces for re-
claiming professional autonomy and collaborating with
like-minded peers on public-interest projects (Yoo, 2023).
Many participants in early South Korean civic hacking
communities shared frustrations with exploitative labor
conditions and hierarchical organizational cultures in both
IT ﬁrms and public institutions (Yoo, 2025). The following
analysis shows how these experiences informed Nullfull’s
turn toward personally meaningful engagement, horizontal
collaboration, and slow but sustainable civic hacking
grounded in everyday routines.
Personally meaningful civic engagement

known as CfS, founded in 2014 by Creative Commons
Korea (CCK). Initially, CfS collaborated with Seoul city of-
ﬁcials, built public-service applications, and participated in
government-sponsored hackathons (Code for Seoul, 2015).

social concerns meaningful to members and was feasible
within their everyday constraints. From April 2017 onward,
Nullfull pursued diverse projects, ranging from applications
using government data to initiatives critiquing media bias,
political corruption, and corporate misconduct. Across
these efforts, public beneﬁt operated as a practice-based cri-
terion, negotiated through participants’shared experiences,
identities as civic hackers in Korea, and everyday project
workﬂows.
Reorganizing for sustainable civic hacking
Nullfull’s grassroots reorganization emerged in response to
constraints faced by its members— freelance developers,
college students, contract web designers, and job seekers
who lacked the time, money, or institutional backing to sus-
tain traditional forms of activism. Myﬁeldnotes from 2017–
2019 detail how their Monday night “hacknights” were
carefully structured to use time meaningfully. Meetings be-
gan at 7 PM with brief introductions, then moved to two or
three ongoing projects, each discussed for 20–30 min. The
proposer of a project usually led its session, while others
contributed through coding, data scraping, or research.
Some members took on“housework” such as sharing up-
dates on social media or posting summaries on GitHub.
Around 9:15 PM, the group transitioned to an informal
gathering at a nearby pub.
These post-meeting gatherings were more than social
hangouts— they were spaces for building group identity, ex-
changing information about data policy and tech trends, and
discussing the direction of ongoing projects. Members jok-
ingly called themselves “French-fries-eaters,” referencing

Building the foundations for everyday civic
engagement
Nullfull members shared a commitment to civic action or-
iented toward the public good, but their prior experiences
with hierarchical and exploitative labor conditions led
them to pursue an autonomous path, avoidingﬁnancial sup-
port from or formal collaboration with governments or civil
society organizations. In contrast to outcome-driven and
hackathon-style civic hacking, Nullfull’s approach toslow
civic hackingprioritized completion and sharing over speed
and scale. To sustain engagement across lengthy project cy-
cles, members cultivated an organizational culture rooted in
personal meaning and peer learning, exchanging knowl-
edge in both formal meetings and informal settings.
Democratic practices were reinforced through equal partici-
pation in decision-making and the rotation of organizational
roles.
Alongside group organization and culture, shared rou-
tines played a crucial role in enabling civic hackers with
limited ﬁnancial resources, professional networks, or dis-
cretionary time to remain engaged in collective projects.
Members integrated project work into the rhythms of every-
day life, compensating for the limited time available during
weekly meetings by contributing incrementally during com-
mutes, evenings, or breaks. Together, these practices point
to what I call routinized data activism— a sustainable,
grassroots form of civic participation shaped by democratic
values and embedded in everyday life— which I elaborate in
the next section.
Routinized data activism: Data activism
woven into everyday life
In this section, I examine how Nullfull’s civic hacking fos-
tered a form of data activism that is woven into daily rou-
tines, which I call routinized data activism. Members
integrate technical practices with non-technical daily activ-
ities— such as community socializing, news consumption,
and everyday errands— to develop routinized citizen data
practices. I analyze how these varied practices gained mean-
ing as critical engagements with data through their coordin-
ation and repetition in everyday life, foregrounding how
civic engagement with data is sustained through ongoing
participation across multiple domains of daily activity.
Socializing as data activism: Jonmat Assembly
One of the clearest examples of blending civic engagement
with social activity is the Jonmat Assembly project (https://
nullfull.github.io/jonmat/), originally created by the civic
hacking group Codenamu in 2016. Jonmat is a pun based
on Korean slang meaning“fucking delicious,” commonly
used to praise tasty restaurant food. Using public data on
congressional expense accounts and a community mapping
tool, Jonmat Assembly visualizes popular restaurants fre-
quented by lawmakers and shows how much allowance
each lawmaker spent at each location.
Nullfull, whose founding members had ties to
Codenamu and CfS, took on a central role in maintaining
the project after these groups disbanded. Because the gov-
ernment published these receipts only as scanned PDFs,
Nullfull volunteers transcribed them into spreadsheets— a
tedious but crucial task. In 2019, they crowdsourced the
data entry, designing a public-facing interface with
step-by-step instructions (see Figure 3) that made transcrip-
tion into a shared civic practice.

News consumption as data activism: Recharted and
Yeonam News
Nullfull members extended their civic hacking into the
everyday act of reading the news, transforming routine
media consumption into opportunities for data critique,
intervention, and public accountability. Two key projects
— Recharted and Yeonam News— demonstrate how data
activism can be embedded in habitual digital media use.
Recharted is a collaborative initiative aimed at correcting
misleading charts and graphics in news coverage. When a
member encountered a poorly designed or deceptive info-
graphic, theyﬂagged it on Slack. The group then analyzed
whether the issue stemmed from statistical misrepresenta-
tion, visual exaggeration, or political bias. Believing that
many of these shoddy infographics were not honest mis-
takes but deliberate misrepresentations, members redrew
the charts with more accurate visualizations and uploaded
them to a public GitHub repository titled “Recharted”
(https://github.com/NullFull/recharted).
For example, Figure 5 shows Nullfull’s correction of a
budget forecast graphic published by the conservative outlet
Money Today, which distorted the scale to exaggerate bud-
get increases under President Moon Jae-in (2017–2022).
Nullfull’s version (on the right) stripped away the visual
bias to present a more accurate rendering, exposing the
ideological framing embedded in data design.
Recharted became a playful group activity, with mem-
bers informally competing to ﬁnd the worst “infographic
sins” and showcase their wit through creative redesigns.
Most of this work happened individually as part of daily
life rather than in group meetings. Over time, members de-
veloped a habit of critically scanning charts and graphs in
the news they encountered during commutes, lunch breaks,
or late-night scrolling— transforming casual media con-
sumption into acts of data vigilance.
Beyond correcting individual graphics, the group pursued
broader efforts to improve datastorytelling in Korean media.
For instance, Nullfull translated the Financial Times’
“Visual Vocabulary ” (http://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-
vocabulary/)— a guide to effective data visualization— and
made it publicly available for Korean journalists and media
workers. They also used social media to advocate for hiring
professional data visualization designers and adopting tools
like Angular, Observable, and Atlas to raise design standards.
In this way, members moved beyond critique and worked to
support systemic improvements in data communication.

Nullfull members also extended civic hacking into everyday
consumer routines through a project called IsNamyang
(https:/ /isnamyang.nullfull.kr/). This project targets
Namyang Dairy Products, a major Korean dairy corporation
infamous for its abusive labor practices and discriminatory
treatment of women employees, contract workers, and small
retailers. The app’s tagline,“Enjoy the Gapjil,”references the
Korean slanggapjil: bullying by those in power toward those
in weaker positions. In effect, the project encourages consu-
mers to reverse gapjil through boycott. The name
“IsNamyang” derives from the programming convention of
an “isSomething” function, which checks whether a value
meets a certain condition. Likewise, the IsNamyang app
checks whether a given productis made by Namyang.
Using the app, shoppers could simply scan a barcode to
check a product’sa fﬁliation with Namyang (see Figure 6).
This allowed users to make real-time boycott decisions dur-

members compiled a comprehensive database of barcodes
from Namyang’s many products— ranging from milk and
yogurt to snacks— and tested the beta version during their
own grocery trips. After each grocery run, they would share
any issues or successes at the next group meeting, such as
the barcode scanner’s failure to recognize a particular item’s
packaging. Theseﬁrsthand experiences fed directly into im-
proving the app’s vision accuracy and user interface design,
ensuring that IsNamyang would work smoothly in actual
shopping situations.

Interdependency and coordination of citizen data
practices
Across these examples, a clear pattern emerges: Nullfull’s
data activism is not a separate or specialized activity but
one deeply interwoven with members ’ everyday lives.
The group blurred the line between technical engagement
and ordinary practices, such as socializing over shared
meals (Jonmat Assembly), critically reading news headlines
(Recharted and Yeonam News), or scanning items while
grocery shopping (IsNamyang). Rather than treating
data-related practices as exceptional or expert-driven,
Nullfull fostered forms of citizen data engagement
embedded in social habits, informal learning, and commu-
nal routines.

civic and political engagement, and routinization and coord-
ination. Everyday routines— news reading, socializing, gro-
cery shopping— become linked to explicit data practices
such as transcription, scraping, visualization redesign, or
database construction. These, in turn, are oriented toward
speciﬁc forms of civic engagement, including monitoring
public spending, challenging media bias, or supporting la-
bor justice. Crucially, these practices are sustained through
recurring patterns of coordination.
These recurring patterns did not emerge immediately or
through a formal decision to create routines. They evolved
between April and August 2017, after Nullfull’s reorganiza-
tion, as remaining members sought to continue civic hack-
ing without the pressure of speed and scale. Recharted, one
of the group’s ﬁrst projects after reorganization, emerged
amid heightened public attention to political news during
the 2016–2017 presidential impeachment crisis, as mem-
bers shared misleading infographics encountered through
ordinary news consumption. Similarly, after Nullfull took
over Jonmat Assembly from the disbanded Codenamu,
members maintained it by distributing receipt-data updates
across idle moments. These activities became durable be-
cause small tasks could be woven into members’everyday
routines and repeatedly fed back into weekly meetings and
online coordination.
This coordination centered on the group’s lightweight
but consistent organizational rhythm. Each Monday meet-
ing opened with approximately 30 min dedicated to updates
on Recharted and Yeonam News. Members brought in a
chart or headline encountered during the week — often
something noticed during routine media consumption—
and the group collaboratively discussed possible revisions

Discussion and conclusion
This article has analyzed how data citizenship is enacted
through ordinary engagements with data amid the temporal
and labor pressures of dataﬁed societies. Nullfull’s data ac-
tivism was sustained not through formal institutional sup-
port or expert-driven production, but through habitual
participation, peer learning, and routinization— the linking
of everyday routines, data-related practices, and lightweight
forms of coordination. Through this integration, civic en-
gagement with data became embedded in the rhythms of or-
dinary life. Routinization was therefore not only a means of
sustaining participation, but also the process through which
data citizenship took shape through repeated engagements
with data-mediated public issues.
The concept of routinized data activism developed here
describes a mode of civic engagement in which data prac-
tices are integrated into everyday activities and maintained
through repetition and coordination. While existing scholar-
ship has broadened data activism to include mundane en-
gagements with data politics (Dencik, 2019; Fotopoulou,
2019; Kennedy, 2018) and shown that hackers’ techno-
logical work is intertwined with discursive and relational
practices (Kelty, 2010; Kubitschko, 2015), this study asks
how such engagements become durable— how they are sus-
tained through interdependency and recurring coordination
within routine contexts.
Across Nullfull’s projects, speciﬁc technological, media,
and social practices within members’everyday lives grad-
ually become linked and oriented toward collective
civic engagement with data. In the Yeonam News and
Recharted projects, members encountered misleading info-
graphics or sexist headlines while reading news as part of
ordinary media use. They shared screenshots and tentative
revisions on Slack, often in brief intervals between work
and other obligations. Monday meetings then functioned
as recurring sites of alignment: members reviewedﬂagged
cases, selected examples, reﬁned visualizations or head-
lines, and circulated outputs through GitHub and social
media. Similarly, barcode checks conducted during grocery
shopping fed into iterative updates of the IsNamyang
database, while data transcription carried out during com-
mutes accumulated into shared spreadsheets for Jonmat
Assembly. Occasional matjip gatherings combined social
bonding with project visibility, sustaining participation
through affective ties. In these cycles, individual attentive-
ness in everyday settings was repeatedly folded back into
collective projects.
Routinized data activism does more than show that mun-
dane practices carry political meaning; it explains how small,
situated contributions become recurring rather than episodic
as they are interdependently linked, coordinated, and or-
iented toward shared civic goals in ways that sustain partici-
pation. Papacharissi (2010) describes“new civic habits” as
repeated, embodied actions that blur the boundary between
private routines and public intervention. Nullfull’s practices
exemplify such habits: small acts— ﬂagging a headline, en-
tering receipt data, scanning a barcode— became part of on-
going interventions addressing political spending
transparency, gender bias in media, and labor injustice.
Situating Nullfull’s practices within 2010s South Korea
further shows how routinization emerged as a practical re-
sponse to constraint. Many members experienced employ-
ment instability, long working hours, and institutional
distrust through freelance or contract-based IT work. In
this context, hackathon-style intensive production, entrepre-
neurial scaling, and institutional collaborations were often
perceived as extensions of exploitative labor. Nullfull ’s
horizontal collaboration and peer learning offered a way
to secure autonomy and sustain meaningful civic participa-
tion without reproducing those pressures. This effort pro-
duced an alternative temporality of data engagement —
slow, recurring, and relational rather than oriented toward
the speed, scale, and extractive logics of contemporary
data capitalism. These practices resonate with Kitchin and
Fraser’s (2020) notion of “slow computing,
” which cri-
tiques the acceleration of digital technologies. Yet,
Nullfull’s slow temporality was grounded less in a program-
matic commitment to deceleration than in the practical need

to sustain civic agency under conditions of constrained IT
labor, institutional disillusionment, and voluntary data
work.
These ﬁndings contribute to debates on data citizenship
by emphasizing how civic agency is formed and enacted
through routinized and interdependent practice. Critical
digital scholars conceptualize digital citizenship as an on-
going process enacted through digital acts that make claims
and contest power (Isin and Ruppert, 2020; McCosker et al.,
2016). Studies of data citizenship further demonstrate that
civic capacities develop through informal and relational
processes such as networks of literacy, collaborative sense-
making, and sociotechnical imaginaries (Carmi and Yates,
2023; Gabrys et al., 2016; Lehtiniemi and Ruckenstein,
2019). These works emphasize that data citizenship does
not reside in individual competence or infrastructural access
alone, but emerges through collective capacities.
Seen in this light, data citizenship in Nullfull takes shape
through repeated and shared practices that foster attentive-
ness, responsibility, and sustained engagement with data-
mediated public issues. Weekly cycles of attention, discus-
sion, revision, and circulation not only produced corrected
infographics and updated databases but also cultivated mu-
tual accountability and a shared civic orientation. Members
enacted digital acts of critique not as isolated interventions
but as recurring contributions to collective projects. Over
time, these practices fostered a sense of belonging and col-
lective capacity, aligning with Isin and Ruppert’s (2020) ar-
gument that digital citizenship“comes into being” through
situated acts of participation. By publicizing and sharing
their projects through social media and engaging citizens
beyond the group, Nullfull members recognized them-
selves— and were increasingly recognized by others— as
actors capable of addressing politicians, media organiza-
tions, and corporations through data practices (Bae, 2024).
Lastly, this study underscores the contextual nature of
data citizenship. When data citizens are understood as pol-
itical subjects who critically engage with dataﬁcation and
act on data for social change (Isin and Ruppert, 2020;
Milan, 2019), their practices cannot be explained solely
by technological affordances or processes of dataﬁcation.
By situating Nullfull ’s routinized data activism within
South Korea’s civic tech context, this study demonstrates
that data citizenship emerges not from technical compe-
tence or data access alone, but from the interplay of every-
day practices, collective identities, and sociopolitical
conditions. Future research can examine how routinized
forms of engagement take shape across different civic
tech and labor contexts, and how they contribute to context-
speciﬁc formations of data citizenship.
