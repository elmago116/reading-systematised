1. Understanding Information Architecture (IA): 
Evolution, Deﬁnitions, and Scope. Data, 
Information, and Knowledge Architectures 
Deﬁning Information Architecture (IA) has always been a challenge, as its meaning 
has shifted with technological change and professional practice. Rather than a 
single ﬁxed concept, IA has evolved through multiple paradigms, each highlighting 
different priorities , methods, and contexts. This section traces the discipline’s 
historical development and conceptual breadth, providing a foundation for the 
principles that guide IA today. 
1.1. Evolution of Information Architecture 
The origins of IA can be traced back to the 1970s, when Richard Saul Wurman 
coined the term during his 1976 address to the American Institute of Architects. 
Wurman framed the “information architect” as someone who “organizes the 
patterns inherent in data, making the complex clear” (Wurman, 1997, as cited in 
Resmini & Rosati, 2011, p. 36; Bigby, 2023). This perspective, grounded in 
information design, focused on clarity, comprehension, and the communicative 
arrangement of data through charts, maps, and infog raphics (Anderson et al., 
2020). 
By the 1980s, IA was reinterpreted through the lens of information systems . As 
enterprises adopted complex digital infrastructures, IA became synonymous with 
structuring databases and processes to ensure efficiency and interoperability 
(Anderson et al., 2020; Resmini & Rosati, 2011). Roger and Elaine Evernden (2003) 
later deﬁne d IA as “a foundation discipline describing the theory, principles, 
guidelines, standards, conventions and factors for managing information as a 
resource” (as cited in Resmini & Rosati, 2011, p. 35). 
In the 1990s, IA was redeﬁned within the context of the World Wide Web, primarily 
through Rosenfeld and Morville’s Information Architecture for the World Wide Web  
(1998). They articulated IA as “the structural design of shared information 
environments,” emphasising usability, navigation, and ﬁndability (Rosenfeld, 
Morville, & Arango, 2015, pp. 23–24). This user -centred approach bridged 
information science, library science, and human–computer interaction, making IA 
central to web design. 
Alongside this, a Knowledge Management (KM) –based approach  emerged, 
consolidated in the 2010s through consultancies such as Earley Information 
Science and Enterprise Knowledge. Here, IA is conceived as the design of enterprise 
taxonomies, ontologies, and metadata frameworks that enable digital 

4 
 
transformation and AI readiness (Earley, 2013; Earley & Maislin, 2016; EK Team, 
2024; Earley, 2025). 
The 2010s introduced Pervasive IA, extending the ﬁeld beyond websites to cross -
channel and ubiquitous computing contexts. Resmini and Rosati (2011) argued that 
IA was not conﬁned to digital interfaces but played a role in designing “place -
making” across hybrid ecosystems. Examples include multichannel banking  and 
urban information ecologies , where digital and physical experiences intersect 
(Resmini & Rosati, 2011). 
Finally, in the 2020s, AI-ﬁrst IA  reframes the ﬁeld as the orchestration of 
information not only for humans but also for intelligent systems. Scholars such as 
Nudelman (2025) and Bock (2024) stress that invisible layers —data pipelines, 
ontologies, and algorithmic logic —now form the major ity of the user experience. 
Concepts such as Digital Twins (Nudelman, 2025) and Iceberg UX illustrate how AI-
ﬁrst IA requires designing simultaneously for human comprehension and machine 
processing (Nudelman & Kempka, 2024; Nudelman, 2025; Bock, 2024). 
• 1970s – Information Design (Wurman: clarity, visual explanation). 
• 1980s – Information Systems  (enterprise data ﬂows, resource 
management). 
• 1990s–2000s – Web IA (Rosenfeld & Morville: usability, ﬁndability). 
• 1990s origins, 2010s –present – KM-based IA  (ontologies, taxonomies, 
digital transformation). 
• 2010s–present – Pervasive IA  (cross-channel, ubiquitous computing, 
place-making). 
• 2020s–present – AI-ﬁrst IA  (dual mediation: structuring for humans and 
machines).

While deﬁnitions of IA vary, they converge on the idea of “making the complex clear” 
(Wurman, 1997; Anderson et al., 2020, p. 4). Across design, systems, knowledge 
management, and AI, the discipline revolves around structuring, categorising, and 
labelling information to support human and machine sensemaking. Anderson et al. 
(2020, p. 4) summarise: 
Alongside its sister discipline of content strategy, the work of information architecture today 
involves identifying and developing detailed content models that ensure designers can 
architect experiences persisting across any and all channels, including ones that may not 
yet exist. 
This synthesis highlights IA’s continuing evolution: from visual clarity to systemic 
interoperability, from usability to enterprise knowledge scaffolding, and from 
human-centred navigation to dual human–machine mediation. 
1.2. Foundations of our approach to the Information Architecture 
(IA) 
Information Architecture (IA) is a foundational discipline that structures and 
organizes information to enhance usability, discoverability, and user experience. 
Within contemporary practice, IA has evolved around three core paradigms: 
1. User-Centered Design (UCD) 
2. Knowledge Management and Semantic Layer 
3. AI-ﬁrst Information Architecture Framework 
These paradigms collectively deﬁne modern IA, shaping the ways information is 
presented, structured, and consumed. 
1.2.1. Paradigm 1: User-Centered Design (UCD) 
Deﬁnition: User-Centered Design (UCD) prioritizes the involvement of users in the 
design process, leveraging participatory methods such as design thinking and co -
creation to create intuitive and meaningful experiences. 
Methods, Techniques, and Tools: 
• Participatory Design Workshops 
• Co-creation Sessions 
• Focus Groups 
• Design Thinking Framework 
Practical Example and Case Study: The "HerStory&NeSyAI" project employs UCD to 
mitigate gender bias by actively engaging community groups in developing 

9 
 
information systems. This approach ensures diverse user needs are addressed 
through collaborative design practices. 
1.2.2. Paradigm 2: Knowledge Management and the Semantic Layer 
Deﬁnition: Knowledge Management within IA emphasizes structured data through 
a semantic layer, utilizing taxonomies, metadata, and ontologies to contextualize 
and link data across different systems. 
Methods, Techniques, and Tools: 
• Taxonomies and Controlled Vocabularies (e.g., SKOS) 
• Metadata Standards (e.g., Dublin Core) 
• Ontologies and Knowledge Graphs 
• Semantic Web Technologies (W3C standards) 
Practical Example and Case Study : The EK Team highlights a semantic layer 
implementation in enterprises to ensure data connectivity and contextual accuracy 
across various applications. Taxonomies and ontologies manage industry data 
consistently, enhancing information retrieval and user experience. 
1.2.3. Paradigm 3: AI-ﬁrst Information Architecture Framework 
Deﬁnition: The AI-ﬁrst IA framework integrates artiﬁcial intelligence directly into the 
IA design, enabling contextualized, personalized user experiences driven by AI 
functionalities. 
Core Components 
1. Analysis Overview 
2. Category Analysis 
3. LLM Search Results 
4. Item Detail with Contextualized Search 
5. Q&A Maintenance 
Methods, Techniques, and Tools 
• Large Language Models (LLMs) 
• Contextualized Search Interfaces 
• Dynamic Content Generation 
• AI-driven Personalization 

10 
 
Practical Example and Case Study: Nudelman and Kempka demonstrate the AI-ﬁrst 
framework in the redesign of an e -commerce experience, showcasing improved 
user interaction through dynamic, AI -driven recommendations, personalized 
summaries, and enhanced contextual search. 
1.3. Typology of Digital Spaces and Information Architecture 
Information Architecture (IA) manifests differently depending on the digital space 
where it is applied. It is not only a technical discipline but also a set of principles 
and practices that shape user experience in increasingly complex environments. 
From w ebsites to mobile applications, through intranets, social media, and 
Generative Artiﬁcial Intelligence Systems, each typology presents speciﬁc needs, 
distinctive components, and unique challenges. Examining these typologies 
provides insight into how IA m akes digital interactions accessible, usable, and 
meaningful. 
1.3.1. Websites 
Websites remain one of the most prominent spaces where IA operates. Their core 
need lies in organizing vast volumes of content into clear hierarchies that align with 
both user goals and organizational objectives (Optimal Workshop, 2022). Essential 
components include sitemaps, labeling systems, navigation structures, and search 
functionalities (AltexSoft, 2025). Major challenges involve avoiding overly complex 
structures and ensuring long-term scalability.  
Notable case studies include GOV.UK, whose minimalist architecture supports 
public services, and the BBC, which manages extensive multimedia content with 
clarity (Numiko, 2024; Stevens, 2024). 
1.3.2. Apps 
Mobile applications demand IA that prioritizes simplicity, speed, and clarity. Unlike 
websites, the limited screen space requires fewer navigation layers and direct 
interactions (Optimal Workshop, 2022). Core components include streamlined 
menus, hierarchi cal ﬂows, and mobile -speciﬁc IA patterns such as bottom 
navigation and hamburger menus (Yuchia Liu, 2022). The main challenge is 
balancing functionality and simplicity: too many options overwhelm users, but too 
few limit utility (Nayak, 2024).  
Case studies like the Charity App on Dribbble illustrate how user research ensures 
relevant pathways and sustained engagement (Anﬁsa, 2025). 
1.3.3. Intranets 
Intranets function as internal ecosystems that support organizational productivity 
and knowledge sharing. Their IA must enable rapid access to documents, 

11 
 
workﬂows, and communication tools (Optimal Workshop, 2022). Core components 
include hierarchical content structures, role -based permissions, labeling, and 
advanced search (Ward, 2025). Key challenges involve managing large volumes of 
information while pre serving simplicity and coherence across departments 
(IntranetBlog, 2025).  
Examples such as Claromentis intranets demonstrate how intuitive IA enhances 
employee engagement and efficiency (Claromentis, 2021). 
1.3.4. Social Media 
Social media platforms represent complex environments where IA structures 
massive, dynamic, and personalized interactions. According to Krishnan (2025), 
their architecture must manage feeds, social graphs, content storage, and 
notiﬁcations with scalability and reliability in mind. A case study by Moore (2014) on 
Twitter shows how structuring event backchannels through hashtags, moderation, 
and content curation fosters participation and knowledge sharing. Interface design 
also plays a critical role: Rjha (2025) highlights the imp ortance of walls, proﬁles, 
messaging, and chronological ﬁlters to organize information effectively.  
At the technical level, frameworks such as MERN (MongoDB, Express.js, React, 
Node.js) allow for building scalable social platforms where IA manifests in data 
schemas, APIs, and client interactions (GeeksforGeeks, 2025). Together, these 
examples show how IA  in social media balances scalability, real -time interaction, 
and user-centered design. 
1.3.5. Generative AI Systems 
Generative Artiﬁcial Intelligence (GAI) systems introduce new forms of IA, as they 
do not merely distribute information but also generate and transform it in real time. 
Core needs include managing semantic data, orchestrating autonomous agents, 
and suppor ting conversational and multimodal ﬂows (Li, 2025). Essential 
components comprise taxonomies distinguishing modalities and autonomy levels 
(Centelles, 2025), interoperability frameworks, and IA -ﬁrst interfaces with 
contextualized dashboards, advanced search, and knowledge maintenance 
(Nudelman & Kempka, 2024). Key challenges involve scalability, transparency, 
cognitive load, and the ethical classiﬁcation of outputs.  
Case studies include GitHub Copilot, which structures coding contexts; ChatGPT, 
Gemini, and Claude, which integrate multimodal channels; and AutoGPT, which 
tests IA’s ability to manage open -ended agentive workﬂows. Collectively, these 
illustrate how IA mu st evolve to ensure reliability, explainability, and coherence in 
AI-ﬁrst ecosystems.

1.4. Data, Information, and Knowledge Architectures 
The study of digital systems requires distinguishing between data architecture, 
information architecture, and knowledge architecture , while also recognizing 
their interdependence. 
• Data architecture  focuses on the structured storage and management of 
raw data. As Fillottrani and Keet (2020) explain, “traditional data 
management comprises a sequence from requirements to conceptual data 
model, transforming it into a relational model, and from there cre ating a 
physical database schema” (p. 488). This layer provides the foundation for 
storing and querying discrete facts. 
• Information architecture  organizes data into meaningful and usable 
structures. According to Mockplus (2024), it is “the practice of organizing, 
structuring, and labeling content effectively and sustainably, so users can 
ﬁnd information and complete tasks efficiently” (para. 5). Kuroki and 
Gottschalg-Duque (2024) further extend this view by deﬁning a multimodal 
information architecture  as “a multimodal view of organizing data” that 
supports transformation into actionable resources for AI applications (p. 3). 
• Knowledge architecture  integrates information with organizational goals 
and reasoning processes. Bedford (2020) emphasizes that “our knowledge 
architecture needs to support a much broader set of capabilities… design 
basics and conceptual models will persist” (p. 45). This enables systems not 
only to store or structure information, but also to support learning, 
prediction, and decision-making. 
Together, these architectures form a continuum. Shao et al. (2019) summarize: 
“data are obtained through observation… information is data contextualized for 
analysis… knowledge is a general understanding and experience from accumulated 
information” (p. 216). 
Architectures that combine these layers allow for adaptive, intelligent systems, 
such as recommendation services that transform user data into contextual 
information and ultimately knowledge -driven personalization (Shao, Duan, Zhou, 
Zou, & Gao, 2018).

posterior no signiﬁcaria sinó una delimitación artiﬁcial, simplista, de la realidad en 
los contextos de la aplicación. Los siguientes casos reales, analizados en la

(Absent in Brown) 
These are principles that  Brown did not articulate in 2010, but which later authors 
argue are essential for contemporary practice. 
1. Ethics  
• Principle: Information systems are never neutral; their structures shape 
power, transparency, and equity. 
• Example: Lacerda & Lima -Marques (2015) discuss smart -city 
infrastructures, where data from sensors, services, and citizens is 
collected and structured. If IA makes that data open, interoperable, and 
understandable, it supports civic transparency and democratic 

24 
 
participation. If poorly designed, it risks exclusion or manipulation (pp. 
162–164). 
• Key distinction: Ethics focuses on the moral and societal consequences  
of IA choices —whether they promote fairness, accountability, and 
inclusivity. 
2. Embodiment 
• Principle: In XR and hybrid environments, users do not just “see” 
information—they inhabit it with their bodies. IA must design places, paths, 
and affordances that are spatially legible. 
• Example: Resmini et al. (2023) highlight XR heuristics  where embodiment 
ensures that spatial layouts, continuity, and affordances align with how 
humans perceive and move in space. For instance, consistent scaling and 
persistence of objects across sessions support a sense of presence (pp. 
507–509). 
• Key distinction: Embodiment is about the physical, spatial experience of 
information—how the body itself becomes the interface. 
3. Agency 
• Principle: Users should be able to act meaningfully and autonomously  
within information environments, even when systems or devices also act. 
• Example: In IoT ecosystems, “ objects acquire agency ” (Lacerda & Lima -
Marques, 2015, p. 160). A thermostat may automatically adjust 
temperature, but the IA must ensure that humans retain control —through 
clear dashboards, override options, and transparent ﬂows of information. 
Similarly, in XR heuristics, Resmini et al. (2023) stress that users must be 
able to take consistent, consequential actions that matter to their goals. 
• Key distinction: Agency focuses on who has the power to act and ensuring 
that IA empowers human choice  rather than letting automated or opaque 
systems dominate. 
4. Pervasive Responsibility 
• Principle: Because information now permeates every device, object, and 
space, IA carries a structural responsibility for reliability, safety, and trust. 
• Example: Lacerda & Lima -Marques (2015) describe how pervasive 
healthcare systems (such as wearable monitors) rely on constant data 
ﬂows. If the IA of alerts, dashboards, or interoperability fails, it may lead to 
delayed responses or errors with real health consequences (pp. 161 –162). 

25 
 
Thus, responsibility is not optional but built into IA wherever information 
pervades daily life. 
• Key distinction: Pervasive responsibility emphasizes the inescapable duty 
of IA in contexts where failures directly impact safety and social well-being. 
5. Accessibility and Inclusivity 
• Principle: Design information architectures so all users—including people 
with disabilities, different languages/cultures, and varied levels of 
expertise—can ﬁnd, understand, and act on information with equal 
effectiveness. In IA terms, that means clear labeling and navigation , 
redundant/alternative pathways, semantic structure, and compatibility 
with assistive technologies (e.g., screen readers, keyboard navigation). 
This aligns with current IA/UX trends emphasizing inclusive patterns and 
content structur e (Tsentserensky, 2025), as well as cognitive -load aware 
organization (Arango, 2024) and evaluative focus on labeling/navigation 
systems (Głowacka, 2021). 
• Example: A university digital library redesign prioritizes inclusive 
ﬁndability. The IA team: 
• refactors organizational and labeling systems to reduce ambiguity 
across multilingual collections (Głowacka, 2021); 
• standardizes heading hierarchies and landmarks  so screen readers 
convey document structure; 
• provides keyboard-ﬁrst navigation and skip links; 
• offers faceted search with easily understood ﬁlters and example-driven 
category cues (Brown, 2010: “Exemplars”); 
• writes plain-language labels and adds progressive disclosure to avoid 
cognitive overload (Arango, 2024).  
Result: users with screen readers can traverse pages predictably; novice 
researchers rely on exemplars and facets to orient; multilingual users 
beneﬁt from consistent, comprehensible labels. 
• Key Distinction: While Ethics concerns the moral and societal stakes of 
structuring information and Pervasive Responsibility  emphasizes 
designers’ duty in ubiquitous systems , Accessibility and Inclusivity  
focuses on operationalizing equity in the IA itself —how navigation, 
labeling, and structure explicitly remove barriers  to perception, 
comprehension, and action. It overlaps ethically (fair access) and with 
responsibility (systemic duty), but remains distinct  by centering practical, 
testable IA decisions (e.g., semantic markup, alternative paths, consistent 

26 
 
patterns, assistive-tech compatibility). In hybrid/XR contexts, this extends to 
spatial legibility and consistent placemaking so orientation and action are 
inclusive for diverse bodies and abilities (Resmini et al., 2023); in pervasive 
ecosystems, it implies interoperable ﬂows that do not exclude users at 
device or platform boundaries (Lacerda & Lima-Marques, 2015). 
Key questions for each of the additional IA principles. 
• Ethics = Are the values of fairness and transparency built into the structure? 
• Embodiment = Does the body experience information as place? 
• Agency = Do people remain in control and able to act meaningfully? 
• Pervasive Responsibility =  Is the responsibility for safety and reliability 
recognized in systems that surround everyday life? 
• Accessibility and Inclusivity = Is the IA structured so everyone—including

3. Objectives of Information Architecture: Design for 
Finding, Understanding, and Utility 
Principles provide the normative compass for Information Architecture (IA), but 
practice is directed toward concrete objectives. IA aims to make information 
environments navigable, meaningful, and valuable to users and organisations. 
Scholars consistently frame these goals in terms of ﬁndability, understandability, 
and utility  (Arango, 2022; Rosenfeld, Morville, & Arango, 2015). This section 
explores each objective in turn, with examples, evaluation methods, and sector -
speciﬁc adaptations. 
3.1 Findability 
Findability is the capacity of a system to ensure that users can efficiently and 
effectively locate the information they seek. Arango (2022) deﬁnes it succinctly as 
“making stuff easier to ﬁnd” (p. 2). 
Key Concepts: 

27 
 
• Users engage in diverse information-seeking behaviours: known-item 
seeking, exploratory seeking, exhaustive research, and reﬁnding (Rosenfeld 
et al., 2015, pp. 43–45). 
• A common mistake is using an overly  simple model that treats information 
seeking as a straight “question → answer” pipeline. That view ignores 
ambiguity and wastes effort. (Rosenfeld et al., 2015, pp. 40 –43). Real-world 
search looks more like berrypicking and pearl-growing: people move back 
and forth between searching and browsing, and they branch out from useful 
examples (“more like this”). Systems should support easy pivots and growth 
from good exemplars. (Rosenfeld et al., 2015, pp. 46 –48). Bates’s (1989) 
berrypicking model shows search is iterative and adaptive: users reﬁne and 
change their queries as they learn. 
• Common obstacles include ambiguous terminology and over -reliance on 
search engines without structural clarity (Good, 2024a, 2024b). 
3.2 Understandability 
Understandability refers to the user’s ability to make sense of information once it 
has been located. Arango (2022, p. 3) frames it as “making sense of stuff,” while 
Rosenfeld et al. (2015, p. 53) highlight IA as a placemaking activity that shapes 
meaning through context. 
Key Concepts: 
• Designers use typologies and organizing principles (consistent navigation 
terms, semantic groupings) so environments are legible and learnable (pp. 
63–66). Rosenfeld_et_al_2015_Chapter4 
• Structural order, rhythm, typologies, and modularity provide legibility 
(Rosenfeld et al., 2015, pp. 55–68). 
• Mismatched mental models, broken typologies, or unclear labels reduce 
clarity. 
3.3 Utility 
Utility describes the degree to which information, once found and understood, 
supports purposeful action. It connects IA to broader strategic aims. Arango (2022) 
deﬁnes utility as the “value created by information once it is found and understood” 
(p. 4). 
Key Concepts: 
• Utility is a strategic lens that connects information structures to purpose —
what is this good for? —and aligns with choice -making frameworks (e.g.,

outcomes (Arango, 2022, n.p.). 
• Risks include overemphasis on micro -usability, neglect of strategy, and

5. Relationships between Information Architecture 
(IA) and User Experience (UX) Design 
Information Architecture (IA) and User Experience (UX) Design are often mentioned 
together, yet their relationship remains debated. While IA focuses on structuring 
information environments, UX addresses the broader quality of user interactions 
with systems  and services. This section synthesises academic and practitioner 
perspectives, outlining different positions on how IA and UX intersect, complement, 
and depend upon one another. 

32 
 
5.1. IA as the Backbone of UX 
One of the most common formulations portrays IA as the structural backbone of 
UX. Without coherent navigation, clear labelling, and logical hierarchies, user 
experiences cannot be intuitive or efficient. Larson (2025) describes IA as “the 
backbone of UX” that makes systems usable and scalable. Similarly, Adam Fard 
(2025) emphasi ses IA as “key to making intuitive, effective user experiences,” 
typically preceding wireframing in the design process. 
Empirical studies support this claim: well -structured IA demonstrably reduces 
search effort, increases task completion, and improves user satisfaction (Stefanuk, 
2020). As Slickplan (2025) puts it, “IA provides the structure, while UX focuses on 
how users feel.” 
5.2. IA Inside UX 
A second position treats IA as a phase or sub-discipline within UX design. Here, IA 
is situated between research and prototyping, when content is inventoried, 
grouped, labelled, and structured according to user needs. The UOC Design Toolkit 
(2021) explicitly places IA within UX workﬂows, emphasising card sorti ng and tree 
testing as validation methods. 
Fuzzy Math (Baldino, 2019), BrowserStack (2025), and Divami (2021) all frame IA as 
a technical stratum of UX practice, providing the foundation for navigation planning 
and content ﬂows. This view underscores IA’s embeddedness in UX methodology 
but risks downplaying its independence as a discipline. 
5.3. IA and UX as Distinct but Interdependent 
A third stance recognises IA and UX as separate but mutually dependent  
disciplines. IA specialises in structure and ﬁndability, while UX encompasses the 
broader experiential spectrum —research, interaction design, usability, and 
evaluation. Topcoder (2023) contrasts the two by deliverables: taxonomies and 
sitemaps belong to IA, while prototypes and usability testing belong to UX. Yet, both 
must work hand-in-hand for coherent products. 
Zion & Zion (2015) and Benz (2021) argue along similar lines, stressing that UX 
cannot function without IA, but IA may extend beyond UX contexts. This balanced 
perspective is increasingly inﬂuential in both academic teaching and professional 
discourse. 
5.4. IA Beyond UX 
Some authors argue that IA’s remit extends beyond UX , encompassing the 
structuring of information in both physical and digital spaces. Margolles (2018), 
drawing on the Information Architecture Institute’s deﬁnition, highlights IA as 

33 
 
relevant “in the real world as well as online.” This perspective situates IA within a 
larger cross -context practice, overlapping with knowledge management, service 
design, and even urban planning. 
GeeksforGeeks (2021) likewise distinguishes IA from UX by roles and concerns: IA 
is about structuring information resources, while UX ensures usability and 
satisfaction. 
5.5. IA for Emergent UX (AI, Zero-UI, AR) 
As interfaces evolve toward voice, gesture, and spatial interactions, IA’s role 
becomes even more critical. Structured content, taxonomies, and ontologies 
enable AI-powered experiences, conversational agents, and augmented reality (AR) 
environments. Slickplan (2025) stresses that IA must evolve for Zero -UI and AI -
mediated systems, while Adam Fard (2025) highlights that without structured IA, 
adaptive interfaces cannot function. 
Andersen (2025) goes further, suggesting that as AI transforms interface work, IA 
gains renewed prominence as the enabler of semantics and data-driven 
experiences. This position anticipates a closer integration between IA and AI —
explored in detail in Section 6. 
5.6. Integrated Assessment of UX and IA 
Recent scholarship also suggests embedding IA evaluation explicitly within UX 
assessment. Santos, Lima, and Conte (2025) propose heuristics that integrate IA 
into Garrett’s “planes of UX,” ensuring that structural issues (navigation, labelling, 
categorisation) are tested alongside broader experiential criteria. This approach 
offers a more holistic method for design evaluation. 
Rather than mutually exclusive, these positions highlight IA’s versatility. Depending 
on context, IA may be a subset of UX practice, a parallel discipline, or the 
infrastructural enabler of new interaction paradigms. 
As UX itself evolves in response to artiﬁcial intelligence, so too does IA. The next 
section explores the deep interdependencies between IA and AI, showing why 
“there is no AI without IA” and how structured information enables intelligent 
systems. 
6. Relationships between Information Architecture 
(IA) and Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) 
Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly integrated into digital products and 
services, reshaping how users interact with information. Yet, AI cannot function 

34 
 
without structured, well -governed information environments. As Earley (2025) 
argues, “there is no AI without IA” . This section examines how IA underpins AI 
systems, the processes by which the two disciplines intersect, and the practical 
applications that result. 
6.1. Why AI Needs IA 
AI systems depend on IA’s foundational structures —taxonomies, controlled 
vocabularies, ontologies, and knowledge graphs. These provide the semantic 
scaffolding that enables algorithms to interpret intent, infer relationships, and 
deliver context-appropriate results (Rice & Resmini, 2021). Without structured IA, 
machine learning models operate on uncontextualised data, leading to poor 
accuracy, bias, or meaningless outputs. 
6.2. The End-to-End Process of Applying AI in IA 
Drawing from recent frameworks (Earley, 2025; Rice & Resmini, 2021), a six -step 
process illustrates how IA supports AI: 
1. Deﬁne business problems and use cases – Identify signals such as 
queries, behaviours, or sensor data, and set measurable outcomes. 
2. Inventory and prepare data  – Locate sources, clean them, and map them 
to IA structures. Even unstructured data requires metadata and 
normalisation. 
3. Model the knowledge – Build vocabularies, taxonomies, and ontologies that 
deﬁne equivalence, hierarchy, and association. 
4. Implement AI techniques – Apply entity extraction, classiﬁcation, inference 
engines, or machine learning aligned with IA models. 
5. Deliver experiences  – Use IA -aware AI to power personalisation, 
recommendations, and assistants across channels. 
6. Govern and improve  – Establish governance, metrics, and ethical 
safeguards, iterating through curation and feedback loops.

7. Conclusion of the Chapter 1 
Information Architecture has evolved from Wurman’s early vision of information 
design to a discipline central to digital, cross -channel, and AI -mediated 
environments. Its principles provide designers with a shared foundation, while its 
objectives—ﬁndability, understandability, and utility —offer clear goals. Achieving 
these requires systematic processes embedded in user -centred design, close 
integration with UX, and robust structures that underpin AI. Together, these 
perspectives establish IA as a critical discipline for designing meaningful, usable, 
and sustainable information environments.
