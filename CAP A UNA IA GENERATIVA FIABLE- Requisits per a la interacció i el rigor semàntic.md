0. Introduction
The irruption of generative artificial intelligence into everyday life has brought to light some limitations in the way these systems interact with people. Phenomena such as algorithmic biases, hallucinations and poorly contextualised responses compromise the reliability of the results and hinder critical decision-making. Studies based on the analysis of real users confirm that misinformation and an insufficient organisation of information are predominant problems that negatively affect the user experience (Alabduljabbar, 2024). These shortcomings can reduce understanding, erode trust and increase the cognitive load required to interact with the systems.

In the face of this scenario, disciplines such as information architecture, user-centered design and user experience become essential for guiding the improvement of human-system interaction. These approaches make it possible to mitigate cognitive overload and ensure that systems adapt to people's capabilities and not the other way round (Omar et al., 2025; Pastrakis et al., 2026). Even so, the specialised literature points out that, despite the theoretical focus on UCD, many generative AI systems continue to prioritise anthropomorphism and the simulation of social patterns over the real informational and cognitive needs of users (Voronin and Rafikova, 2026). This trend highlights the distance between user expectations and the behaviour of the technology, especially in the face of the so-called articulation barrier, where users have difficulty communicating precise instructions in writing (Vacanti et al., 2024). A transition is therefore needed towards models that foster transparency, naturalness and informational coherence, without deceiving about the non-human nature of the system (Silva & Canedo, 2024).

The main objective of this work is to demonstrate how the principles of information architecture, user-centered design and user experience can improve interaction with generative AI systems, and it applies this perspective to the HerStory-NeSyAI case, an initiative that combats data fragmentation and the historical invisibility of women by means of a neuro-symbolic architecture based on knowledge graphs and language models (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025).

Drawing on the literature review, the work formulates two complementary requirement schemes: one to guide the design of responsible generative AI technologies, integrating principles of UCD, UX and information architecture; and another focused on knowledge graphs, to guarantee accurate representation, semantic coherence and interoperability.

The study is structured in three parts: two scoping reviews that

map the state of research in generative AI and in knowledge graphs; a conceptual synthesis that defines the fundamental requirements of user-centered design; and an applied analysis of the HerStory-NeSyAI case aimed at identifying knowledge representation needs and proposing improvements to its information architecture.

2. Methodology
This work adopts a systematised literature review, an approach that, as Codina (2022) argues, brings rigour and transparency to the bibliographic review process. Unlike narrative reviews, systematised reviews follow an explicit protocol that makes it possible to justify the search, selection and exclusion decisions and ensures that the final corpus is representative and consistent with the research questions.

To structure this process, the SALSA method (Search, Appraisal, Synthesis, Analysis) has been used, which organises the review into four phases: a planned search, an appraisal of the documents according to defined criteria, a structured synthesis of the information and a final analysis that integrates the results.

This methodological framework ensures that the review is systematic, complete and reproducible, and provides a solid basis for identifying requirements applicable to generative artificial intelligence systems and to knowledge graphs.

3. Theoretical framework
3.1. Relevant concepts:
This section mentions some of the fundamental concepts that structure the theoretical framework of the work and that are necessary to understand some of the dynamics involved in the design of generative artificial intelligence systems. Concepts such as cognitive load, hallucinations, or anthropocentrism serve as a basis for interpreting the problems identified in the literature and for deriving the requirements presented in other blocks.

Cognitive Load Theory is defined as the amount of mental effort a user needs to process information or solve a task in a digital environment (Pastrakis et al., 2026). This theory distinguishes between intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load, and is central to the design of systems that seek to reduce information overload. Pastrakis et al. (2026) position it as a determining factor in cultural heritage applications, proposing a modular architecture that minimizes information density. Omar et al. (2025) link cognitive load to accessibility, warning that technical complexity can exceed the processing capabilities of some users. Other authors, such as Shin et al. (2025), highlight that interaction modes must adapt to individual differences in cognitive processing, while Vacanti et al. (2024) relate it to the need to reduce short-term memory load. Overall, the literature agrees that cognitive load is a key element to ensure usability and quality of interaction.

Modular Information Architecture organizes content into independent, coherent, and recombinable units, facilitating navigation and reducing cognitive load. Pastrakis et al. (2026) define it as a structure based on "discrete and recombinable units governed by consistent rules," where each module is meaningful in itself and allows progressive disclosure of content. Kuroki Júnior et al. (2024) expand on this idea by describing MIA as the construction of "architectonic worlds" based on relational models grouped by spacetime contexts. In an earlier work, the same authors operationalize MIA in five steps to prepare information spaces intended for training neural networks, using modal logic to manage diverse semantics. This modular approach is presented as a flexible alternative to traditional hierarchies, especially useful in dense or multimodal environments.

16

Explainable artificial intelligence (explainable AI or XAI) is defined as the ability of AI systems to generate understandable explanations about their functioning and their decisions. Pastrakis et al. (2026) integrate it as a necessary layer in modular architectures to guarantee interpretable recommendations in cultural settings. Yeo et al. (2026) analyze the effectiveness of textual explanations generated by LLMs, showing that they can improve transparency and user satisfaction. Yamamoto (2024) emphasizes the importance of XAI in critical decisions, while Virvou (2023) links it to the need to address biases and discrimination. In the field of hybrid AI, Maree (2025) and Centelles and Ferran-Ferrer (2025) use neuro-symbolic architectures to generate relational explanations grounded in structured data. Overall, XAI is presented as an essential requirement for trust and error mitigation in generative systems.

Keshavjee (2026) defines the knowledge graph as an architecture capable of modeling complex states and relations with longitudinal reasoning capability. Li and Li (2026) use them to map emotional states and behaviors in educational and psychological settings, while Spillo et al. (2024) implement tripartite KGs to encode explicit knowledge and logical rules in recommendation systems. Zhao et al. (2026) describe KGs as a semantic layer that connects data silos to enable "cognitive manufacturing." In the field of hybrid AI, Maree (2025) and Vidal et al. (2025) highlight their capacity to integrate heterogeneous data and improve transparency through symbolic reasoning. Centelles and Ferran-Ferrer (2025) use them as a kind of "anchor of truth" in RAG processes2, reducing hallucinations and guaranteeing the provenance of information.

The concept of hallucination refers to the phenomenon in which language models generate plausible but factually incorrect responses or responses not based on real data (Yamamoto, 2024). In usability contexts, users report this phenomenon as one of the main challenges of generative tools (Alabduljabbar, 2024). Herrera et al.

(2026) describe the "PaC Hallucination Crisis," in which AI can generate syntactically correct but logically insecure code. To mitigate this risk, Centelles and Ferran-Ferrer (2025) propose the use of knowledge graphs that validate and constrain the output of the generative model.

2 Retrieval-Augmented Generation, an architecture that integrates a large language model (LLM) with an external knowledge base to structure text generation around retrieved evidence, instead of relying exclusively on the model's parametric memory (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025) 17

Automation bias is defined as the human tendency to rely excessively on the recommendations of an automated system, to the point of ignoring contradictory information or failing to verify additional data (Yamamoto, 2024). This blind trust can lead users to accept false information generated by AI, fostering disinformation and the loss of critical thinking and basic skills. Buçinca et al. (2021), cited by Yamamoto (2024), propose cognitive forcing functions as a strategy to counter this bias, forcing the user to reflect before accepting an automated response.

Anthropomorphism is defined as the attribution of human characteristics, behaviours, or mental states to non-human agents (Truong & Chen, 2025). Voronin and Rafikova (2026) observe that, as generative AI imitates human conversational patterns, engagement and the sense of intimacy with the system increase. The study conducted by Truong and Chen (2025) indicates that this perception reduces psychological distance and fosters favourable attitudes toward the artificial agent. Silva and Canedo (2022) link anthropomorphism to "naturalness" in conversational design, while warning that it may confuse the user about the bot's non-human identity. On the other hand, Wang et al. (2023), cited by Voronin and Rafikova (2026), show that anthropomorphism increases perceived enjoyment and actual use of systems.

Neuro-symbolic AI combines the neural processing of deep learning with symbolic reasoning based on logic and symbol manipulation (Zhao et al., 2026; Maree, 2025). This approach seeks to overcome the limitations of purely neural models, especially in tasks requiring logical coherence or explainability. Zhao et al. (2026) present NSAI as a way to integrate symbolic representations into industrial and cognitive manufacturing processes, while Maree (2025) demonstrates that neuro-symbolic architectures enable the generation of relational explanations grounded in structured data.

The articulation barrier describes the difficulty users, especially non-experts, face when trying to communicate their intentions or instructions to generative artificial intelligence precisely and effectively through prompts. Vacanti et al. (2024) identify this barrier as a critical usability dilemma in text-to-image models, where the user must be able to articulate abstract concepts and technical vocabulary in order to avoid results that do not meet their expectations. For his part, Alabduljabbar (2024) situates this concept as a fundamental challenge in interaction with GenAI applications, highlighting that writing complex requests is one of the friction points reported by users in real-world environments. In conclusion, this obstacle is central to the shift toward an intention-based interface paradigm, where users no longer tell the computer "how" to perform a task, but rather "what" result they desire, which demands a high degree of written precision.

3.2. Problems identified in the literature

The literature review reveals a set of recurrent problems that transversally affect generative environments. Although they appear in diverse domains, they converge on the same diagnosis: current artificial intelligence is often not aligned with human cognitive, emotional, and social capacities, generating confusion, disorientation, mistrust, and dependence.

A first set of problems relates to inadequate presentation of information and cognitive load, which can hinder the formation of mental models and overwhelm the user. In heritage environments, narrative density and rigid hierarchies can cause disorientation when information is not presented progressively or in segments (Pastrakis et al., 2026). In mobile applications, the lack of clear hierarchies, visual inconsistency, and difficulty locating functionalities constitute common usability obstacles (Weichbroth, 2025). In multimodal systems, the combination of text, image, audio, and video can generate opaque structures, especially when modelling overly granular cases or extreme configurations that provide no real value (Kuroki Júnior et al., 2023). In image generation platforms, the need to remember parameters across screens increases memory load and hinders interaction (Vacanti et al., 2024). This implies that in socio-cultural domains where contextualisation of information is essential for constructing meaning, inadequate presentation can overwhelm the user and ultimately obstruct effective access to knowledge.

A second set of problems revolves around transparency and trust. Many generative AI systems function as "black boxes," making it difficult for users to understand why the system makes a recommendation or generates a particular result. The lack of clear explanations can erode trust and generate uncritical dependence or extreme scepticism (Yeo et al., 2026; Shin et al., 2025). In generative applications, users report problems with reliability, unexpected errors, and inconsistencies that hinder trust in the system (Alabduljabbar, 2024). Moreover, AI often does not communicate its uncertainty, which can lead users to interpret probabilistic results as "absolute truths" (Virvou, 2023).

The literature also identifies problems related to the emotional and relational dimension of interaction with AI. The use of overly naturalised conversational styles, emotionally charged responses, or excessive anthropomorphism can generate false expectations, confusion, or emotional dependence (Truong & Chen, 2025; Yang & Shen, 2025). Social presence can improve satisfaction, but it can also lead users to attribute intentions or competencies that the AI does not possess (Truong & Chen, 2025). In sensitive contexts, the lack of genuine emotional intelligence can lead to inappropriate responses (Li & Li, 2026).

Finally, several studies warn about biases, errors, and ethical limitations. AI can reproduce or amplify biases present in training data, generating unfair or discriminatory results (Omar et al., 2024; Virvou, 2023). In recommendation systems, this can lead to filter bubbles and reinforcement of confirmation bias (Shin et al., 2025). In generative models, hallucinations and disinformation compromise the reliability of the system (Yamamoto, 2024; Kuroki Junior et al., 2023). In sensitive settings, such as healthcare or emotional support, these problems can have serious consequences, which is why various authors call for protocols of supervision, transparency, and ethical review (Li & Li, 2026).

Overall, the literature shows that current AI has significant limitations in terms of information presentation, transparency, trust, emotional management, ethics, and cognitive autonomy. These problems justify the need for models and frameworks that guide the design of more responsible and user-centred generative systems.

3.3. Methods and solutions proposed in the literature

In response to the identified problems, the literature proposes a set of methods and frameworks that seek to improve the understanding, trust, and effectiveness of artificial intelligence systems. Despite the diversity of approaches, a common pattern emerges: the need to integrate user-centred design principles, coherent information structures, and transparency mechanisms.

Several studies propose conversational and cognitive strategies that foster active participation and critical thinking. The suggestive ending shows that chatbots can stimulate curiosity and reflection when they avoid closed-ended responses and offer clues that invite exploration (Yamamoto, 2024). In educational settings, nudges designed to unlock ideation processes allow AI to act as a facilitator without replacing the student's reasoning (Chia et al., 2025).

The literature also proposes solutions to improve transparency and explainability, especially in recommendation systems and generative models. Studies show that trust depends on the level of detail and clarity of explanations, more than on the type of algorithm used (Yeo et al., 2026). Explanations can be intrinsic, when the model is interpretable by design, or post-hoc, when added after the decision, but in all cases they must be understandable, contextualised, and useful to the user (Yeo et al., 2026; Shin et al., 2025). This need connects with the principles of Information Science, which proposes semantic structures and ontologies to give coherence to knowledge (Kuroki Júnior et al., 2024).

Finally, in the area of knowledge representation, several studies propose knowledge graphs to manage complex and multidimensional information. In clinical contexts, graphs allow modelling relationships, states, and trajectories in a flexible manner, facilitating knowledge continuity and intuitive navigation (Keshavjee, 2026). This approach is transferable to generative systems that need to represent entities, relationships, and contexts coherently, and also improves transparency and safety, since it allows tracing the origin of information and applying shared semantic rules (Keshavjee, 2026).

3.4. Models

Among the set of articles analysed, three models stand out for their ability to address recurrent problems in the design of generative AI systems. These are the following articles:

1. AI-enhanced modular information architecture for cultural heritage: designing cognitive-efficient and user-centered experiences by Pastrakis et al., 2026;
2. AI for all: bridging accessibility and usability through user-centered ai design by Omar et al., 2024;
3. Artificial intelligence and user experience in reciprocity: contributions and state of the art by Virvou, 2023.

These models do not replace the rest of the articles, but they offer a clear synthesis of the principles that guide the design of responsible generative systems. 21

3.4.1. AI-enhanced modular information architecture for cultural heritage: designing cognitive-efficient and user-centered experiences (Pastrakis et al., 2026)

Pastrakis et al. (2026) propose a modular architecture aimed at reducing cognitive load in dense and complex environments. The

model argues that information must be organized into discrete, interpretable semantic units connected by metadata, so that each module makes sense on its own and facilitates non-linear navigation. The proposal integrates three pillars: cognitive load theory, mental models, and semantic modularity. These principles are embodied in strategies such as progressive disclosure, which allows information to be revealed in layers and avoids information overload, and in the need to maintain a clear information scent that guides the user through consistent informative cues. The model is especially relevant in generative systems that operate on fragmented, heterogeneous, or sensitive information, since modularity facilitates interpretability, traceability, and integration with hybrid approaches such as Neuro-Symbolic AI, which combines neuronal robustness with symbolic explainability.

3.4.2. AI for all: bridging accessibility and usability through user-centered AI design (Omar et al., 2025)

Omar et al. (2025) present a framework aimed at ensuring that AI is accessible, usable, and ethical for a diversity of users. The model articulates four pillars: User-Centered Design, Inclusive Data Practices, Adaptive Interaction, and Regulatory and Ethical Alignment. These pillars establish guidelines to ensure that AI is understandable, inclusive, and aligned with ethical principles. The model highlights the importance of multimodality, interpretability, consistency, and controlled personalization, and stresses that accessibility cannot be a final requirement but rather a cross-cutting principle that must inform all phases of development. This framework is especially useful for generative systems operating in sensitive environments or with diverse groups, as it provides clear criteria for avoiding biases, improving transparency, and ensuring that interaction is inclusive and safe.

22

3.4.3. Artificial intelligence and user experience in reciprocity: contributions and state of the art (Virvou, 2023)

Virvou (2023) in her model conceptualizes the relationship between AI and user experience as a bidirectional process. According to this model, artificial intelligence can improve experience through mechanisms of adaptation, personalization, and prediction, while UX provides the necessary principles for AI to be understandable, reliable, and emotionally appropriate. The model highlights factors such as balanced social presence, moderate empathy, communicative coherence, uncertainty quantification, and short- and long-term contextual memory as key elements for a good user experience. These elements are essential for ensuring that interaction with generative systems is meaningful, respectful, and aligned with users' cognitive and emotional expectations. The model reinforces the idea that artificial intelligence cannot be designed merely as a functional technology, but as a communicative agent that must adequately manage trust, proximity, and responsibility.

In summary, although they come from different disciplines and approaches, the three models analyzed converge in the same conceptual direction. They all underscore the importance of modularity as a mechanism for managing complex information and facilitating understanding; they advocate for accessibility and inclusion as structural design requirements; and they place reciprocity between UX and AI as the basis for ensuring reliable, respectful, and emotionally appropriate interactions. Likewise, they coincide on the need for explainability and transparency, the value of maintaining semantic coherence that ensures knowledge traceability, and the relevance of adequately managing cognitive load to avoid overload or disorientation. Finally, all three models emphasize sensitivity to human needs and limitations, recognizing that generative AI must adapt to the cognitive, emotional, and contextual capacities of users.

4. List of requirements

Based on the conceptual synthesis and the interpretive analysis of the results of Objective 1, a set of requirements is identified that must guide the development of any artificial intelligence system so that it is understandable, reliable, and aligned with user needs. These requirements derive directly from the problems and solutions identified in the literature and in the reviewed models. The result is a structured framework that integrates principles of information architecture, user experience, and human-centered design, and establishes criteria for building responsible, interpretable, and cognitively sustainable generative systems.

4.1. Information architecture and user experience

1. Modular content structure: Organization into discrete, recombinable semantic units that facilitate non-linear navigation (Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Cognitive overload, difficulty forming mental models, rigid navigation.
2. Semantic coherence and explicit relations: Consistent metadata that makes relationships visible and avoids ambiguities (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Semantic ambiguity, inconsistencies, hallucinations.
3. Diverse navigation pathways: Exploration by topics, periods, roles, spaces, or trajectories, respecting mental models (Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Dependence on a single query path, difficulty exploring complex narratives.
4. Clear information scent: Visible, consistent categories, panels, or maps to guide searching (Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Disorientation, abandonment, difficulty anticipating content.
5. Progressive content presentation: Layered disclosure to avoid information overload and reduce cognitive load (Omar et al., 2025; Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Information overload, divided attention, cognitive load.
6. Traceability and provenance: Generative responses with references to verified sources or semantic structures (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Vidal et al., 2025). Problem it solves: Hallucinations, lack of verification, system opacity.
7. Integrated multimodality: Coherent combination of text, image, audio, and video integrated into unified interface units to avoid the split-attention effect (Omar et al., 2025; Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Divided attention, inconsistency between modalities, perceptual confusion.
8. Modal logic-based labeling: Reduction of ambiguity and improvement of model coherence (Kuroki Júnior & Gottschalg-Duque, 2023, 2024). Problem it solves: Semantic ambiguity, incoherence in the interpretation of relations.

4.2. Accessibility, Usability, and User-Centered Design

1. Inclusivity in data representation: Data and ontologies that actively reflect and make visible the diversity of profiles, contexts, and genders (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Omar et al., 2025). Problem it solves: Invisibilization, gender biases, lack of representativity.
2. Controlled personalization: Useful but non-intrusive adaptation to the user's needs, always guaranteeing their control and autonomy. (Omar et al., 2025) Problem it solves: Emotional dependence, loss of autonomy, over-adaptation.
3. Error recovery mechanisms: Possibility of detecting and correcting queries in an adaptive way and reorienting human-machine interaction (Omar et al., 2025; Virvou, 2023). Problem it solves: Frustration, query errors, loss of trust.
4. Communicative transparency: Clearly expressed communication of the limitations, operating criteria, and possible uncertainties of intelligent systems (Silva & Canedo, 2024; Vidal et al., 2025). Problem it solves: Opacity, misleading perception of capabilities, distrust.
5. Consistent interaction patterns: Stable, predictable, and unified communicative style throughout the entire interface to create usage habits (Omar et al., 2025; Silva & Canedo, 2024). Problem it solves: Confusion, inconsistency, difficulty learning the interface.
6. Accessible multimodality: Complementary modalities (such as audio, text, or haptic systems) easily usable to support different sensory capacities (Omar et al., 2025). Problem it solves: Sensory barriers, exclusion of users with functional diversity.

4.3. Reciprocal interaction between AI and users

1. Explicit uncertainty management: Transparent indication of the degree of confidence and representation of doubts or ambiguities in generated responses (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Virvou, 2023). Problem it solves: Uncritical trust, misinterpretation of responses, risk of hallucinations.
2. Balanced social presence: Dialogic proximity and perceived empathy without falling into excessive or deceptive anthropomorphism (Silva & Canedo, 2024; Truong & Chen, 2025). Problem it solves: Excessive anthropomorphism, emotional dependence, identity confusion.
3. Contextual memory: The assistant's capacity to acquire and recall the user's context (family, social environment, and historical preferences) in an active manner (Maree, 2025; Virvou, 2023). Problem it solves: Disconnected interactions, loss of continuity, irrelevant responses.
4. Communicative coherence and moderate empathy: Respectful, courteous, and context-appropriate linguistic responses to strengthen the interaction bond (Truong & Chen, 2025). Problem it solves: Emotional tension, robotic perception, poor conversational experience.
5. Avoiding emotional anthropomorphization: Not simulating artificial human affect or feelings, nor generating psychological dependence of the user toward the system (Silva & Canedo, 2024). Problem it solves: Psychological dependence, confusion about the nature of the system.

4.4. Data quality and semantics

1. Semantic enrichment: Native linking of data and concepts to global reference ontologies and authority control (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Vidal et al., 2025). Problem it solves: Incomplete data, poor relations, lack of context.
2. Symbolic logic-based inferences: Formal first-order logic (FOL) rules that govern and constrain the system's decisions (Herrera et al., 2026; Spillo et al., 2024). Problem it solves: Logical incoherences, hallucinations, reasoning errors.
3. Automatic consistency validation: Automatic detection or querying of possible contradictions, inconsistencies, or implausible dates (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Vidal et al., 2025). Problem it solves: Contradictions, impossible dates, factual inconsistencies.
4. Semantic ambiguity control: Clear labeling, avoiding redundancies or flattened definitions through the use of declared entity schemas (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025). Problem it solves: Redundant labeling, confusing relations, loss of precision.

4.5. Narrative, Cognition, and Knowledge Presentation

1. Support for non-linear narratives: Allowing multiple routes of free exploration and active learning, fostering user serendipity (Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Narrative rigidity, difficulty exploring complex relations.
2. Adequate contextualization: Integration of temporal, geographic, and role metadata that bring historical richness to the represented knowledge (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025). Problem it solves: Decontextualization, loss of historical meaning, erroneous interpretations.
3. Avoiding excessive simplification: Preserving the richness and complexity of real relations (using reified statements) instead of flattening reality into simple lists (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Vidal et al., 2025). Problem it solves: Semantic flattening, loss of complexity, invisibilization of nuances.
4. Cognitive load management: Clear, step-by-step, contextually adapted generative responses to avoid overwhelm (Omar et al., 2025; Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Overwhelm, abandonment, difficulty understanding.

4.6. Good practices

1. Content-based explainability: Translation and expression of complex graph relations and routes into friendly responses expressed in natural language (Maree, 2025; Yeo et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Opacity, incomprehension of decisions, distrust.
2. Suggestive responses: Active proposal of options or follow-up questions that activate the user's reflection processes and critical thinking (Yamamoto, 2024). Problem it solves: Cognitive passivity, lack of critical thinking.
3. Engineering the "social encounter": Presentation of courteous communicative styles, unequivocal and coherent self-definition of the system's identity, and respectful use of facts (Silva & Canedo, 2024). Problem it solves: Identity confusion, misleading perception, lack of communicative courtesy.
4. Learning scaffolds: Support structures and cognitive scaffolding designed to activate and foster autonomy in writing and learning, avoiding offering already solved or prefabricated responses (Chia & Frattarola, 2025; Pastrakis et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Cognitive dependence, difficulty learning, loss of autonomy.
5. Syntactic aid in query formulation: Active writing suggestions or autocompletion (assisted prompts) to guide non-expert users during writing (Vacanti et al., 2024). Problem it solves: Articulation barrier, difficulty formulating complex queries.

4.7. Practices to avoid

1. Hiding non-human identity: An event of special ethical gravity; the system must always transparently announce itself as a chatbot or AI from the start (Silva & Canedo, 2024). Problem it solves: Deception, loss of trust, serious ethical risks.
2. Dependence on the "hallucination circle": Not exposing users to probabilistic language models without the deterministic control and strict validation of a data ontology or RAG based on a curated KG (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Herrera et al., 2026). Problem it solves: Factual errors, incoherences, misinformation.
3. Creating an articulation barrier: Users should not be required to write extremely complex, technical, or artistically styled instructions to achieve good results (Vacanti et al., 2024). Problem it solves: Exclusion of non-expert users, difficulty of access.
4. Repetitive or mechanical messages: Avoiding excessively repetitive, robotic, or content-empty communication that generates rejection in the conversational experience (Silva & Canedo, 2024). Problem it solves: Rejection, robotic perception, poor conversational experience.

5. Ignoring decision styles: Avoiding designing rigid interaction paths; support must be given both to people who analyze down to the last detail (maximizers) and to those who seek quick and sufficient paths (satisficers) (Shin et al., 2025). Problem it solves: Frustration, inadequacy of the interaction flow.

5. Applied case: HerStory-NeSyAI

5.1. General description of the HerStory-NeSyAI project

HerStory-NeSyAI is a research project that seeks to develop a hybrid artificial intelligence architecture to structure and connect fragmented historical data on Francoist repression. The project (PID2023-147673OB-I00), coordinated by the Women and Wikipedia team at the Universitat de Barcelona, is being carried out between 2024 and 2027 and is framed within Digital Humanities, Information Science, and neurosymbolic AI (Ferran-Ferrer, 2023; Ferran-Ferrer & Centelles, 2025).

According to Gómez-Rico (2026), the system's design is based on a double hybridization:

1. Human-machine hybridization, which integrates the participation of historians, relatives of victims, activists, and Wikipedians through user-centered design methodologies, action research, and citizen science. This approach, conceptualized as citizen digital humanities, seeks to ensure that the domain modeling and system semantics adequately reflect the social and historical realities they aim to document (Gómez-Rico, 2026; Ferran-Ferrer, 2023).
2. Technological hybridization, which combines large language models with knowledge graphs in a neurosymbolic architecture.

The project implements a two-layer graph that combines within a single structure an ontological layer, which formalizes the classes, properties, and axioms of the domain and incorporates feminist perspectives in the definition of categories, and an entity layer, which contains biographical profiles of individuals, places, and historical facts linked to Wikidata and Wikipedia (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025). The graph is structured in RDF, uses OWL vocabularies, and is published via SPARQL, API, or dumps, following the FAIR and CARE principles (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Ferran-Ferrer, 2023). Moreover, it acts as the knowledge base for a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline that guides the behavior of LLMs. In addition, it structures semantic retrieval, favours contextual coherence, allows dynamic updating, and contributes to providing explainability and traceability in generated responses (Ferran-Ferrer & Centelles, 2025).

33

The initial graph data come from five key repositories: Cultura y Censura, SIDBRINT, the list of common graves in Catalonia, the judicial proceedings database (IHR), and the collection on repression of teachers and students at the Universitat de Barcelona. Through OpenRefine, these data are reconciled and linked with Wikidata, integrating local knowledge into the global semantic web. As Centelles and Ferran-Ferrer (2025) indicate, this connection facilitates the generation of verifiable biographical drafts and contributes to expanding the presence of women who were victims of repression in open knowledge environments such as Wikipedia.

5.2. Theoretical framework: HerStory-NeSyAI

The analysis of scientific literature published between 2022 and 2026 shows that the most recurrent challenges in generative AI systems are the lack of semantic coherence, the absence of traceability, and the difficulty in controlling hallucinations in sensitive domains.

5.2.1. Semantic coherence

Herrera et al. (2026) describe the logical-syntactic dissociation, a phenomenon whereby LLMs can generate syntactically correct texts that are epistemologically erroneous.

To mitigate this, the literature recommends incorporating symbolic mechanisms that validate generative outputs in a more controlled manner. HerStory-NeSyAI partially adopts this recommendation by using the knowledge graph as a "symbolic anchor" intended to constrain the probabilistic hypotheses of the model and seeks to ensure that narratives about Francoist repression are generated primarily from verified data.

Furthermore, Chen et al. (2025) demonstrate that models combining empirical data with symbolic rules initially extracted with LLMs and subsequently validated by experts achieve much higher coherence than conventional machine learning models. This evidence supports the choice of a hybrid architecture that integrates expert validation, OpenRefine, and data schema patterns as part of the HerStory-NeSyAI workflow.

5.2.2. Traceability

The fragmentation of historical memory across heterogeneous repositories is a recurring problem in the Digital Humanities. Centelles and Ferran-Ferrer (2025) point out that many high-value databases end up confined in silos that hinder the retrieval and visibility of historically silenced groups. In response, HerStory-NeSyAI adopts the Wikidata ontology as a core interoperability layer and uses reified statements with qualifiers of role, temporality, and references to capture complex historical relationships without simplifying them.

This approach aligns with Vidal et al. (2025), who identify knowledge graphs as the most robust technology for integrating heterogeneous and multimodal data. Furthermore, the experiments of Spillo et al. (2024) show that introducing first-order logic rules extracted from graphs can improve the precision and diversity of results, reinforcing the need for symbolic models that contribute to providing traceability and consistency.

5.2.3. Hallucination control in sensitive domains

In heritage environments, keyword searches often fail to reveal the familial, spatial, or repressive connections that structure the trajectories of victims. Faced with this limitation, Maree (2025) proposes neurosymbolic architectures that combine the internal semantic relationship of the graph with the user's contextual relevance to guide them toward significant discoveries. This model provides theoretical support for the functioning of the HerStory-NeSyAI conversational assistant, which integrates an LLM with structured data to generate understandable explanations tailored to the cognitive needs of historians and family members.

Finally, Zhao et al. (2026) introduce the concept of dynamic ontology instantiation through intelligent agents that orchestrate, in real time, the search and reasoning over knowledge graphs. This approach opens the door to the future automation of data curation in HerStory-NeSyAI, which could allow the system to continuously adapt to new archival findings or contributions from citizen science without compromising hallucination control.

5.3. List of requirements, best practices, and bad practices

The recent literature and technical documents of the HerStory-NeSyAI project identify a set of technological and design requirements that should guide the construction of the knowledge graph and its integration with language models. These requirements are complemented by best practices that guide modelling and hybridization, and by bad practices that must be avoided to not compromise the quality, coherence, and epistemic justice of the system.

35

5.3.1. Requirements

1. Semantic interoperability and global alignment:
Knowledge graphs must be designed using standard modelling languages such as OWL (Web Ontology Language) and RDF (Resource Description Framework) to guarantee semantic rigour and enable formal logic. This requirement implies resolving interoperability conflicts and harmonising fragmented data through alignment with global vocabularies and semantic cores such as Wikidata. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Herrera et al., 2026; Vidal et al., 2025) Problem it solves: Fragmentation of knowledge, schema conflicts, semantic inconsistencies, and impossibility of performing reliable global queries.

2. Structural validation:
It is critical to implement quality controls that deterministically validate that data conform to defined guidelines. Data schemas should be used to establish the expected behaviour of classes, and SHACL (Shapes Constraint Language) to check and enforce logical constraints as knowledge evolves. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Vidal et al., 2025) Problem it solves: Modelling errors, incomplete data, violations of logical constraints, and inconsistencies that can increase the risk of hallucinations in LLMs.

3. Multidimensional modelling with reified structures:
To avoid the loss of historical or contextual nuances (time, place, role), the architecture must use reified statements, qualifiers, and references, which allow representing complex assertions without flattening the information into a single path. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025) Problem it solves: Semantic flattening, loss of historical context, invisibilisation of nuances, and inability to represent complex relationships.

36

4. Traceability of provenance and equity audits:
Every triple or statement introduced into the graph must have a rigorous record of archival or institutional provenance. This is fundamental to enabling SPARQL queries that detect coverage gaps, evaluate data quality, and allow epistemic justice audits on the visibility of silenced groups. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025) Problem it solves: Lack of factual verification, absence of quality control, invisibilisation of groups, and impossibility of detecting structural biases.

5. Logical time management:
When domain knowledge is ephemeral or changing, the graph must incorporate a relaxed temporal factor through Time-Aware Knowledge Graphs (TCKG), using temporal vectors to track the evolution of relationships and prevent semantic drift. (Herrera et al., 2026) Problem it solves: Semantic drift, errors in temporal inferences, confusion between sequential facts, and inability to represent historical evolutions.

5.3.2. Best practices

1. Neurosymbolic architectures as logical safety barriers:
By coupling LLMs with knowledge graphs, the graph can serve as a "symbolic anchor" that helps constrain the probabilistic output of the text generator, reducing the risk of factual hallucinations and promoting more controlled logical coherence. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Herrera et al., 2026; Vidal et al., 2025) Problem it solves: Factual hallucinations, logical inconsistencies, opacity in generation, and lack of deterministic verification.

2. Heuristics for selecting FOL rules according to graph density:
When injecting first-order logic (FOL) rules into graph vectors (KGE), heuristics based on confidence and coverage metrics must be applied. (Spillo et al., 2024) Problem it solves: Erroneous inferences, semantic noise, indiscriminate application of rules, and loss of precision in link prediction.

37

3. Modelling of positive, negative, and counterfactual facts:
To optimise precision in link prediction or sensitive decision-making, it is good practice to explicitly encode positive facts, negative facts, and counterfactual facts, rather than assuming that what does not appear is false. (Vidal et al., 2025) Problem it solves: Ambiguity in predictions, incorrect classifications, absence of negative facts, and inability to represent alternative scenarios.

4. Integration pipeline and co-creative phased design:
The migration from databases to knowledge graphs should be carried out through a structured participatory workflow. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Chen et al., 2025) Problem it solves: Chaotic migrations, loss of ontological coherence, human errors in semantic reconciliation, and lack of expert participation.

5. Dynamic ontological instantiation:
In domains where data changes in real time, semantic, kinetic, and dynamic elements must be coordinated through dynamic ontology instantiation, which guides the behaviour of local Small Language Models and resolves latency problems. (Zhao et al., 2026) Problem it solves: Latency in dynamic data, outdated local models, inconsistencies between ontology and emerging data, and synchronisation problems.

5.3.3. Bad practices

1. Creation and maintenance of data silos:
Duplicating or storing entities in local databases without persistence connectors or external links fragments information, hinders indexing, and perpetuates historical invisibility. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025) Problem it generates: Fragmentation of knowledge, duplication of entities, invisibilisation of profiles, and loss of interoperability.

38

2. Reliance on generative AI without any verification element:
Relying on probabilistic generative models without deterministic validation controlled by a knowledge graph can produce syntactically impeccable but logically false results. (Herrera et al., 2026) Problem it generates: Factual hallucinations, partially incorrect results, ethical implications, and potential reduction of system credibility.

3. Semantic flattening of complex relational data:
Reducing dense relationships to flat taxonomies prevents the detection of indirect relationships or logical contradictions. (Herrera et al., 2026) Problem it generates: Loss of nuance, inability to detect contradictions, excessive simplification, and invisibilisation of context.

4. Meaning-agnostic searches over relationships:
Relying on graph traversal algorithms ignores the semantic meaning of the graph and generates a combinatorial explosion of trivial or irrelevant paths. (Maree, 2025) Problem it generates: Irrelevant paths, combinatorial explosion, unhelpful results, and exploration disconnected from meaning.

5. Use of rigid categories and binary gender taxonomies:
Fixed categories invisibilise minorities and degrade the quality of the knowledge organisation system. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025) Problem it generates: Invisibilisation of minorities, gender biases, classification errors, and degradation of knowledge quality.

6. Massive data uploads without verifying licences or provenance:
Uploading massive data without checking the licence (such as Creative Commons licences) or without documenting provenance compromises the reliability and security of researchers. (Centelles Velilla & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025) Problem it generates: Legal risks, unverified data, loss of graph reliability, and compromised security for researchers.

5.5. HerStory-NeSyAI vs LLM models

HerStory-NeSyAI represents a significant advance over conventional LLMs and classic documentary systems (Ferran-Ferrer & Centelles, 2025). Traditional generative models function as probabilistic black boxes: they generate coherent text, but without truthfulness control, with risk of hallucinations, omissions of women due to lack of representation in training corpora, and reproduction of gender biases (Herrera et al., 2026; Vidal et al., 2025). In sensitive historical environments, these limitations are especially problematic, as they can compromise the verifiability, the right to memory, and documentary traceability (Ferran-Ferrer & Centelles, 2025).

HerStory-NeSyAI partially addresses this problem by using the knowledge graph as a truth anchor and integrating it with a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) mechanism. This architecture guides the LLM to base itself primarily on verified, structured, and contextualised data, reducing the risk of hallucinations and improving logical coherence in narratives about Francoist repression (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025; Herrera et al., 2026). Unlike traditional databases, which act as isolated silos and hinder the reconstruction of life trajectories, HerStory-NeSyAI integrates and reconciles the project's five databases,

links them with Wikidata and opens them to the global semantic web (Centelles & Ferran-Ferrer, 2025). This allows a relational, temporal and contextual exploration of the information and facilitates the supervised generation of reliable biographical drafts, contributing to reducing the gender gap in open knowledge environments such as Wikipedia (Ferran-Ferrer, 2023). Overall, HerStory‑NeSyAI is presented as a potentially safer, verifiable and fairer alternative to conventional LLMs, and as a promising model in Digital Humanities, Information Science and responsible AI (Ferran‑Ferrer & Centelles, 2025; Gómez-Rico, 2026).

6. Discussion
The two scoping reviews offer a clear framework for interpreting the challenges posed by the use of generative models in heritage contexts: beyond the technical limitations, the literature emphasises the epistemological and social implications of automated narrative generation (Ferran-Ferrer, 2023; Ferran-Ferrer & Centelles, 2025). From a user-centred design perspective, this means that any technical solution must be accompanied by information architecture decisions that facilitate verification, comprehension and control on the part of the affected communities. In practical terms, this not only orientates the design of the interface or the information flow, but also conditions the priorities of curation, provenance documentation and validation mechanisms (Kuroki Júnior & Gottschalg‑Duque, 2023; Omar et al., 2025; Virvou, 2023). The review on knowledge graphs and neuro-symbolic architectures can be read as a condition of operability: the integration of structured data with neural models reduces certain forms of hallucination, but also requires explicit decisions about modelling, reification and alignment with external repositories (Centelles & Ferran‑Ferrer, 2025). This raises practical tensions that the literature has not yet fully resolved: how to balance automation and human control, what levels of semantic granularity are viable, and what maintenance and governance costs these solutions entail. Finally, the methodological limitations of the study must be considered: the selected time frame (2022-2026) and the reduction in the type of document — remember that only scientific articles were selected — may have left out some works that could have provided additional information to the reviews. Nonetheless, these limitations affect the coverage but not the internal coherence of the proposed requirements framework.

7. Conclusions
7.1. Response to the objectives
The aim of this study was to identify and systematise requirements for trustworthy generative AI in sensitive contexts. In relation to objective 1, the state of the art on the integration between information architecture, user experience and user-centred design in generative AI environments has been mapped. With regard to objective 2, two lists have been derived — of requirements, and of good practices and bad practices — that orient the design of generative systems that are comprehensible, traceable and sensitive to biases. Finally, objective 3 has been addressed through the analysis of the HerStory‑NeSyAI project, which has allowed exploring the transferability of these requirements in a real case linked to epistemic justice.

7.2. Contributions of the study
The study provides a conceptual map that articulates challenges and possible lines of solution, two structured lists of requirements extracted from the recent literature, and an applied analysis that identifies areas where traceability, governance and semantic coherence play a particularly relevant role. This set of results configures a framework that can serve as a basis for future approaches to the design of generative systems in heritage environments or in other sensitive contexts.

7.3. Limitations and future lines
The main limitations of the study relate to the time frame of the analysed corpus (2022–2026) and the exclusive selection of academic articles, which may have left out complementary contributions. Furthermore, the HerStory‑NeSyAI prototype is still under construction, which prevents direct validation of the identified requirements. With an eye to future lines of research, it would be pertinent to explore the application of these requirements in other sensitive domains and in multimodal generative systems, as well as to evaluate them in the HerStory‑NeSyAI prototype when it becomes operational.
