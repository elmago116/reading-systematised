Shaping Knowledge: Access, Education, and Struggle

 Information Heroism

Ken

Herold

Introduction

Archie Dick
 writes of information heroes
 (
Dick, 2014
) in vividly descriptive language, binding traditional duties performed by librarians
, archivists, scholars, non-professional librarians, prisoner-librarians, and activist library members with their heroic actions in times of riot, social conflict, revolution, war, censorship
, unrest, and other states
 of emergency. Personal risk is an essential factor in his telling, as exemplified by arrest, detention, interrogation, deportation, surveillance, and otherwise severe harm occurring in apartheid
-era South Africa, or indeed in any suffering community. What are these heroic actions? To assure access to information
 by saving antiquities, preserving cultural heritage, treating banned materials, securing borrower privacy, providing reading kits and instruction, opening spaces for debate and discussion such as reading circles
 and study groups, and generally making safe refuge for teaching and learning. All who act unselfishly within an information struggle do so under extraordinary circumstances and duress and deserve our recognition for being brave information heroes.

The precise English phrase “information hero” has most extensively been utilized by the international media freedom watchdog Reporters Without Borders
. On World Press Freedom Day in 2014, the same year as Dick’s work, 100 information heroes were recognized. “Through their courageous work or activism, these ‘100 heroes’ help to promote the freedom … to seek, receive and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers” (Reporters Without Borders [RSF], 2014, n.p.). Journalists, public media officers, and whistleblowers in general perform heroic tasks in the cause of freedom of information. While Dick has explicitly included whistleblowers within his expositions of the information hero, I will argue for a basis of understanding such from the ground of global library and information science/studies (LIS) and not from journalism per se. The hard truth is today’s journalists are targeted for murder and assassination and librarians
 for arrest and prosecution.

I hypothesize “information heroism” as a collective form of charismatic intuition and begin with an examination of heroism science
 and the notion of cyberhero
. Characterized not in terms of first-person leadership and its typical achievement of the hero’s personal quests, instead I seek a mode of digital altruism and online collaborative problem-solving. I then review the concept of information ecologies to situate the heroic struggle within systems of people, practices, values, and information technologies as grounds for altruism and collaboration. An apex 
notion here is an informational 
bodhisattva
 principle
: nurturing the plural awareness of others within the infosphere
 as a priority over one’s own attainments. This highly engaging role is found in the view of librarians
 as a keystone species
, libraries being the seminal evolutionary development of the knowledge world. Roots of information use are deeply documented in our LIS professional literature, with more recent examinations of the epistemological challenges
1
 for our keystone role.
