necessity programmatic. But I hope they suffice to show that there is some mileage to be gained 
from thinking of epistemic risk in less individualistic terms than does the current literature.

2.1 | Chance
The first desideratum in the literature is that an account of epistemic risk should meet a 
“chance condition” (Broncano-  Berrocal  2015, 1): it should count as risky only events that are 
quite likely to occur. I spell out some more specific ways of understanding this notion in sec -
tion 4. For now, let me note that some such condition is part of both our folk and our technical 
notions of epistemic risk. Concerning the former, consider a lecturer saying to a student, “You 
are risking expulsion, but you are highly unlikely to be expelled.” There is something conversa -
tionally infelicitous about this. The whole point of warning someone of a risk is to warn them 
of something that is likely to happen to them.
2.2 | Luck
As mentioned at the opening of this paper, the main point of having the concept of epistemic 
risk is to have a handle on a serious obstacle for granting a (true) belief the status of knowledge— 
luck. The link between risk and luck is sometimes taken to be so strong that some authors 
argue that the two notions are interchangeable (Broncano-  Berrocal  2015).3 Others maintain 
that a risk-  based epistemology can do the same work for us as, and do it better than, a luck 
epistemology can (Pritchard 2016 ).
There are, however, also accounts in the literature that deviate from this narrow, anti-  safety 
conception of epistemic risk. For instance, Navarro's argument that not finding out about 
something—  that is, not having a belief— can be an epistemic risk puts safety out of the picture. 
And so does Broncano- Berrocal's notion of agential risk, since safety isn't a property of agents 
but one of events (cognitive successes).
So, the most reasonable version of this desideratum on an account of epistemic risk is that 
it has some connection to epistemic luck.
2.3 | Significance
The third desideratum is what Broncano-  Berrocal calls “the significance condition”: R needs 
to be of significance to someone or would be if she were fully informed, in order to qualify as a 
risk for them. This is also part of our folk notion of risk. Consider “You are at risk of destroy -
ing a pile of soil in my garden.” If there is a way of making this sentence meaningful, it would 
be by showing how this pile of soil is important.

2.5 | Control
Finally, some authors posit what Broncano-  Berrocal  (2015, 1) calls a control condition on 
risk— roughly, the requirement that in order for something to be risky it needs to be beyond 
one's control. Now, I am not convinced that this condition is necessary for epistemic risk or for 
risk more generally.5 (For example, I can ask you not to point that electric jigsaw at me because 
you are placing my hand at risk. If you weren't in control of this risk, it would seem silly of me 
to ask you to desist.) But as we will see, the phenomena that I discuss below often meet this 
condition in any case, so we needn't settle this issue here.
With these rough desiderata in place, the stage is set for making the case that some forms of 
epistemic injustice pose epistemic risks. I first make an intuitive case for this claim (section 3) 
and then show that the intuitive risks also qualify for being epistemic risks proper, since they 
satisfy the desiderata just outlined (section 4).
3 | WHITE IGNORANCE AND ITS RISKS
In this section, I argue that white ignorance comes with distinctive epistemic risks, where we 
understand such risks in a pre-  theoretical way.
3.1 | White ignorance
White ignorance is the deliberate ignorance of Black people's experiences and of the ways in 
which white privilege depends on oppression and injustice. 6 Charles Mills, the term's godfa -
ther, defines white ignorance as “a non- knowing, that is not contingent, but in which race— 
white racism and/or white racial domination and their ramifications—  plays a crucial causal 
role” (Mills 2007, 20).
Such ignorance can concern any aspect of Black lives in a still largely racist world, and it 
serves to maintain the white person's sense of innocence about what white privilege costs oth -
ers. White ignorance can range over purely factual beliefs, for example concerning routine and 
institutionalised police brutality towards Black people; to ethically thicker beliefs such as the 
prevalent white belief that Black people and white people have equal socio-  economic opportu -
nities; to outright moral judgements, such as the white myth of the virtues of not seeing colour. 
People who claim to have this superpower consider it a fine sign of non- racism, instead of what 
it is— one of the most pernicious forms of racism, now popularly known as “colour-  blind rac -
ism” (e.g., Bonilla- Silva, 2006).7

3.2 | The epistemic risks of white ignorance
I now make the case that white ignorance comes with distinctively epistemic risks, where this 
notion is understood in a pre-  theoretical sense. Two groups of risks are conspicuous—  self- 
directed and other-  directed.
Self- directed risks
The first self- directed epistemic risk of white ignorance is that the white- ignorant person makes 
certain epistemic reasons unavailable to herself: insisting on not seeing the very thing that is re -
sponsible for one's dominance automatically makes unavailable certain kinds of evidence. For 
instance, suppose that an award got given— yet again— to a white male graduate by an all- white 
panel with a reputation for racism.9 The white- ignorant person is likely to see the award as evi -
dence that the graduate was the most able candidate in a pool of candidates who all had a genu-
inely equal opportunity to get the award. This conveniently erases race in at least four ways: (a) the 
racial homogeneity of the panel members, (b) the strange coincidence of their race and the suc -
cessful candidate's, (c) the panel's reputation for racism, and (d) the fact that all the unsuccessful 
candidates who supposedly had this equal opportunity are Black. This erasure of race, in turn, 
amounts to an erasure of all sorts of evidence against the belief that merit was all that mattered in 
the selection procedure; that the institution in which the selection process took place is unjust (a 
and c); that the panel is likely to maintain the racist status quo (b and c); and that not all candidates

The second self-  directed epistemic risk of white ignorance are various epistemic vices. To 
begin with, the most recent analyses of ignorance construe it as itself an epistemic vice or a 
manifestation of one even when it isn't of the wilful variety that white ignorance is. For in -
stance, Anne Meylan argues that the only way to capture the disvalue of ignorance is by think -
ing of it as the manifestation of epistemic insouciance (Meylan  2020; see also Pritchard  2021). 
This kind of vice, however, can't be a risk of white ignorance, since a risk is meant to be some 
likely consequence  of white ignorance, not a constitutive part of it.
But it is easy to see how other vices can be consequences of white ignorance, in the rele -
vant sense. The most obvious are the vices José Medina isolates as “the epistemic vices that 
privileged subjects develop to protect themselves” (Medina  2013, 33)— epistemic arrogance, 
epistemic laziness, and closed-  mindedness.
Epistemic arrogance is one of the natural props of white ignorance conceived as a mecha -
nism for the self- protection of white privilege. Such self-  protection requires what Medina calls 
(after Tocqueville) “ruling without resistance” (31). But such ruling, in turn, crucially involves 
“not being called into question in one's opinions, that is, having an undisputed cognitive au -
thority” (31). Assuming this undisputed authority when it hasn't been earned is, of course, 
simply the vice of intellectual arrogance.
But white privilege isn't simply maintained  through white ignorance. It is also kept hidden 
from view, in order to sanitise the white person's sense of integrity and moral worth. This 
leads to epistemic laziness, the “socially produced and carefully orchestrated lack of curios -
ity” (Medina  2013, 31). This description should remind the reader of what I called the second 
feature of white ignorance in section 3.1— that it is a form of group- motivated irrationality that 
affects all cognition. It is precisely such orchestrated lack of curiosity that enables white group 
interest to suppress evidence against uncomfortable beliefs and uphold the convenient ones in 
the face of counterevidence.
The third and final vice (I discuss here) that white ignorance fosters is that of closed- 
mindedness. This should already be apparent from the discussion of epistemic laziness, where 
the dominant person was said to need undisputed and unearned cognitive authority. But re -
call, too, that white ignorance is partly defined as a deliberate avoidance of knowledge of 
the experiences of the oppressed as well as of what one's privilege costs others. This is plain 
closed- mindedness—  “when one's mental processing remains systematically closed to certain 
phenomena, experiences, and perspectives, come what may” (Medina 2013 , 34).
Other- directed risks
White ignorance also comes with many other-  directed risks. Putting aside all the obvious 
moral ones, let me focus on two epistemic risks. The source of both is that white ignorance cre -
ates and widens what Miranda Fricker calls “hermeneutical lacunas” (Fricker  2007, 151)— 
gaps in our collective self-  understanding, which make certain explanations and reasons for 
belief unavailable in the general knowledge-  economy. White ignorance ensures that resources 
developed by the oppressed to make sense of their experiences don't get uptake, hence don't 
come into the knowledge-  economy, enforcing their owners' exclusion from knowledge produc -
tion and undermining their ability to effectively combat epistemic injustices. 10

The first other-  directed epistemic risk, then, is to one's epistemic environment. 11 This envi-
ronment is integral to knowledge formation. The most drastic dramatisation of this comes 
with the sceptic's old friends the evil genie and the proverbial vat. Less dramatically, knowl -
edge can also be undermined through “environmental luck” (Pritchard  2016, 568), where the 
peculiarity of one's environment ensures that although one's belief is true and justified, it 
cannot constitute knowledge. This is one version of epistemic risk, conceived in the narrow 
sense, since it equally breaches the safety condition: when one “enjoys” environmental luck, 
one's belief could have been so easily false. I have more to say on these points in section 4 . For 
the moment, it is enough to note that the first other-  directed epistemic risk of white ignorance 
is the way its attendant hermeneutical lacunas degrade one's epistemic environment by mak -
ing unavailable to everyone the epistemic resources that should be available.
The second other-  directed risk of white ignorance is that of epistemically harming oth -
ers. One potential kind of harm is again expressible in vice terms: those oppressed by white- 
ignorant persons may develop the obverse of the vices of privilege just discussed, such as 
excessive humility (Medina  2013, 41; see also Kidd  2020, 73). But since some argue that the 
oppressed also develop epistemic virtues as a result of their oppression (Medina  2013, 40–  48), 
this risk of white ignorance is more complicated than the clear ones with which I am building 
an intuitive case here. So, I will not push this point beyond a mere hint.
The more obvious sense, then, in which white ignorance harms those around the white-  
ignorant person is that they are literally deprived of reasons to explain and justify to others what 
they think and do. Recall that the way I made the hermeneutical lacuna point was that existing 
epistemic resources don't get uptake and so don't get to contribute to the knowledge-  economy. 
Thus, for instance, if a Black person tries to explain something to a white-  ignorant person by 
appeal to double consciousness, the white- ignorant person will be literally unable to understand 
the kind of explanation that is being offered.12 Since this white- ignorant person doesn't have the 
concept of double consciousness in their hermeneutic repertoire, they cannot use it to feature in 
reasons to believe things about the oppressed. Thus, white ignorance literally deprives the op -
pressed of reasons when it comes to explaining and justifying their thoughts and behaviour.
It could be objected at this point that this sense of harm is too tenuously related to knowledge 
to merit the label “epistemic risk.” After all, the reasons in question here are not ones that base 
belief in a way that determines whether it gets to qualify as knowledge. But I think that the objec-
tion is misguided. When an epistemic resource is continuously ignored by people in power, the 
owners of this resource either start losing confidence in its hermeneutical powers or leave the 
epistemic community, forming their own community, where their resources are perfectly ade -
quate. If they start losing confidence, this resource's ability to base the owners' beliefs is likewise 
weakened. Thus, if the epistemically powerful keep failing to understand my explanation of my 
action in terms of double consciousness, I may come to doubt both myself and my belief that I 
have indeed acted the way I did due to double consciousness. This doubt then serves as a defeater 
to my knowledge that I have acted for this reason.13 This is a risk for the marginalised.

then there are risks for both sides. Since we are focusing on other-  directed risk, let me focus 
only on the risk for the marginalised: it is that they are now completely beyond the possibility 
of being part of the mainstream knowledge-  economy— including the possibility of shaping it

There is much more to say on white ignorance, but I hope that I have provided enough of a 
rough sketch to give a sense of its intuitive epistemic risks. A final note: white ignorance isn't 
some isolated, rare phenomenon. It is a thoroughly pervasive feature of our world. It is also,

dominant social identity and whose maintenance is in that identity's interest. 15 These are all 
forms of “willful hermeneutical ignorance” (Pohlhaus  2012), the chief cognitive mechanism for 
sustaining our white supremacist, andronormative, heteronormative, and ableist world. So, the 
risks that these kinds of ignorance pose cannot be ignored by a comprehensive epistemology.
4 | SOCIALISING EPISTEMIC RISK
I have so far used the term “epistemic risk” loosely in order to tease out intuitions about the 
sorts of harms that come with white ignorance. In this section, I show that these harms indeed 
merit the label “epistemic risk.” I first sketch, based on the discussion so far, a more social 
notion of risk than the individualistic ones available in the literature—  what I call “epistemic 
injustice risk” (EIR) (section 4.1). I then show that EIR meets the desiderata outlined in section 
2, and so merits the label “epistemic risk’” (section 4.2).
4.1 | Epistemic injustice risk
Here is a rough proposal based on the discussion of white ignorance:
Epistemic Injustice Risk (EIR)
R is an epistemic injustice risk of practice P if and only if:
 (i) Unjust: P is epistemically unjust.
 (ii) Likely: R is a very likely consequence of epistemic practice P and when R obtains, it 
does so in virtue of practice P.
 (iii) Risky : R poses a threat to knowledge by creating and widening hermeneutical

agents' confidence in their reasons, and/or perpetrating other similar epistemic

freeway and making it hard for some people to get to a library— also come with epistemic risks. 
But the kind of risk I have in mind needs to be embedded in unjust practices, in order to have 
the structural and systematic character that epistemic injustices do.

The second thing to note is that EIR can be a property of events, beliefs, and situations, on 
the one hand, and of epistemic agents, practices, and environments, on the other. I have thus 
left it unspecified for  what or whom EIR is a risk, stipulating only that it is associated with a 
practice. This is in line with the received view in the epistemic injustice literature, mentioned in 
section 2.1, that such injustices can be both structural and individual in character. We should, 
thus, understand the risks accompanying them in similarly broad terms. A fuller account of 
EIR would, of course, write this into the conditions, but I am just making a prima facie case 
here, hence a rough characterisation of EIR should be enough.
4.2 | Earning the label “epistemic risk”
Let me now show how these conditions meet the core desiderata for an account of epistemic 
risk, while at the same time going beyond them. This ensures that what we have here is epis -
temic risk proper, while simultaneously expanding our current concept.
Unjust
The first condition is that the practice whose risks we are considering be epistemically unjust. 
I leave the notion of epistemic injustice suitably intuitive, as simply something that wrongs 
someone in their capacity as epistemic agent. 16 The operative word here is “wrongs,” which I 
would like to distinguish from “harms.” (If I am thinking racist thoughts of you on a desert 
island, I am wronging you without harming you.) Importantly, the kind of wrongs that are 
relevant here are embedded in our largely unjust socio-  political structures and their attendant 
power relations.
Spelling out Unjust in terms of wronging rather than harming is important for making my 
account of epistemic risk non-  trivial. If we thought of epistemic injustice in terms of epistemic 
harms, then it wouldn't be surprising that such injustice comes with epistemic risks: whatever 
harms served to individuate the injustice in question would trivially turn out to be the risks of 
this practice. Here, in contrast, we can let the epistemically unjust practice be individuated by 
its wrongs, while the harms serve to base our account of the characteristic risks of the injustice. 
Notice that this strategy has the independent advantage of being able to better distinguish 
between various kinds of epistemic injustice. Many of these kinds come with harms similar to 
white ignorance, so if we were to appeal to harms alone in characterising the practices, we 
wouldn't be able to distinguish between them. 17
Now, Unjust isn't related to any core features of epistemic risk in the literature. It is, instead, 
what gives EIR its uniqueness. This was part of the original aim, recall: to show both how EIR is a 
form of epistemic risk and how it goes beyond existing conceptions of such risk. Unjust serves this 
latter function: it captures the sense in which our notion of risk goes beyond the individualistic 
existing conceptions. It also serves to make the notion of EIR more precise by specifying its causal 
history further. For instance, if I am a bad teacher, arguably I run the risk of widening herme -
neutical gaps, cultivating epistemic vices, and so on in my students (and so I meet Risky), and the 
likelihood that I do so is pretty high (and so I meet Likely), but I don't thereby run EIR.

Likely
The second condition, Likely , tallies with the first, chance desideratum. Chance conditions 
can be spelt out in probabilistic or in modal terms.18 Here, for instance, are two options offered 
by Broncano- Berrocal:
Probabilistic Risk (PR): E is at risk of occurring at t if and only if there is high 
probability that E will occur at t. (Broncano-  Berrocal 2015, 6)
Modal Risk (MR): E is at risk of occurring at t if and only if E would occur at t in 
a large enough proportion of close possible worlds. (5)
Likely can go with either of these readings,19 as long as the relevant probability and modality are 
understood conditionally. The probability that white ignorance results in hermeneutical gaps, the 
invisibility of reasons, epistemic vices, and the defeat of knowledge is really high, as I argued in 
section 3. The conditional version of this is that R (hermeneutical gaps, and so on) is highly prob-
able on P (white ignorance, and so on). Thus, if we let P(E) = P(R/P), EIR clearly satisfies Probability 
Risk.
Similarly, EIR satisfies Modal Risk , where our evaluation of E's modal closeness includes 
keeping the initial conditions fixed (Pritchard  2016, 563). The condition most relevant here is 
the presence of white ignorance. EIR will occur in most worlds in which there is white igno -
rance. And all those worlds are nearby: the nearness of a possible world is fixed by its similar -
ity to the actual world; and as long as conditions of racial injustice persist—  as they would in 
nearby possible worlds—  so will epistemic injustices with their risks. We would have to change 
a lot about the actual world, in other words, if we are to avoid epistemic injustices and their 
attendant risks.
Thus, we don't need to decide here on whether Likely  should be cashed out in probabilistic 
or modal terms. If, however, we want to keep the notion's connections to safety, we'll be better 
served by the modal version, since safety is a modal notion by all accounts. I have left the for -
mulation vague for those who think of risk in probabilistic terms, as long as they are clear that 
some of the advantages of the notion will be lost (such as the connection to luck in Risky below).
Notice that in light of the distinction between wrongs and harms I have just drawn, Likely  
isn't trivially satisfied: the wrongs of epistemic injustices are guaranteed, yes; but the harms 
aren't— they are merely very likely.
Risky
The third condition for EIR is that the risk candidate pose a threat to knowledge by creating 
and widening hermeneutical gaps, making reasons unavailable to oneself and others, fostering 
epistemic vices in oneself and others, and/or defeating knowledge by undermining a believer's 
confidence, and/or perpetuating other similar harms. This condition serves at least four func -
tions that connect it to the core desiderata for epistemic risk accounts.
First, Risk enables EIR to meet the second— Luck— desideratum, both in a general and in a 
more technical sense. To begin with, EIR is inimical to the knowledge enterprise as a whole in 
obvious ways. Knowledge requires that we aren't in an environment with large hermeneutical 
gaps, where epistemic vice proliferates and epistemic reasons are regularly made invisible. The

one's epistemic environment generally less knowledge conducive should be as important to 
epistemology as the technical safety of our beliefs.
EIR also relates more directly to luck. Recall from the opening of this paper that traditionally 
an epistemically risky belief- forming process is one in which, were it to produce a true belief, this 
success would be a matter of luck: that is, the belief could have been easily false on that basis. We 
can say the same of the risks of white ignorance. For instance, if a white- ignorant person were to 
form a true belief concerning someone's experience as a result of an explanation in terms of dou-
ble consciousness, that would be epistemically lucky: given one's intellectual arrogance and 
closed- mindedness and the fact that the concept “double consciousness” isn't in one's hermeneu-
tical repertoire, one could have so easily formed a false belief instead of the true one.20
Second, Risky clearly ties EIR to the third desideratum, Significance : in order to classify 
as a risk, R needs to be of significance to someone or would be if she were fully informed. 
Presumably not degrading our epistemic environment and thus undermining our knowledge 
projects would be significant to all of us were we fully informed.
Third, by being framed in terms of threats, the condition captures the negative valence of 
epistemic risk, the fourth desideratum. At the same time, this condition also adds to the social -
ity of this notion of risk: it is no longer just the interests and beliefs of individual agents that fix 
something's negative valence and hence its status as a risk. Rather, it is the significance of the 
harms in question to the whole epistemic community and environment that partly determines 
whether something is a risk.
Thus, EIR meets four central desiderata for an account of epistemic risk: it has a probabi -
listic or modal dimension ( Likely); it has the right connection to epistemic luck; it has a signif -
icance dimension; and it is negatively valenced ( Risky). The only desideratum that is missing is 
Control. Now, I have not posited such a condition because, as I said, I don't think it's necessary 
either for epistemic risk in general (as discussed in section 2.5 ) or for EIR in particular.
That said, as I noted earlier, many instances of EIR often meet, as a matter of fact, a version 
of Control. There are two reasons for this. First, such risks concern ignorance and hermeneutical 
lacunas, and they make unavailable to us reasons to believe that they exist. Thus, both EIRs and 
the practices from which they stem are often cognitively opaque to us, thereby undermining our 
control over their effects. But more important, epistemically unjust practices are often structural 
and systematic, so they pervade our lives in ways beyond our individual control.
To see this, consider two types of control condition—  what Broncano- Berrocal calls “effec -
tive control” and “tracking control.”
Effective Control: A has effective control over B  if and only if (i) it is A 's aim that 
B is in a certain state S, (ii) A  has a disposition to cause B  to be in S, and (iii) B  is 
in S because of A's disposition to cause B  to be in S. (Broncano- Berrocal 2015, 19)
Clearly, a white- ignorant person doesn't have this kind of control over EIR. Although it may be 
said that in some sense this person has the disposition to degrade the epistemic environment, 
cause hermeneutical lacunas, and so forth— thus meeting (ii)— and that the environment is this 
way because of their disposition (iii), this person certainly doesn't aim to do so, in any meaningful 
sense of “aim.” Their aim, rather, is to protect their moral hygiene. The rest comes whether they 
will or not. Thus, (i) is violated by the white- ignorant person.

5 | CONCLUSION
No doubt a detailed account of epistemic injustice risk will have to make more precise each of 
these conditions and relate them to neighbouring ones in the literature. But instead of doing 
this, I'd like to conclude by gesturing at the theoretical fruitfulness of having a social notion of 
risk along EIR lines. This will, one hopes, boost its philosophical credentials.
I have already suggested how a more social notion of epistemic risk such as EIR would 
benefit the epistemic- risk literature: it allows the literature to catch up to more recent con -
ceptions of knowledge, which honour the sociality of epistemic practices and practitioners. 
Once we have accepted this sociality, any account of central epistemic concepts that re -
volves solely around individuals and their properties becomes incomplete. EIR plugs this 
theoretical gap.
But EIR is also theoretically useful for the epistemic injustice literature. For instance, it 
holds the promise of helping epistemic injustice theorists solve what Robin McKenna (MS) 
calls their “responsibility problem.” Inspired by Heather Battaly's ( 2019) similar problem for 
vice epistemology, McKenna argues that this problem faces all epistemologies that take seri -
ously the social embeddedness of knowers and the sorts of biases and power relations that 
come with such embeddedness. 21 Epistemic injustice scholarship is his prime example. The 
problem, McKenna argues, is that once we acknowledge these social dimensions and their 
largely involuntary character, it becomes difficult to see where to locate our responsibility for 
character traits that perpetrate injustice but involve such largely involuntary elements.
To make the concern clearer in the context of the present paper, most of white ignorance is 
based on initial racist biases many of which are implicit, therefore beyond our ken and control. 
And as I have argued, white ignorance is procreative in this respect: it further exacerbates this 
involuntariness by creating large hermeneutical gaps that make unavailable as evidence the 
very concepts of the oppressed that would help the white-  ignorant person understand. Given 
this, it is difficult to see how to hold the white-  ignorant person epistemically responsible for 
their white ignorance. What are they responsible for?
If the current social proposal for epistemic risk works, the locus of epistemic responsibility 
is obvious: they are responsible for epistemic behaviour that incurs grave epistemic risks for 
themselves, others, and the epistemic environment. This is admittedly a consequentialist view 
of responsibility, but such a view is not implausible in the present context. As Eduardo Bonilla- 
Silva has persuasively argued ( 2006, 6–  9), if we construe responsibility for racism as purely 
a function of intention and motivation, rather than also as a function of the harmful conse -
quences of one's actions and attitudes, we would be overlooking important and pernicious 
forms of racism. (Think of my touching a Black colleague's hair. This is a classic case of a racist 
micro- aggression that enforces oppressive structures. My intentions are completely irrelevant
