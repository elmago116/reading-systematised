ABSTRACT
The paper argues that some tools from the epistemic injustice literature can be 
fruitfully applied to the debate on epistemic decolonisation. The first step for 
such a project is to defuse recent misgivings about the liberatory potential of 
epistemic injustice scholarship. I group these misgivings under the slogan 
‘Epistemic injustice is white-people stuff’, or ‘the WPS challenge’, for short, 
and use them to set desiderata for good theorising with epistemic injustice 
tools. I then look at three such tools – epistemic oppression, white ignorance, 
and contributory injustice – and argue that they advance theorising 
epistemic decolonisation while meeting the three desiderata. If the argument 
works, its implications for the topic of this special issue are that we don’t 
need to decolonise epistemic injustice; we can instead use the tools of the 
epistemic injustice literature to advance the decolonisation project.

D1 The tools aren’t appropriated or otherwise exploitatively obtained/

2. Epistemic decolonisation
Epistemic decolonisation, as I have argued elsewhere (Mitova 2020b), is in 
the first instance a project of recentring the knowledge enterprise to one’s 
own geo-political and social location. This crucially involves, as Sabelo 
Ndlovu-Gatsheni (2018) urges, reclaiming the right and ability to think 
and theorise from one’s own geo-cultural location, that is to choose for 
oneself the focus of one’s epistemic endeavours in accordance with 
one’s own conceptual schemas, social identities, and attendant interests.
The idea of recentring is most explicit in current African decolonial 
scholarship. For instance, Ndlovu-Gatsheni thinks of decolonisation as 
‘[a]n intellectual and academic process of centring Africa as a legitimate 
historical unit of analysis and epistemic site from which to interpret the

3. Epistemic oppression
According to Kristie Dotson, epistemic oppression is ‘the persistent epis -
temic exclusion that hinders one’s contribution to knowledge production’ 
(
2014b, 115). She develops an elaborate account of such exclusion, but for 
our purposes it is enough to understand this as ‘persistent infringement 
on one’s ability to use persuasively shared epistemic resources’ 
(Dotson  2014b, 116), where such ability ranges: 
from one’s ability to utilize those resources for effective and accurate communi-
cation; to being able to use a given set of shared resources to make sense of 
one’s experiences; to the ability to rely upon the existence of fair and accurate 
standards within shared epistemic resources. (Dotson 2014b, 116)
The underlying idea, as articulated by standpoint theorists and other fem-
inist epistemologists, is that one’s social identity embeds one in power 
relations which partly fix the limits of one’s epistemic agency and 
resources.

At the same time as being able to shed light on epistemic decolonisa -

ata for good use of epistemic injustice tools. First, Dotson is highly 
sensitive to the dangers of appropriation and exploitation. Recall that

Second, the notion of epistemic oppression, although possibly institu -
tionalised in some places in ways that might serve dominant rather than 
marginalised interests, is certainly not so in its original formulation, which 
I am using here. This explains why it can be helpful for advancing the 
interests of the colonised.
Finally, my use of the notion of epistemic oppression meets the third 
desideratum – it centres the perspective of the oppressed. Thinking 
about epistemic oppression itself places the experiences distinctive of 
marginalised identities at the centre of the knowledge enterprise. It is 
this perspective that matters in articulating which epistemic resources 
count for transforming this enterprise into a just one. And when using 
this tool to think about decolonisation, we are centring the perspective 
of the colonised.
Thus, the notion of epistemic oppression emerges both as useful for 
theorising epistemic decolonisation and as satisfying the desiderata for 
making just use of epistemic injustice tools.
4. White ignorance
Another resource of this kind is the concept of white ignorance. White 
ignorance, according to the term’s godfather Charles Mills is ‘a non- 
knowing, that is not contingent, but in which race – white racism and/ 
or white racial domination and their ramifications – plays a crucial 
causal role’ (Mills 2007
, 20). Mills provides a rich and harrowing character -
isation of the phenomenon, but I will only isolate here features of it that 
are of help to my argument. 16

5. Contributory injustice
The final concept from the epistemic injustice literature I will consider 
here is that of contributory injustice. Contributory injustice is, very 
crudely, what we get when we put together epistemic oppression and 
wilful ignorance. Less crudely: 
Contributory injustice is caused by an epistemic agent’s situated ignorance, in 
the form of willful hermeneutical ignorance, in maintaining and utilizing struc -
turally prejudiced hermeneutical resources that result in epistemic harm to the 
epistemic agency of a knower. (Dotson 2012, 31)
Such injustice occurs when marginalised epistemic resources don’t get 
uptake by dominant knowers, and hence are precluded from coming 
into, or shaping, the mainstream knowledge economy. This reinforces 
the marginalisation and exclusion from knowledge production of the 
marginalised, and consequently exacerbates their inability to effectively 
combat their epistemic oppression.
The notion of contributory injustice is, to my mind, indispensable for 
the project of epistemic decolonisation. In particular, I am not sure how 
we could articulate the core epistemic wrong of colonialism without 
appeal to contributory injustice. Colonisation was precisely about 
denying epistemic authority to the experts and epistemic resources of 
the colonised.

6. Conclusion
If these thoughts are on the right track, at least three concepts in the post- 
Fricker literature on epistemic injustice can help us theorise epistemic 
decolonisation in good ways, i.e. ways that meet the desiderata for just 
use of epistemic injustice tools. Moreover, these three concepts aren’t 
lone outliers in a sea of useless and WPS-prone concepts. On the contrary, 
they are related to many other concepts in the literature that are equally 
theoretically fruitful and attuned to the desiderata inspired by the WPS 
challenge.

## Can theorising epistemic injustice help us decolonise?

[Veli Mitova](https://philpapers.org/s/Veli%20Mitova "View other works by Veli Mitova")

*[Inquiry: An Interdisciplinary Journal of Philosophy](https://philpapers.org/asearch.pl?pub=420)* (forthcoming) Copy B IB T <sub>E</sub> X

## Abstract

The paper argues that some tools from the epistemic injustice literature can be fruitfully applied to the debate on epistemic decolonisation. The first step for such a project is to defuse recent misgivings about the liberatory potential of epistemic injustice scholarship. I group these misgivings under the slogan ‘Epistemic injustice is white-people stuff’, or ‘the WPS challenge’, for short, and use them to set desiderata for good theorising with epistemic injustice tools. I then look at three such tools – epistemic oppression, white ignorance, and contributory injustice – and argue that they advance theorising epistemic decolonisation while meeting the three desiderata. If the argument works, its implications for the topic of this special issue are that we don’t need to decolonise epistemic injustice; we can instead use the tools of the epistemic injustice literature to advance the decolonisation project.
