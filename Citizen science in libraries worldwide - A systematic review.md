Abstract
Open science and citizen science aim to make scientific research more inclusive, accessible, and transparent by involving the public 
in various aspects of the research process. Previous systematic reviews have explored the role of citizen science, focusing on specific 
regions, library types, or thematic areas. However, a comprehensive, global review addressing all library types and their involvement in 
citizen science is lacking. This study provides a systematic overview of the global landscape of citizen science in libraries, identifying key 
theoretical concepts, practical implementations, and national trends. A systematic review was conducted across four major databases: 
Web of Science, Scopus, LISTA, and Library & Information Science Collection. Papers were included in the research based on their 
relevance to citizen science in libraries, with criteria encompassing all library types, global geographical coverage, and multilingual 
sources. A total of 58 papers directly addressing libraries and citizen science were analyzed. The findings reveal that the analyzed 
works address several key theoretical topics and concepts, including open science as a central concept, libraries as facilitators and hubs 
for engagement, community engagement and crowdsourcing, educational roles and capacity building, digital humanities and historical 
projects, and contributions to sustainable development. Examples of practical implementations include transcription and annotation 
projects, crowdsourcing and community engagement, health and environmental citizen science, and digital innovation. At the national 
level, studies focus on higher education libraries, public libraries, perceptions and collaboration, frameworks for sustainability, and 
bridging the gaps between science and society. Although the analysis provides valuable insights into citizen science initiatives, the study 
could not directly confirm that these publications have shaped or influenced specific projects or their recommendations, highlighting 
this as an area for further research. This research concludes that libraries worldwide are well-positioned to act as key enablers of 
citizen science by addressing challenges and adopting best practices. This review highlights diverse approaches to developing a library’s 
roadmap for engaging in citizen science initiatives.

Open Science Policy Platform within the European Union 
defines it as collaborative, transparent, and reproducible 
research, with outputs freely accessible to the public. 
Citizen science is included under this framework along-
side open code, altmetrics, open resources, and open data 
(European Commission, 2018). Wehn et al. (2020) illus-
trated this relationship graphically (Figure 1).
It is important to highlight that citizen science and open 
science represent paradigms that reflect not only how 
research is conducted but also how it should be conducted. 
While they may evolve independently, they often share 
common values focused on advancing both science and 
society. Open science emphasizes access to research out-
puts and infrastructure, while citizen science expands 
openness by involving non-professional researchers in the 
research process (Wehn et al., 2020).
Citizen science was first defined in a modern context 
by Alan Irwin (1995) and Rick Bonney (1996). It involves 
public participation in scientific research, where non-pro-
fessionals (citizen scientists) contribute to data collection, 
analysis, and interpretation. Citizen science is also known 
as community science, crowd-supported science, or pub-
lic participation in scientific research (Schoenenberger 
et al., 2020). Participants can engage in various stages of 
the scientific process, including formulating research 
questions, gathering data, analyzing results, and sharing 
findings (Land-Zandstra et al., 2021). Haklay (2013) 
identified levels of citizen science participation, from 
“citizens as sensors” (crowdsourcing) and “citizens as 
interpreters” (distributed intelligence) to deeper involve-
ment in defining research questions (participatory sci-
ence) and full engagement in the research process 
(extreme citizen science). Citizen science projects can 
access new knowledge by involving individuals with 
unique perspectives or local expertise, leading to new dis-
coveries and innovative solutions (Ivanjko et al., 2024). 
Haklay et al. (2021) reviewed 34 definitions of citizen sci-
ence and noted regional differences in interpretation and 
implementation. They concluded that definitions are 
influenced by geographical and policy differences, reflect-
ing diverse practices across Europe and the United States.
Open science, including citizen science, is relevant to 
numerous stakeholders—researchers, policymakers, the 
public, and libraries. Libraries, as providers of access to 
publications and other scientific research products, play a 
key role in advocating and raising awareness about open 
access and open science, maintaining the infrastructure of 
open science, and educating users—researchers, students, 
and the public. Libraries can engage in several ways as 
active participants in citizen science projects. According to 
Ayris and Ignat (2018), libraries should educate—build 
skills for engaging in citizen science projects, support and 
directly participate in the development of citizen science 
projects, create resources such as toolkits, protocols, educa-
tional materials, and offer and maintain existing open sci-
ence infrastructure for citizen science projects (e.g. 
institutional repositories). Libraries should also participate 
in the evaluation process of projects (e.g. the FAIR princi-
ples), offer materials thematically related to citizen science 
projects, engage in promotional activities, and more. 
Mumelaš and Martek (2024) highlight the additional value 
of libraries’ involvement in citizen science projects—
attracting new user groups, promoting the library in the 
media, working on further education of librarians, and cre-
ating opportunities for local and international cooperation.

Several studies based on a review of literature that par -

zen science have so far been conducted. Diverse 
methodologies were employed in those studies, and sev-

Moreover, the reviews often focused on specific types of 
libraries (e.g. exclusively academic libraries).

through various services and programs. Their study, which 
included 54 works, emphasized the role of librarians in 
enhancing citizen science by offering resources and advo-
cating for open science practices. Similarly, Guedes Farias 
et al. (2023) focused on the role of academic libraries in 
scientific dissemination, highlighting activities such as 
public hearings and consensus conferences that strengthen 
public engagement with science. Both studies underscore

tools such as Transkribus demonstrated how libraries 
could harness technological innovations in order to engage 
the public and preserve cultural heritage.
While these studies provide valuable insights, they are 
often limited to specific types of libraries (e.g. academic or 
health sciences) or regional contexts (e.g. Europe). A uni-
fied, global perspective that includes public, academic, 
research, and special libraries across diverse cultural and 
linguistic backgrounds remains lacking. This systematic 
review aims to fill this gap by synthesizing findings from a 
wide range of sources, offering a comprehensive overview 
of citizen science in libraries worldwide. The objective is 
to identify the best practices, familiar challenges, and 
opportunities for libraries to engage in citizen science, fos-
tering a more inclusive and globally connected framework 
for public participation in scientific research.
Methodology
This study follows the guidelines for conducting a system-
atic review, aiming to provide a comprehensive overview 
of the involvement of libraries in citizen science. The sys-
tematic review was planned and conducted according to 
the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for 
Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines 
(Figure 2). Although no formal protocol was registered, 
the search strategy and inclusion criteria were carefully 
defined before conducting the review to ensure consist-
ency and transparency in the process.
The research aims to answer the following questions:
RQ1: Which specific theoretical topics and concepts 
relate to citizen science in libraries?
RQ2: What are the examples of practical implementa-
tion of specific citizen science activities/projects/pro-
grams in libraries?
RQ3: What topics are the focus of studies about citizen 
science in libraries at the national level?
Search strategy
The search was conducted among four major databases: Web 
of Science Core Collection, Scopus, Library, Information 
Science & Technology Abstracts (LISTA—Ebsco), and 
Library & Information Science Collection (ProQuest). The 
following Boolean search formula was used:
“citizen science” OR “participatory science” OR 
“community science” OR “citizen participation” OR  
“citizen engagement” OR “citizen involvement” OR “citi-
zen collaboration” OR “public participation” AND “infor-
mation science” OR “information specialist” OR librar.”
Search fields included Title, Abstract, and Keywords 
where applicable. The search covered publications up to 
June 1, 2024.

Studies were selected based on the following eligibility 
criteria:
Inclusion criteria:
•• Studies focused on citizen science within the con-
text of libraries.
•• All types of libraries (academic, public, research, 
national, and special libraries).
•• Global geographical coverage and multilingual 
sources.
•• Peer-reviewed journal articles, reviews, and confer-
ence papers.
Exclusion criteria:
•• Studies related to museums, archives, and galleries.
•• Geographic information science (GIS) and other 
unrelated disciplines.
•• Posters, introductions, and abstracts without full 
analytical content.
The screening and selection process was conducted in two 
phases:
1. Title and abstract screening—Two independent 
researchers (Author 1 and Author 2) screened the 
titles and abstracts of all idenfied papers for rele-
vance based on the defined inclusion and exclusion 
criteria. In cases of disagreement, the third author 
was consulted to reach a consensus.
2. Full-text screening—After the initial screening, the 
remaining papers were reviewed in full text by the 
same two authors. Any discrepancies or uncertain-
ties were resolved through discussion and mutual 
agreement.
Out of an initial pool of 1002 papers, 451 remained after the 
removal of duplicates. After the screening process, 79 papers 
were selected as directly related to the broader field of infor-
mation science. Following a more detailed full-text review, 
58 papers were identified as directly addressing citizen sci-
ence in libraries and were included in the final analysis.
Key data were extracted from each paper, including 
theoretical frameworks and key concepts related to citizen 
science, practical examples of citizen science activities in 
libraries, and national level studies and trends in citizen 
science engagement. The data were coded and organized 
into three thematic categories corresponding to the research 
questions:
1. Theoretical topics and concepts related to citizen 
science in libraries.
2. Practical implementation of citizen science activi-
ties in libraries.
3. National-level patterns and trends.
The study encompasses works in the English, German, 
Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, Croatian, Serbian, and 
Chinese languages.
Results
The selected papers offered a wealth of insights into the 
theories and practices of citizen science in libraries. The 
papers were read in full, and their content was analyzed 
and divided into three main categories corresponding to 
the first three research questions. A portion of the analyzed 
literature (seven papers) consists of previous systematic 
reviews on citizen science in libraries, which are summa-
rized and discussed in detail in the chapter “Previous 
Research.” These reviews provide a foundational context 
for understanding the broader landscape of citizen science 
in libraries and inform the interpretation of the results pre-
sented in this study. The papers are organized into several 
main sections.

Libraries as facilitators and hubs for engagement. Librar-
ies—whether academic, public, or special—are consist-
ently portrayed as facilitators and community hubs for 
citizen science. Morriello (2021) described libraries as 
enablers of citizen science activities, supporting sustaina-
ble development through community engagement. Cas-
sella (2022a) emphasized the potential of libraries to 
bridge communities and researchers, fostering scientific 
literacy, and societal impact through projects such as 
crowdsourcing and Europeana Transcribe. Munke (2023) 
provided examples from Saxony libraries in Germany, 
emphasizing open access and participatory initiatives. 
Dakić et al. (2023) showcased the Citizen-Enhanced Open 
Science in Southeastern Europe project, highlighting the 
potential for libraries to contribute to regional knowledge 
hubs.
Community engagement and crowdsourcing. Community 
engagement is a cornerstone of citizen science in libraries. 
Holley (2010) discussed the benefits of crowdsourcing, 
including its ability to increase library outreach and pro-
vide volunteer recognition through innovative approaches 
to engagement. Ellis (2014) expanded on this by present-
ing several British library projects, analyzing the strengths 
and limitations of crowdsourcing. Gardner and Gardner 
(2017) examined platforms such as Twitter and Reddit for 
crowdsourced research-sharing, concluding that libraries 
must improve interlibrary loan usability and reduce fulfil-
ment times to better serve their communities. Haklay 
(2013) categorized crowdsourcing as the first level of citi-
zen science, while Proulx (2020) described it as an evolu-
tion of traditional volunteering, significantly enhancing 
library services, and public participation. Díaz-López et al. 
(2019) highlighted the use of VR (virtual reality) to expand 
citizen science’s educational and societal impact, enabling 
greater interaction between citizens and researchers.
Educational roles and capacity building. Libraries play a  
significant educational role by teaching citizens about sci-
entific methodologies and by fostering the public under -
standing of science. For example, Rehbein and Ernst 
(2023) analyzed how libraries adapt to technological 
changes, proposing new models of knowledge creation 
where all stakeholders act as co-producers. Cohen et al. 
(2015) provided a literature review on citizen science defi-
nitions and emphasized the role of librarians in ensuring 
project accuracy and supporting future scientific endeav-
ors. Cassella (2022b) focused on training initiatives and 
the tools libraries can use to empower individuals, such as 
Europeana Transcribe and crowdsourcing platforms.
Digital humanities and historical projects. Digital humanities 
intersect with citizen science through projects that involve 
digitizing historical documents and creating digital news-
paper clippings. Anglada (2019) highlighted the examples 
such as Transcribe Bentham and Mapa Literari Català, pro-
posing future directions for libraries to strengthen commu-
nities through such initiatives. Munke (2022) emphasized 
the Wikiverse as a platform for organizing citizen science 
activities and described the added value of combining digi-
tal and analog content to maximize library impact.
Contributions to sustainable development. Several papers 
highlight the role of libraries in advancing sustainable 
development goals through citizen science. Kennan et al. 
(2012) reported on the role of academic libraries in advo-
cating environmental data management, focusing on train-
ing and supporting novices in managing “wild data” 
generated by voluntary environmental groups. Morriello 
(2021) linked citizen science to sustainability practices, 
emphasizing the libraries’ potential to address global chal-
lenges. Díaz-López et al. (2019) emphasized using VR as 
a tool to foster sustainability, enhance knowledge sharing, 
and address societal challenges.

collections. For instance, the Digitalkoot projects by the 
National Library of Finland (Pääkkönen, 2015) engaged 
participants in gamified OCR (Optical Character Recogni-
tion) corrections and digital clippings, generating metrics 
and user feedback to refine development efforts. Wiederkehr 
(2019) described the ETH Library’s The Image Archive  
campaign, where participants improved photograph meta-
data and transcribed documents, fostering both virtual, and 
on-site community building. The Greek Key Project (Gib-
son et al., 2018) further demonstrated how crowdsourcing 
could enhance multidisciplinary digital humanities pro-
jects, involving librarians and citizens in participatory 
research. In public libraries, Ting and Jeng (2023) discussed 
the Taipei Old Photos Collection (TOPC), which invited 
citizens to submit and digitize historical photographs, fos-
tering public engagement and digital preservation. Beales 
(2016) presented a unique example involving the Facebook 
group Biome Reconstitution, where medical librarians sup-
ported citizen scientists by providing reliable medical 
information, countering misinformation, and fostering 
quality discussions on health topics. The Borys Grinchenko 
Dictionary Project, described by Opryshko and Nazarovets 
(2021), utilized elements of digital humanities, gamifica-
tion, and citizen science to engage university students in 
updating the dictionary of the renowned Ukrainian ethnog-
rapher Borys Grinchenko. This project highlighted the use 
of wiki platforms to foster community participation.
Health and environmental citizen science. Citizen science 
in health and environmental domains demonstrates how 
libraries can extend their societal impact. Overgaard and 
Kaarsted (2018) analyzed A Healthier Funen (AHF), a 
Danish initiative involving an academic library and 
regional partners. Citizens voted on research funding 
allocations, fostering public participation and enhancing 
democratic debate. Stanifer et al. (2024) showcased the 
use of Citizen Science Kits in Kentucky public libraries 
to measure radon levels. Libraries acted as intermediar -
ies, lending kits, educating users, and collecting data. 
That initiative demonstrated the practical application of 
libraries in environmental health research. The Patients 
Participate! project (Duke, 2015) highlighted how 
libraries collaborated with medical research charities to 
engage the public in writing lay summaries of research, 
making scientific information more accessible and 
comprehensible.
Digital innovation. Libraries are increasingly embracing 
digital innovation to create participatory environments. 
Hernández-Pérez et al. (2022) described the Library Liv-
ing Lab in Barcelona, where libraries integrate technology, 
community engagement, and cultural heritage to drive 
social transformation. This space fosters collaborative 3D 
creation, connects libraries with museums, and promotes 
digital storytelling. Kern (2022) highlighted the University 
of Washington Libraries’ Going Public symposium, which 
bridges academic research with community engagement. 
This initiative equips researchers with skills for public 
scholarship and citizen science, fostering deeper connec-
tions between universities and local communities. Gupta 
et al. (2024) developed a unique toolkit at the University of 
Victoria Libraries, focused on empowering BIPOC (black, 
indigenous, and people of color). professionals in STEM 
(science, technology, engineering, and mathematics) disci-
plines. This program used short videos to enhance com-
munication skills, fostering inclusivity and knowledge 
sharing. The Taipei Public Library’ s Citizen Science Initia-
tive involved lay participants in cataloging and preserving 
old photographs, providing them with training and incen-
tives to contribute. Ting and Jeng (2023) emphasized the 
social impact of engaging citizens in creating and curating 
cultural archives.
These conclusions can serve as inspiration and a model 
for other libraries looking to expand their role within the 
lives of the members of the local public and to become 
hubs of innovation and participation. Implementing simi-
lar practices enables libraries to respond to the needs of 
their users, support the development of digital skills, and 
elevate inclusivity and diversity in scientific research. 
Additionally, these findings underscore the importance of 
libraries as spaces for collaborative learning, encouraging 
active citizen participation, and fostering open and acces-
sible approaches to knowledge.

study pertains to public libraries, while all others relate to 
higher education libraries.
In Spain, a study conducted within the Barcelona 
Network of Public Libraries highlighted the preparatory 
efforts required for implementing citizen science activi-
ties. A training course for public librarians preceded two 
organized activities: Citizen Science Lab and Science and 
Citizen Action. Through participatory observation, sur -

librarians, and professional scientists were analyzed to 
assess the benefits and challenges of these initiatives. 
Findings suggest that public libraries can assume a lead-

mission as local community hubs. However, challenges 
such as the complexity of collaboration, uncertainty about 
research co-creation, and participant retention strategies 
were identified as areas needing further attention (Cigarini 
et al., 2021).
Rykhtorova (2022) analyzed over 400 pages of Russian 
research institutes and library websites to identify the obsta-
cles hindering the integration of citizen science within the 
open science ecosystem. The study emphasized libraries’ 
potential roles in bridging the gaps between science and 
society by serving as information intermediaries, develop-
ing outreach activities, and enriching the public understand-
ing of scientific knowledge. Despite a growing reliance on 
digital communication, significant gaps in citizen science 
practices persist, and libraries are well-positioned to address 
them. The study suggests that libraries should strengthen 
their outreach efforts and leverage their expertise to enhance 
citizen science communication.
A cross-sectional survey and in-depth interviews con-
ducted in Malaysia revealed that academic libraries pre-
dominantly adopt a participatory citizen science approach. 
The findings highlight challenges faced by librarians, 
including limited awareness and comprehension of citizen 
science, insufficient skills, and difficulties in managing 
crowdsourced data. These barriers underline the need for 
training programs and capacity-building initiatives to 
empower librarians and promote citizen science activities 
within the academic institutions (Nurfarawahidah et al., 
2023).

Discussion
To address RQ1 (Which specific theoretical topics and 
concepts relate to citizen science in libraries?), it is evi-
dent that citizen science in libraries is shaped by several 
interrelated theoretical topics and concepts. Open science 
stands out as a central framework, encompassing princi-
ples such as open access, EOSC, and RDM, with libraries 
playing a critical role in fostering these practices. Through 
their infrastructure and expertise, libraries provide essen-
tial support for citizen science projects, ensuring accessi-
bility and collaboration. Another prominent theme is the 
role of libraries as information specialists and facilitators. 
Libraries serve as hubs for public engagement, offering 
resources, tools, and training to encourage active partici-
pation in scientific inquiry. Community engagement and 
public participation also emerge as key elements, with 
libraries managing initiatives such as crowdsourcing and 
providing platforms that enable diverse groups to contrib-
ute to research. This aligns closely with their educational 
roles, as libraries enhance public understanding of science 
by teaching methodologies, offering training, and building 
research capacity. Furthermore, digital humanities demon-
strate how libraries utilize digital tools to involve the pub-
lic in humanities research, such as annotating historical 
documents. Finally, libraries contribute to sustainable 
development by addressing environmental and societal 
challenges through citizen science projects. The intercon-
nectedness of these themes underscores the pivotal role 
libraries play in advancing citizen science globally. By 
fostering collaboration, leveraging digital tools, and 
empowering communities, libraries position themselves as 
indispensable partners in addressing complex global 
issues.
The practical implementation of citizen science in 
libraries, as observed in the analyzed examples, under -
scores their significant, and varied contributions to this 
domain. To answer RQ2 (What are the examples of practi-
cal implementation of specific citizen science activities/
projects/programs in libraries?), we can highlight the fol-
lowing examples: transcription and annotation projects, 
crowdsourcing and community engagement, health and 
environmental citizen science, and digital innovation. 
Libraries are uniquely positioned to serve as facilitators, 
mediators, and innovators, bridging the gap between aca-
demic research and community involvement. Through ini-
tiatives ranging from transcription projects to health and 
environmental research, libraries provide critical infra-
structure, resources, and platforms for public engagement. 
A key strength of libraries lies in their ability to adapt citi-
zen science projects to diverse contexts. Transcription and 
annotation projects highlight how libraries utilize their 
collections to involve the public in preserving and expand-
ing access to cultural heritage. These initiatives not only 
foster community participation but also contribute to the 
creation of valuable research assets. Crowdsourcing pro-
jects emphasize the libraries’ capacity to engage communi-
ties in a wide range of activities, from enriching metadata 
to supporting large-scale digital humanities initiatives. 
Such projects demonstrate the libraries’ ability to mobilize 
collective intelligence while maintaining rigorous quality 
standards. In the health and environmental sectors, librar -
ies expand their traditional roles by partnering with scien-
tific organizations and public health institutions. By 
distributing citizen science kits and enabling lay summa-
ries of research, libraries ensure that scientific endeavors 
are inclusive and accessible to a broad audience. These 
efforts not only democratize research but also address 
critical societal challenges, such as environmental moni-
toring and public health education. Hence, digital innova-
tion projects illustrate the transformative potential of 
libraries in citizen science. By leveraging tools such as 
virtual research environments, gamified platforms, and 
participatory storytelling spaces, libraries facilitate the 
creation of new knowledge while empowering diverse 
groups to actively contribute to scientific and cultural 
advancements. Overall, the reviewed examples demon-
strate that libraries excel in fostering collaboration, inte-
grating technology, and addressing community needs 
through citizen science. However, these projects also 
reveal challenges, including the need for sustainable fund-
ing, effective volunteer management, and cross-institu-
tional collaboration. Addressing these challenges will be 
essential for maximizing the long-term impact of citizen 
science initiatives in libraries.
These findings reinforce the role of libraries as pivotal 
players in citizen science, positioning them as innovative 
hubs that advance knowledge, bridge social divides, and 
empower communities. Future efforts should build on 
these successes to further expand the scope and impact of 
citizen science in library contexts.
National level studies reveal a broad spectrum of citi-
zen science activities in libraries, tailored to the specific 
contexts and challenges of different countries. The analy-
sis highlights several key themes which also address RQ3 
(What topics are the focus of studies about citizen science 
in libraries at the national level?): participation of higher 
education libraries, role of public libraries, perceptions 
and collaboration, frameworks for sustainability and bridg-
ing the gaps between science and society. Academic librar-
ies are frequently involved in citizen science, focusing on 
participatory approaches and addressing challenges such 
as limited awareness, lack of skills, and the complexities 
of managing crowdsourced data. Studies from Malaysia 
and South Africa particularly emphasize capacity building, 
integrating citizen science into library curricula, and lever-
aging existing collaborations with academic departments.

the European Open Science Cloud (EOSC) and other col-

the integration of citizen science into library services.
Conclusion
This systematic review confirms that libraries worldwide 
play a significant role in citizen science, acting as facili-
tators, enablers, and hubs for public engagement in scien-
tific research. The findings indicate that libraries support 
citizen science through various means, including provid-
ing access to open science infrastructures, fostering 
community participation, and leveraging digital tools for 
knowledge creation and dissemination. They contribute 
to enhancing scientific literacy, preserving cultural herit-
age, and addressing societal challenges through partici-
patory research projects.
The key insight from this study is the diversity of 
approaches libraries take to integrate citizen science into 
their services. From transcription and annotation projects to 
crowdsourcing initiatives and environmental monitoring 
programs, libraries have demonstrated their adaptability in 
engaging citizens and researchers alike. However, while 
numerous initiatives highlight the potential of libraries in 
citizen science, challenges remain in sustaining funding, 
fostering institutional support, and ensuring long-term 
engagement of participants.
At the national level, libraries have been identified as 
key actors in bridging the gaps between scientific research 
and society. Their involvement in open science policies, 
training initiatives, and collaborative partnerships further 
strengthens their position as essential stakeholders in the 
citizen science ecosystem. Nevertheless, disparities in 
institutional readiness, regional policies, and access to 
technological resources continue to shape the effective-
ness of these efforts.
Ultimately, this review underscores the vital role of 
libraries in advancing citizen science on a global scale. By 
adopting the best practices, securing sustainable resources, 
and strengthening cross-sector collaborations, libraries can 
continue to act as catalysts for public engagement in 
research, contributing to more inclusive and accessible sci-
entific knowledge.
Glossary of terms
Annotation
Adding notes or comments to a text, document, or dataset 
to provide additional information or context.
Citizen science
Scientific research conducted with the participation of 
non-professional scientists, often involving data collection 
or analysis by the public.
Citizen scientist
A member of the public who contributes to scientific 
research, typically without formal scientific training.
Community engagement
The process of working collaboratively with community 
members to address issues, build relationships, or share 
knowledge.

Crowdsourcing
Collecting input, ideas, or data from a large group of peo-
ple, often through online platforms, to complete a task or 
solve a problem.
Digital humanities
An interdisciplinary field that combines humanities 
research with digital tools and methods, such as analyzing 
texts or digitizing archives.
European open science cloud (EOSC)
A European initiative to create a virtual environment for 
sharing and accessing research data and services across 
disciplines and countries.
Fair principles
Guidelines for data management that ensure data is 
Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable.
Higher education libraries
Libraries affiliated with universities or colleges that sup-
port academic research, learning, and teaching.
Open access
The practice of providing free, unrestricted online access 
to scholarly research and publications.
Open data
Data that is freely available for anyone to access, use, and 
share, typically without restrictions.
Open science
A movement that aims to make scientific research and data 
accessible to everyone, promoting transparency and 
collaboration.
Research data
Information collected or generated during scientific 
research, such as datasets, measurements, or observations.
Transcription
The process of converting spoken words or handwritten/
digitized documents into written or typed text.
