Design Thinking Process Model Review 
A systematic literature review of current Design Thinking models in practice 
 
Lukas Waidelich1, Alexander Richter1, Bernhard Kölmel1 and Rebecca Bulander1 
1IoS3 - Institute of Smart Systems and Services

Abstract— The presented research provides an overview of 
thirty-five analyzed process models in the field of Design Thinking 
for application in a practical environment. The overall objective is 
a comparison of Design Thinking process models according to a 
number of process steps and their specific terminology. The 
structure of the paper is as follows: First, an introduction helps the 
readers to become involved in the topic. Second the methodology 
of the systematic literature review and the focus of this paper are 
explained. Afterwards, the results of the literature review are 
presented. Then in the next step the key findings are discussed. 
The paper concludes with a summary of the main findings and 
provides an outlook of future research tasks. 
Keywords—Design Thinking, Literature review, Design 
Thinking models, Design Thinking process model, Design Thinking 
characteristics. Design Thinking methods. Design Thinking phases.  
I.  INTRODUCTION 
Lauded as a panacea Design Thinking (DT) is one of the 
most promising concepts in today’s business world in the 
context of innovation methods. Coming from the heart of 
Silicon Valley in California, developed at Stanford University, 
refined by IDEO and supported through Hasso Plattner, one of 
the founders of SAP, to Germany, this is the briefly summarised 
story of DT [1]. 
Nowadays, a complex framework of different factors is 
affecting the business world. Besides new trends driven by 
emerging technologies, globalization processes, changing 
customer requirements of private, business and governmental 
customers, companies have had to adjust their offers to provide 
new solutions to the market. As a consequence, the product or 
service life cycle is becoming shorter, stiff competition prevails 
and a major effort for winning customers and maintain market 
shares has started. This has proved more of a challenge for the 
current business environment than ever before. Overall, the 
current situation are leading to a conflict among existing 
business tools and methods, which were used in the past, but 
will not fit with future requirements of product or service 
development. To sustain and improve competitiveness and 
profitability permanently, companies should seek new 
possibilities by applying a new tool and method set to create 
(added) value to their customers [2]. 
One approach for solving this problem is the DT method 
[2]. DT can be used as a team-based process model, which 
supports interdisciplinary teams during the problem solution 
phase of a design process. Thereby the expression “design” is 
not only applied in terms of designing a product, service or 
business model but the focus is also on the manner in which 
designers work, meaning the perspective is not the object which 
a designer creates but the approach how designers solve 
problems is at the centre of the DT method. As a practical 
example, design thinkers develop empathy for the end user, 
redefine the problem, combine different perspectives, 
brainstorm solutions and develop prototypes í which is beyond 
the scope of designing a product[3]. 
This approach allows the discussion of diverse issues, 
Uebernickel et al. [4] elucidated that the DT method included 
questions like: “How could families and married couples design 
furniture which is attractive for both young and old people as 
well as being useful and suitable for the disabled” or “How 
could the traceability of medical samples in hospitals be 
significantly improved by digitalization” or “A medicine must 
not cost more than one euro per bottle. The available 
components already cost more than two euros today. How do 
you solve the problem?” [4]. This demonstrated the vast areas 
of application the DT method could be used for. 
Once more, a practical example of developed products is 
given by using the DT approach. The impressive success story 
of Apple as the most powerful brand in the world in 2017 has 
already been written, the products, the services and the 
ecosystem are well known [5]. Moreover, Apple’s innovations 
are triggered and performed using the DT approach. The first 
Apple computer mouse can be seen as an example. The 
innovation was a creation of Apple engineers and members of 
IDEO which was a part of Stanford University, regarded as the 
inventor of the DT method [1,6]. 
It is recognized that DT has been experiencing a boom in the 
economy in the last decade, because its practical framework 
leads to clear benefits for companies as displayed above [1]. 
Nevertheless, the DT process varies considerably and a 
consistent and coherent model does not exist so far. This paper 
focuses on different published DT process models and displays 
the different characteristics. The study described in this paper

C. Literature search 
Phase III describes the procedure during the literature 
search. To provide a better understanding how the authors 
performed the systematic literature search figure 2 displays the 
overall literature search process in detail. 
First of all, the framework conditions are defined. 
Therefore, the key terms are set. The underlying literature 
research uses the combination of two keywords sets. The first 
set is “Design Thinking” which is linked by OR with the second 
set which contains different terms like “Process”, “Model”, 
“Practice”, “Practitioners” and “Application” to set the focus on 
the topic of the paper. The terms are searched for in both 
English and German. 
In addition, the media types are established based on Brocke 
et al. [7]. The sources of the current paper consist of textbooks 
(including chapters of textbooks), latest journals papers as well 
as web documents. The aim of this selection is to cover a wide 
range of perspectives and to include practice relevant models. 
Different databases are selected for all three source types. Peer-
reviewed papers in a journal are  searched for in up-to-date 
databases such as, ScienceDirect, Emerald Insight and the 
search engine Google Scholar. Springer is used as  the central 
database for the research of textbooks. Web documents are 
researched through the support of a web search engine like 
Google.

The next process is the performing of the electric search. 
Besides the entered keywords the search settings in the database

sources are identified within that step with the objective to

D. Literature analysis and synthesis 
Phase IV covers the literature analysis and synthesis of the 
collected literature. This is done by a four-step filter approach 
to obtain the final document list. These steps are valid for all 
three types of research (journal papers, textbooks and internet 
sources) (refer to figure 2). 
The first kind of filter refers to the published title. The 
presence document must be related to the DT topic. If this 
requirement is fulfilled the literature is taken into account 
further. Second, the abstract is researched. There, the existing 
literature must show elements of DT referring to process, 
model, practice or applications. If this requirement is also 
fulfilled the literature is considered further. Further on, the full 
text is filtered. If the document contains or refers to a DT 
process model it is put into the final selection. The final step is 
the redundancy analysis, where redundant entries from journal 
papers, textbooks and internet sources are eliminated. This 
process step leads to the result of the final generation of relevant 
literature for DT process models. Overall, the final document 
list contains thirty-five DT process models, thereof seventeen 
are journal papers, thirteen are textbooks and five are internet 
documents. 
Subsequently, the relevant literature is transferred into a 
Microsoft Excel table where the findings are firstly compared 
by means of type of publication, year of publication and 
language. Thereafter, the table is extended with information 
about the individual models like number of process steps and 
exact definition. The table is displayed in the third chapter. 
E. Research Agenda 
Finally, phase V aims to identify research fields using the

agenda are summarized and explained

2)  Description of DT phases 
As determined in the section Terminology comparison of 
DT process phases there is no standardized model with defined 
phases. For this reason, each author has to explain exactly what 
is included in each individual phase and how the phases fit 
together. As an example, Schallmo provides a structured 
description of his model. First he elucidated the objectives of 
each phase, explains the activities which have to be performed 
to reach the objectives and last he provides an overview of 
expected results per phase [10]. A full comparison of DT 
descriptions is useful but out of the scope of this paper. 
3) Applied methods in DT phases 
Furthermore, the DT process is a set of various methods, 
which are applied to achieve the best possible result. As 
mentioned in the preceding section, there is no standardized DT 
process model, which leads to the result that there are no 
standardized method sets in DT either. This is the reason why 
each author uses different methods in different phases. 
Continuing with the Schallmo example, he proposed different 
methods per phase to reach the objective. For example, in the 
third phase “Define point of view” he recommends the 
combination of the methods “User Empathy Map” and “User 
Journey” to concentrate the results through the method “User 
Needs” [10]. Besides the description of DT phases, the applied 
methods in DT phases are an interesting research area, but are 
not included in the scope of this paper. 
IV.
 GENERATION OF KEY FINDINGS 
After the theoretical analysis of the twenty-two examined 
DT process models displayed in chapter three, this chapter deals 
with the interpretation of the findings. Besides finding 
similarities in the models, the focus is also on identified 
differences during this research. 
A. Similarities of DT process models 
First, the results of the subchapter terminology comparison 
of DT process phases are discussed, subsequent developed 
models are highlighted and finally similar models are 
contrasted. 
The phase „Ideation/ Ideate” is most frequently mentioned. 
This implies that the ideation phase is an essential component 
of a DT process model. During that stage, ideas are generated 
to develop a suitable solution for the initial question. This is a 
core element in the DT method which thirty out of thirty-five 
models have in common [14]. The term “Prototype/ 
Prototyping” is also crucial, a majority of the researched models 
include the term. The ideas out of the ideation phase can be 
prototyped in different ways to visualize a possible solution [6]. 
This section deals with the evolution and the gradual 
development of the DT process models discovered. The origin 
of DT is the design company IDEO with David Kelly as founder 
and his brother Tom Kelly, who coined the term DT as indicated 
above. In 2008 David Brown, at that time CEO of IDEO 
published an article in the Harvard Business Review to explain 
the DT approach of IDEO and outlined a three step DT process 
model [10]. Later in 2012, IDEO published a toolkit with the 
title “Design Thinking for Educators” with the purpose to show 
how to use DT in the education system. This toolkit was 
updated in 2013 [26]. The process model according to Plattner, 
Meinel and Weinberg, which was published in 2009, is based 
on experiences out of several DT projects. It was used in the 
Hasso-Plattner-Institute School (HPI) of Design Thinking in 
Potsdam as well as in Stanford University in Paolo Alto at the 
Hasso Plattner Institute of Design, which is called d.school in 
an abbreviated form [12]. In 2010 the d.school [26] published a 
further model which has conceptual similarities with Plattner, 
Meinel and Weinberg [10] because of their cooperation. The 
head of the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design is David Kelly 
[10]. 
Furthermore, a few of the researched models refer to the 
above mentioned models according to Plattner, Meinel and 
Weinberg, d.school and from IDEO and show remarkable 
similarities. Overall Plattner, Meinel and Weinberg form the 
basis for ten of the thirty-five models discovered. Besides 
Engchuan,Geissdoerfer et al., Grots, Gürtler and Mayer, 
Katoppo and Sudradjat, Kretzschmar, Meinel and Thienen, 
Plattner and Meinel, Weinberg as well as Weissmann all refer 
to the original model [3,6,11–13,21,25,28,29,37].Furthermore, 
eight models including Beaird et al., Ewin et al., Fabri et al., 
Goodspeed et al., Gürtler and Mayer, Kretzschmar, Lewis et al. 
and Weissmann also refer to the approach of d.school 
[16,19,20,24,25,29,30,37].Moreover, IDEO is also the basis for 
the process models of Gloger, Kretzschmar and Shapira et al. 
[23,29,35]. Besides, the authors Chou and Whang et al. refer to 
the original DT process model from Brown [17,38].  
As Conclusion, for the most parts English literature 
referencing to d.school and Brown, whereas German literary 
works are based on Plattner, Meinel and Weinberg respectively 
HPI of Design Thinking in Potsdam. 
B. Differences of DT process models 
This section investigates the differences of the DT process 
models discovered.

as examples [11,27,29,36]. In contrast, Ambrose and Harris,

V.
 CONCLUSION 
This chapter combines and concludes the literature review 
of thirty-five published DT process models in practice. 
Beginning with the main findings of this paper, leading over to 
a brief discussion as a result of chapter four. Afterwards, further 
research questions are formulated. This paper closes with an 
outlook of future research tasks. 
A. Main findings 
The literature research of DT process models is based on a 
broad range of published documents like journal papers, 
textbooks and web documents leading to the result of thirty-five 
researched models. The models and terminology of the 
individual phases are displayed in the subchapter terminology 
comparison of DT process phases. The influence of the English 
authored publications is characterized through d.school. On the 
other side are DT process models in the German language 
coined through Plattner, Meinel and Weinberg or the Hasso-
Plattner institute who are the pioneers in the field of Design 
Thinking in practice. Additionally Brown and IDEO are all 
significant preconditions for other models. Furthermore, the 
elements “Ideation/ Ideate” and “Prototype/ Prototyping” are 
used in a majority of the models and it seems that they are 
crucial elements in DT process models. 
B. Discussion 
It should be noted, as already mentioned, that this literature 
review makes no claim to be complete but is instead intended 
to provide a broad overview of DT process models in practice. 
Ten years have elapsed since the basic research of DT 
process models arose from the IDEO, d.school and Hasso-
Plattner-Institute School of Design Thinking network. During 
this period, DT has earned a reputation in the area/sector of 
education and industry at the same time. Hasso Plattner claimed 
that more than 100 research projects have been conducted since 
the Design Thinking research program was founded in 2008. In 
parallel the DT approach has subsequently been further 
developed by deepening the scientific understanding of the 
underlying principles gained out of the research projects. This 
knowledge was conveyed to both students and professionals, 
who apply the DT concept in practice. This could probably be 
one of the reasons why several process models are based on this 
above-mentioned network [39]. 
As stated by Meinel and Thienen, there are several 
comparable models available today [13]. This literature also 
checks the claim that a few researchers have worked out 
different process models with remarkably similar results. 
Nevertheless, there are several approaches, which differ in a 
significant way to the original model of Brown. 
Moreover, there is a trend towards models with more phases 
describing the approach more in detail. In 2008, Brown 
described the DT framework with three phases. In contrary 
Schallmo described the DT approach in an in-depth discussion 
in 2017 by using seven phases [10]. 
C. Further Research 
As has already been discussed in the chapter Results of 
Literature Review  in the section  DT process models in a 
content-related context, the specific terms have been identified, 
contrasted and compared within this paper. As a future research 
task, it is intended to focus on the description of each phase per 
author identified in the present paper. This is an important area 
for future research, as well as the applied method sets 
established during each phase. As a further benefit, all three 
parts could form a unified DT picture. Out of this newly gained 
knowledge an own improved DT process model could be 
generated or an existing one could be improved through the 
recombination of different method sets or through new findings. 
D. Outlook 
The outcome of the described further research needs can be 
a new or improved DT model. This model requires a validation 
in practice e.g. in cooperation with companies, start-ups or in 
education. Afterwards, the feedback from practice applications 
should be used to further improve the model. Subsequently a 
best practice approach using the DT model should be published. 
This could conceivably be in the form of a DT guideline or 
handbook.
