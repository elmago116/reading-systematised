ABSTRACT
 
An interdisciplinary team (from Design, Community 
Development, Computer Science, and Rhetoric & Writing) 
created an interactive, updateable timeline showing the Evolution 
of Participatory Practices (EPP) in five disciplines over 50 years; 
the team members also documented and reflected on their own 
participatory practices. This Experience Report describes how the 
EPP timeline was created and what the team learned about 
interdisciplinary work in the process. It offers insights that may 
help other  interdisciplinary groups overcome communication 
differences ranging from the simple (different vocabularies), to 
the complex (different definitions of shared terms), to the nearly 
invisible (the powerful, tacit epistemological assumptions 
underlying each discipline) by conceptualizing the work process 
in terms of an interdisciplinary rationality island and seeing the 
timeline as a boundary object. It also describes how making the 
process itself an object of study ensured that the reflective work 
necessary to effective teamwork got done.

1. INTRODUCTION 
This Experience Report describes how an interdisciplinary team at 
the University of California, Davis, Center for Design in the 
Public Interest explored the question: What does democratic 
design look like?  To articulate historical contributions to 
democratic design, we developed and deployed an interactive 
timeline showing the “Evolution of Participatory Practices” (EPP) 
in five disciplines over fifty years. We also  documented the 
process, including informal conversations  and rough sketches. 
This report focuses on the process, especially how we overcame a 
perennial barrier to interdisciplinarity — the “cultural barriers” 
between disciplines that can “impede movement across 
boundaries” [1] (p. 4). 
Our process involved much boundary crossing; the team  includes 
two graduate students ( from Community Development and 
Computer Science), two professors ( from Design and the 
University Writing Program ), and a designer, and we received 
input from test users and from subject- matter experts.   
Throughout this rep ort, we understand a  participatory approach as 
“one in which everyone who has a stake in the intervention has a 
voice” and an influence through collaboration with practitioners 
and researchers [2]. 
2. OVERVIEW OF ACTIVITIES 
In April 2014, the UC Davis Center for Design in the Public 
Interest (DiPi) launched and was just beginning to consider 
projects that it would like to work on. The team — then consisting 
of Professors Susan Verba and Sarah Perrault and designer Prerna 
Dudani— was approached by Yohei Kato, a ma sters student in 
Community Development, who was interested in learning about 
information design and shared the Center’s mission of exploring 
the question, “what does democratic design look like?” Susan had 
been thinking for some time about tracing the evol ution of 
participatory design over time and disciplines, a project that Yohei 
agreed would be interesting. As the project evolved, the team 
grew to include an MS student in Computer Science, Sumayyah 
Ahmed, and also various outside advisers including other  
professors and industry experts. The process itself, which we 
documented via meetings notes, sketches, and other artifacts, took 
place in four overlapping and sometimes recursive phases. 
Although we separate them here for simplicity, the phases were 
not (and still are not) this tidy in reality.  


2.1 Collecting Content 
At the start of the project, we knew that interest is rapidly growing 
for practitioners and researchers to implement a participatory 
approach in different fields. However, not much research has 
described how participatory approaches in each field relate to one 
another: methods and resources have not been widely shared 
among different fields [3]. Therefore, we began with the 
following research question: How have participatory practices 
evolved over  time and across disciplines? Also, how have they 
influenced and connected with each other?  
To begin answering this, Yohei conducted a literature review that 
drew on 32 books, 78 web sites and 141 articles that addressed the 
nature of participatory practic es across various disciplines . He 
also conducted a total of fifteen interviews with professionals, 
researchers, and students from across a range of disciplines  
including community design, public health, international 
development, and youth development . Con tacts led him to other 
contacts, as when Henry Sanoff (Distinguished Professor of 
Architecture, NCSU) recommended that he also look into four 
projects involving five more people Yohei had not yet identified.  
Other expert advice helped Yohei keep the proje ct from growing 
too large and unwieldy to be productive, as when Mark Francis 
(Professor Emeritus, Landscape Architecture and Environmental 
Design, UC Davis) recommended limiting the scope, a judgment 
that confirmed the team’s sense that we need ed to stay focused on 
particular disciplines and a particular time frame or risk losing 
connectedness.   
During this research phase, five areas emerged as especially 
relevant to the project:  (1) community design, (2) youth 
development, (3) international development, (4) public health, and 
(5) information and communication technology development . By 
the end of th is phase, Yohei had identified 24 individuals, 19 
organizations, and 15 specific projects and events.  
2.2 Developing the Timeline 
During the research process, Yoh ei, Susan, and Prerna began 
working on giving the information a visual form; over time, this 
included identifying 89 connections between entities.  These 
connections were discovered through the design process; 
information was organized by decades and discip lines with sticky 
notes on large sheets of butcher paper, allowing us to begin to see 
connections, to explore relationships, to pose questions about 
other possible projects, events, and influences, and to gain an  
understanding of the most important/impactf ul people and work.

The design challenge at this point was how to visualize the 
connections across disciplines over time. In a visual equivalen t of 
the literature review, the three began collecting examples of 
timelines on Pinterest, analyzing and talking about the examples’ 
different features. A diagram by artist Mark Lombardi  [4] proved 
especially inspiring for the way it communicated complex 
information using few colors, a visual vocabulary of lines and 
symbols, and a minimum of text for a clean and legible look. 
However, the eventual form the timeline took was not a foregone 
conclusion; other models we looked at and learned from took a 
wide variety of forms.

At this point we had started to realize the timeline would make an 
interesting educational tool, one that we wanted to make 
accessible and useful as a visual research tool for a wide audience. 
This suggested that it should be digital, dynamic, and updateable. 
We had also realized that we did not have the skills to make that 
vision a reality when Sumayyah Ahmed, a graduate student in 
Computer Science, a pproached DiPi with her interest in user -
centered design.  
Sumayyah understood that the site should be dynamic, flexible, 
and simple for a non -coder to update. This meant she had to create 
a web framework, or a customized content management system , 
that could adapt to whatever changes we non-programmers make 
 

in the source files. The team therefore identified two important 
criteria for the project source files and the visual display:  
1. The data and design files needed to be read dynamically 
by the system to accommodate changes to data and data 
characteristics as information is added and edited over 
time. 
2. The output display of the data and design needed to be 
dynamic, with the timeline image providing 
interactivity, that is, being able to show and hide 
information as the user navigates the timeline.  
Her solution was to write a layer of code that sits between the 
source files and the site and converts the information to a web -
friendly format; for example, the following conversion takes place 
between the source and  output files:

With Sumayyah’s programming skills, we were able to take what 
began as a static paper -based poster and turn it into a responsive 
web design with the following features:  
- An interactive menu that takes the user to specific parts

Throughout the project — from the literature review stage to the 
present— we elicited and incorporated input from the audiences 
we hoped would use the site: educators, desig ners, researchers, 
students, and community developers. Our goal was not only to 
create the timeline of participatory practices but to do so in a way 
that was itself participatory, that is, that the work be done not “on 
behalf of the users” but rather that it be “done with the users” [5] 
(p. 165).  Input came from Gilad Meron (a community designer 
and a board member of the Association for Community Design), 
members of the ViDi lab in the Computer Science department at 
UC Davis, and from usability tests that Yohei conducted with a 
group of community designers in Japan during a visit home.  
The final stage of technical development was to calibrate the site 
so it was compatible with all browsers and worked on a range of 
screen sizes; Prerna created a running list  of design changes, 
including diagrams and screenshots of the website to make sure 
the changes were clear.  
Once the site was operating properly, we began two final and 
ongoing activities: revising the content to more clearly reflect our 
focus on participat ory design, and developing a “How To” guide 
to share with others who may want to use the source code. We 
also began thinking about how to let others know about the site 
and to encourage further participation in content development and 
expression.

3. BARRIERS TO SUCCESS 
As noted ear lier, the above sequence of events did not unfurl as 
smoothly as our narrative implies, in part because of challenges to 
interdisciplinary work generally and in part because of challenges 
inherent in this project. Here we discuss the barriers to success we  
faced, and the next section explains how we overcame them.  
3.1 Lack of a Project Brief 
One of the biggest challenges we faced was the fact that we knew 
some of the attributes of what we wanted to create (the “value”), 
but not its form. Phrased in traditional  design terms, we lacked a 
project brief. Susan contrasted it with her experience as principal 
of a design studio, noting that this was an emergent project rather 
than one with client -specified goals, objectives, and constraints. 
Instead, these all came fr om us. In contrast with projects that have 
a clearly defined end product, in this case we had only an end 
goal— to create a useful visual research tool depicting connections 
across fields and over time — and no other goals or constraints 
except those we ident ified for ourselves (and except the budget, of 
course). 
3.2 Disciplinary Differences 
Even on a small team of five people, we faced significant 
differences in disciplinary ways of working. For example, 
Sumayyah and Yohei, coming from Computer Science and 
Community Development, initially saw the timeline itself in very 
different terms, with Sumayyah initially picturing a static image 
with links, while for Yohei  and Susan it was a growing body of 
work. Sumayyah had to, in her terms, “work from that mindset, 
not just ‘oh Yohei made a picture, let’s turn it into a graphic and 
be done.’” She elaborated: 
Normally on a web site the content is pretty stable, you 
have a blog area where you can update the code and 
have that change. But in this case the core is drawing on 
sources that are meant to be updated dynamically, and it 
does that itself. It meant I had to think of a system, not 
what would appear on the web page and have that as my 
playground, but I had to start all the way from what 
Yohei was producing, the paperwork  he was producing, 
and think about that whole process of from paper to 
digital. The web site was not the closed product, it was 
the end product of a series of steps starting at Yohei’s 
files. 
Similarly, Susan had to adjust her own work process to 
Sumayyah’s: 
Sometimes Sumayyah was showing me something  
and I would immediately start to talk about design, and 
Sumayyah would…  be uncomfortable because I was 
focusing on the visual and experiential outcome without 
understanding all the embedded programming 
implications that were behind this… I had to stop and 
realize that she was showing me something deeper than 
the thing I was seeing and wanting to change.  
Such adjustments in team members’ expectations about the 
process were an ongoing challenge throughout the pr oject. Other 
examples included finding an approach to iteration and version 
tracking that all team members could follow and contribute to; 
working on each others’ files, as Prerna and Yohei frequently did; 
and learning to assist each other in disciplinary -specific activities, 
as when Prerna learned about coding from Sumayyah and Yohei 
learned about design from Susan and Prerna.  
4. KEYS TO SUCCESS 
Below we explain four factors that contributed to our success.  
4.1 Time and Space 
In our process, having a shared space  and time for informal 
conversation were vital for overcoming communication 
differences. Reflecting on the keys to success, Prerna noted:  
It was very helpful for me to sit next to Sumayyah and 
work even though we wouldn’t be working on the same 
task. Just having her nearby and being able to get 
explanations in person with diagrams etc. was very 
helpful. I’m a visual learner and often Sumayyah’s 
explanations didn’t make sense to me until she worked 
them out for me on paper.  
Our experience confirmed what res earch has shown in other sites: 
co-location is a “key condition” for successful interdisciplinary 
work [7] (21). 
4.2 Forming an Interdisciplinary Rationality 
Island 
We conceptualize our work on this project in terms of what 
Gerard Fourez calls an interdiscipli nary rationality island (IRI), 
that is, a model tailored to the situation, in this case  our desire to 
produce a visual research tool —  an interactive and updateable 
map of people, events, and organization s over time . An IRI does 
not form simply by bringing  together different disciplines: It 
forms when the people representing those disciplines openly and 
knowingly commit to leaving aside disciplinary hobbyhorses in 
order to create new ways of working together and new ways of 
creating knowledge. Fourez explai ns: “ The efficiency (and 
therefore the value) of the island will be related to its ability to 
offer a representation that contributes to the solution of a specific 
problem” [8] (916).  
In our case, no one discipline could have come up with the 
timeline, nor could we have created it via a traditional division of 
labor (e.g. designers developing the look and feel, then handing 
off the project to the coder for implementation). The form the 
timeline eventually took was dependent on the interactions of 
team members and represents a synthesis of different disciplinary 
approaches.  
4.3 Humility, Agency, and Design by 
Conversation 
Another way to understand the team’s process is to see the IRI as 
forming around the timeline , making that artifact into a “boundary 
object” [9] that could be viewed differently from each discipline 
without losing its essential meaning.  Each team member had her 
or his own evolving ideas of what the timeline should be and do, 
but at the same time accepted that it had other meanings for other 
team members. The resulting disciplinary humility by each team 
member paradoxically allowed greater agency for all team 
members. The following quotes are shared without attributions to 
specific team members to underscore how similar our experiences 
were: 
Executing this project helped jolt me out of a “how 
would I do this to suit myself” mentality, in fact placing 
my ego out of the equation made me approach this 
project laterally.  
When working with others in your field you can get 
proprietary about defending y our work to them…you 

have a little built-in hierarchy. In this project, I didn’t 
have to justify my position to any other person.  
 
It felt like my project as well; things were changing 
based on my suggestions and work. It didn’t feel like I 
was just working on somebody else’s project all the way 
through. 
 
It wasn’t as though you just come to somebody and say, 
here is what we have and you make a web site. It was 
very collaborative from the beginning.  
The end result was what Susan described as “design by 
conversation”: 
We are all the clients; we all want to use and learn from 
this tool— therefore everyone has a voice and a stake in 
the project. Each person saying, “it would be good if it 
could do this”… It was designed by conversation. That 
might not be a bad thing to rally around, “design by 
conversation.”  
Furthermore, this was a conversation between all of us; everybody 
had a voice. 
4.4 Studying Our Own Process 
The final key to our success, and perhaps the most important, is 
that studying our own process is one o f our primary goals at DiPi. 
We take to heart Blair -Early’s advice that designers today “must 
be more malleable and willing to surrender creative control and 
work collaboratively toward a final product and, in some cases, 
the designer must focus on process  rather than final output”  [ 10] 
(212).  
Documenting the process with the meta -goal of understanding the 
interdisciplinary work we were doing kept the focus on the 
process. Susan explains, “Viewing our work as experimentation 
changes how we work. Everythin g opens up— instead of being 
about making an outcome, it becomes about discovering ways of 
doing, being, making together.” 
5. IMPLICATIONS 
5.1 Implications for Interdisciplinary Teams 
Formation of temporary IRIs may help resist the tendency toward 
disciplinarity. According to Frodeman & Mitcham,  “all efforts at 
interdisciplinarity have tended to evolve into narrow disciplines” 
(510), defeating the purpose of bringing together a range of 
scholars and practitioners in the first place . However, forming an 
IRI is not in itself enough. Based on our experience, we suggest 
that: 
- Treating the timeline as a boundary object — as a central 
artifact with multiple, equally valid meanings — helped 
prevent premature closing off of options.  
 
- Documenting the process ensured that we did  not skip 
over the important work of making the invisible 
disciplinary knowledge and work we did visible to other 
team members (and back to us as we reflect on the 
project and the process).  
5.2 Implications for Interdisciplinary 
Research 
While we have focused in this experience report on what we did 
and on specific things we learned, we also see our work in more 
abstract terms as a response to Frodeman & Mitcham’s call for 
“[k]nowledge experiments… that try to respond to the new 
conditions of contemporary knowl edge production” and for “case 
study experiments in interdisciplinary research ” [11] (p. 512).  In 
particular, they argue that we need a c ritical interdisciplinarity that 
focuses on the meaning as much as the knowledge; part of what 
worries Frodeman & Mitc ham is that “ [k]nowledge production 
today has a tendency to swamp knowledge use systems at both 
individual and institutional levels” [11] (p. 507) and the way that 
disciplinary research’s narrowness ends up “crowding out 
awareness of ends or purposes” [11] (p. 508).  
Our experience suggests that one way to counter this myopia is to 
approach research via participatory design, where making 
something more than new knowledge — in our case, making a tool 
to help research become more interactive and experiential — is the 
goal. Spinuzzi explains,  
As the name implies, the approach is just as much about 
design— producing artifacts, systems, work 
organizations, and practical or tacit knowledge — as it is 
about research. In this methodology, design is research. 
[5] (p. 164) 
Based on our experience, working to answer public needs via 
participatory design is an especially effective way to keep the 
effects of research as much in the spotlight as the research itself. 
We hope the challenges and insights described here are useful to  
teams, including our own future teams, in future interdisciplinary 
design projects.
